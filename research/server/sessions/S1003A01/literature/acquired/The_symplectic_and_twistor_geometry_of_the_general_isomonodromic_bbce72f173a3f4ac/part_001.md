\title{
The symplectic and twistor geometry of the general isomonodromic deformation problem
}

\author{
N. M. J. Woodhouse \\ The Mathematical Institute, University of Oxford
}

October 30, 2018

\begin{abstract}
Hitchin's twistor treatment of Schlesinger's equations is extended to the general isomonodromic deformation problem. It is shown that a generic linear system of ordinary differential equations with gauge group $\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ on a Riemann surface $X$ can be obtained by embedding $X$ in a twistor space $\mathcal{Z}$ on which $\operatorname{sl}(n, \mathbb{C})$ acts. When a certain obstruction vanishes, the isomonodromic deformations are given by deforming $X$ in $\mathcal{Z}$. This is related to a description of the deformations in terms of Hamiltonian flows on a symplectic manifold constructed from affine orbits in the dual Lie algebra of a loop group.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

The study of isomonodromic deformations of systems of ordinary differential equations in the complex plane was a significant topic at the beginning of the last century, when the classical work of Painlevé, Schlesinger, and Fuchs was published. It has come back into view in more recent years through connections with quantum field theory (Sato et al 1978, 1979, 1980, Dubrovin 1999), differential geometry (Hitchin 1996), and the theory of integrable systems (see, for example, Ablowitz and Clarkson 1991).

In this paper, I shall explore in detail one aspect of the modern theory, suggested by Hitchin (1995a). He considered the twistor space of a four-dimensional self-dual Riemannian manifold with SU(2) symmetry. This is a three-dimensional complex manifold $\mathcal{Z}$, in which there is a four-dimensional family of projective lines corresponding to the points of the original manifold and on which the symmetry group acts holomorphically. The action is generated by three holomorphic vector fields which are independent in an open subset, but dependent on a special divisor $S$. By taking the vector fields as basis vectors, the tangent space to $\mathcal{Z}$ is identified with $\operatorname{sl}(2, \mathbb{C})$ at each point of the open subset. Thus the action determines a flat holomorphic connection on the trivial $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$ bundle over the open orbit.

The restriction of the connection to a twistor line is an $\mathrm{sl}(2, \mathbb{C})$-valued meromorphic 1-form, with poles at the intersections with $S$. In the case that Hitchin considered, there are four poles and the 1-form determines a Fuchsian system, with four regular singularities. As the line is moved within the family, the poles move, but the monodromy of the system, which is the same as the holonomy of the flat connection, remains unchanged. By calling on the classical theory, therefore, one obtains from this geometrical picture a solution to the sixth Painlevé equation. Hitchin then goes on to exploit this correspondence to construct self-dual Einstein metrics from certain Painlevé transcendents.

Hitchin's correspondence between twistor manifolds with symmetry and isomonodromic families of ordinary differential equations holds more generally. In this paper, I shall follow through the details of his suggestion for the class of isomonodromic deformations considered by Jimbo, Miwa, and Ueno (1981). This enables one to understand their results within the framework of the general deformation theory of Kodaira (1962).

In the general setting, we are given a complex Lie group $G$, a Riemann surface $X$, and a meromorphic 1-form $\alpha$ on $X$ with values in the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. We pick a local coordinate $z$ and write $\alpha=-A \mathrm{~d} z$. Then the equation $\mathrm{d} y+\alpha y=0$ becomes a system of linear ordinary differential equations
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A y,$$
where $y$ is a fundamental solution, taking values in $G$, and $A$ is a meromorphic function of $z$. The first question concerns the existence of twistor spaces: this is answered by Proposition 1, which gives the existence of an embedding of $X$ in a complex manifold $\mathcal{Z}$ on which $\mathfrak{g}$ acts, with the generators independent on an open set, and from which $\alpha$ can be recovered by Hitchin's construction. This structure is not unique, however, even if we restrict attention to a small neighbourhood of $X$ in $\mathcal{Z}$. If $A$ has irregular singularities, then there are different choices for the way in which a divisor $S$ can be attached to the open set so that the whole of $X$ is embedded, including the singularities. Different choices give different possibilities for the normal bundle $N$ of $X$. By Kodaira's theorem, the normal bundle determines the deformations of $X$ in $\mathcal{Z}$ : if
$$H^{1}(X, N)=0 \quad \text { and } \quad \operatorname{dim} H^{0}(X, N)=d_{0},$$
then $X$ is one of $d_{0}$-parameter family of compact curves $X_{t}$, on each of which the $\mathfrak{g}$-action gives a linear system of differential equations. These are isomonodromic (Proposition 6). It is shown that there is a natural choice for the twistor space (the 'full twistor space' in Definition 2), for which the parameter space has the largest possible dimension in the generic case; a full twistor space exists generically (Proposition 2), and is unique in a neighbourhood of $X$ (Proposition 4). In the full case, $N$ can be constructed directly from $\alpha$; if $H^{1}(X, N)=0$, as is the case if
$X=\mathbb{C} \mathbb{P}_{1}$, and generally if $\alpha$ has enough singularities, then every isomonodromic deformation arises from this construction (Proposition 8).

A second theme of this paper is the Hamiltonian nature of the isomonodromic deformation equations. A Fuchsian system on $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$ is a system with regular singularities of the form
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=\sum \frac{A_{i} y}{z-a_{i}},$$
where the residues $A_{i} \in \mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{sl}(2, \mathbb{C})$ are independent of $z$. Apart from gauge and coordinate transformations, the only possible deformations in the generic case are given by moving the poles $a_{i}$. The monodromy is then preserved if and only if
$$\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial a_{j}}=\frac{\left[A_{i}, A_{j}\right]}{a_{i}-a_{j}}, \quad i \neq j, \quad \frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial a_{i}}=-\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{\left[A_{i}, A_{j}\right]}{a_{i}-a_{j}},$$
(Schlesinger's equations). ${ }^{1}$ Hitchin (1997) interpreted these as a Hamiltonian flow on the coadjoint orbits of the $A_{i}$ s. This also generalises: when irregular singularities are present, the flows are on symplectic manifolds constructed from affine orbits in the loop algebra. The symplectic forms can be written down explicitly in terms of $\alpha$ and the Stokes' matrices, and, at least in the case $X=$ $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$, one can also find explicit expressions for the Hamiltonians (Proposition 9). The symplectic structure is related to the structure of $\mathcal{Z}$ in a neighbourhood of $S$.

An appendix outlines the theory of isomonodromic deformations for linear systems on a general Riemann surface.

\section*{Twistor spaces}

We suppose that we are given a complex Lie group $G$, a Riemann surface $X$, and a meromorphic 1-form $\alpha$ on $X$ with values in the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. Our starting point is to interpret a solution $y: X \backslash\{$ poles $\} \rightarrow G$ to the equation
$$\mathrm{d} y+\alpha y=0$$
as a complex curve in $G$ and to think of $\alpha$ as the pull-back of the Maurer-Cartan form on $G$-the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form whose contraction with a left-invariant vector field is the corresponding element of $\mathfrak{g}$.

\footnotetext{
${ }^{1}$ One pole is fixed at infinity, and has residue $-\sum A_{i}$.
}

In a local coordinate $z$ on $X$, (2) is a linear system of ordinary differential equations of the form
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A y,$$
where $A$ is meromorphic, with values in $\mathfrak{g}$. Its poles are the singularities of the system-a pole of order $r+1$ is a singularity of rank $r$. Some familiar results about such systems are summarised in the appendix.

Of course $y$ is singular at the poles of $\alpha$, and it is multi-valued, so the embedding in $G$ is defined only locally. In the twistor picture, we seek to replace $G$ by a complex manifold $\mathcal{Z}$ of the same dimension on which the right action of $G$ is retained and in which the singular points are included. In principle, the construction involves (i) taking a quotient by the monodromy group to make $y$ single-valued and (ii) attaching hypersurfaces on which the right-action of $G$ action is not free. The intersections of $X$ with the hypersurfaces will then correspond to the poles of the differential equation. Except for very special equations, however, the quotient is not Hausdorff. The best that we can do in general is to construct $\mathcal{Z}$ as a neighbourhood of $X$, with the action of $G$ replaced by an action of its Lie algebra (which is enough to determine $\alpha$ ). The second step is generally straightforward in the regular case, but is more subtle when the linear system has irregular singularities.

In the context of the deformation problem, we shall adopt a special understanding of the meaning of the term 'twistor space'.

Definition 1 A twistor space is a complex manifold $\mathcal{Z}$ together with
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] a homomorphism from a complex Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ into the Lie algebra of holomorphic vector fields on $\mathcal{Z}$; and
\item[(ii)] a smooth compact complex curve $X \subset \mathcal{Z}$
\end{itemize}
such that the induced linear map $\phi_{z}: \mathfrak{g} \rightarrow T_{z} \mathcal{Z}$ is an isomorphism for some $z \in X$.

Note that $\operatorname{dim} \mathcal{Z}=\operatorname{dim} \mathfrak{g}$. For the most part, we shall take $\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{sl}(n, \mathbb{C})$, but other examples will also be considered.

Given a basis in $\mathfrak{g}, \Delta=\operatorname{det} \phi$ is a holomorphic section of $\bigwedge^{\operatorname{dim} \mathcal{Z}} T \mathcal{Z}$. We shall make the regularity assumptions that
$$S=\{\Delta=0\}$$
is a complex hypersurface, that $X$ is transversal to $S$, that $S$ is the union of a finite set of components $S_{i}$, and that $\Delta$ has a zero of order $r_{i}+1$ on $S_{i}$. These hold in all the twistor spaces constructed below.

At each $z \in \mathcal{Z} \backslash S$, define $\theta_{z} \in T_{z}^{*} \mathcal{Z}$ by $\theta_{z}=\phi_{z}^{-1}$. Then $\theta$ is a holomorphic 1-form on $\mathcal{Z} \backslash S$ with values in $\mathfrak{g}$. It is meromorphic on $\mathcal{Z}$ and satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation
$$\mathrm{d} \theta+\theta \wedge \theta=0$$

Equivalently, $\mathrm{d}+\theta$ is a flat meromorphic connection on a trivial bundle over $\mathcal{Z}$. The restriction $\alpha=\left.\theta\right|_{X}$ determines a system of the form (2), with poles at the intersection points with $S$. We then say that $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$ is a twistor space for the system.

Example. Let $G=\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ and let $\mathfrak{t} \subset \mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{sl}(n, \mathbb{C})$ denote the diagonal subalgebra. As an $(n-1)$-dimensional additive group, $\mathfrak{t}$ acts on itself by translation, and the action extends to the compactification $\mathbb{C P}_{n-1}$ when we add a hyperplane at infinity. We also have the left action of $\mathfrak{t}$ on $G$, defined by
$$g \mapsto \exp (A) g, \quad A \in \mathfrak{t} .$$
We put
$$\mathcal{Z}=G \times \mathbb{C} \mathbb{P}_{n-1} / \mathfrak{t} .$$
Then the right action of $G$ on the first factor descends to the quotient.
We can think of $\mathcal{Z}$ as being formed by attaching a single hypersurface $S$ to $G$ (the projection of the hyperplane at infinity in $\mathbb{C P}_{n-1}$ ). The effect is to compactify the one-parameter subgroups generated by the semisimple elements of $\mathfrak{g}$. If $A \in \mathfrak{t}$ generates a closed subgroup, then $\{t A\} \subset \mathfrak{t}$ compactifies to a projective line in $\mathbb{C P}_{n-1}$, and this in turn projects onto an embedded copy of $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$ in $\mathcal{Z}$. The corresponding system of linear equations is
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A y,$$
which has a singularity of rank 1, a double pole, at infinity (the intersection with $S)$.

Example. Suppose that $X$ has genus $g$. Let $G=\mathcal{Z}$ be the Jacobi variety (an abelian group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{C}^{g}$ ) and let $X \hookrightarrow \mathcal{Z}$ be the standard embedding (see, for example, Farkas and Kra 1980, p. 87). The corresponding system is
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A$$
where $A=\left(\xi_{1}, \ldots, \xi_{g}\right)$, with the $\xi$ s a basis for the abelian differentials on $X$. Here there are no singularities.

\section*{Existence of twistor spaces}

Does every system of ODEs of the form (2) have a twistor space? Since the restriction of $\theta$ to $X$ cannot vanish, a necessary condition is that $\alpha \neq 0$ at every point of $X$. This condition holds in the generic case (since it fails only if the all the entries in the matrix $A$ have a coincident zero). It is also sufficient.

Proposition 1 Let $\alpha$ be a meromorphic $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on $X$ with no zeros. Then the linear system of $O D E s \mathrm{~d} y+\alpha y=0$ has a twistor space.

Proof. We construct $\mathcal{Z}$ by taking a quotient of a neighbourhood of the identity section $X$ in $X \times G$ by a distribution $F$ constructed from the linear system.

Let $D$ be a neighbourhood of a pole $a$ not containing any of the other poles, and let $z$ be a coordinate on $D$ such that $z=0$ at $a$. Then $\alpha=-A \mathrm{~d} z$ in $D$, where $A: D \backslash\{0\} \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$ is holomorphic and has a pole of order $r+1$ at $z=0$.

Define $F$ to be the distribution on $D \times G$ tangent to the non-vanishing vector field
$$z^{r+1}\left(\partial_{z}-R_{A}\right)$$
where $R_{A}$ is the right-invariant vector field on $G$ generated by $A(z)$. If $D^{\prime}$ is an open set not containing any other poles, then we define $F$ in the same way on $D^{\prime} \times G$, but without the factor $z^{r+1}$; that is $F$ is tangent to $\partial_{z}-R_{A}$. The vector fields are proportional on $D \cap D^{\prime}$, so $F$ is well defined globally as a distribution on $X \times G$. Under the condition on $\alpha$, we have $F \cap T_{x} X=0$ at every $x \in X$. So it is possible to choose an open neighbourhood $N$ of $X$ in $X \times G$ such that the quotient $\mathcal{Z}=N / F$ is a Hausdorff complex manifold of the same dimension as $G$. We then have a double fibration
![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/f3ea20da-5d62-4402-a1e3-3c3755332aec-06.jpg?height=178&width=326&top_left_y=1270&top_left_x=852)
and a smooth curve $\pi_{2}(X) \subset \mathcal{Z}$, which we also denote by $X$.
Because we are looking only at a neighbourhood of the identity section, the right action of $G$ on $X \times G$ does not pass to $\mathcal{Z}$; but the corresponding Lie algebra action does. Each $v \in \mathfrak{g}$ can be identified with a left-invariant vector field on $G$, and hence with a vector field on $X \times G$ tangent to the fibres of $\pi_{1}$. Its projection $V$ by $\pi_{2 *}$ is a holomorphic vector field on $\mathcal{Z}$, and the map $v \mapsto V$ is a Lie algebra representation, satisfying the conditions in the definition of a twistor space. The singular hypersurface has components given by the poles of $\alpha$, and $X$ meets these transversally.

It remains to show that $\left.\theta\right|_{X}=\alpha$. To do this, we note that the meromorphic vector field $\partial_{z}-R_{A}$ on $\mathbb{C} \mathbb{P}_{1} \times G$ is tangent to $F$, and so its projection into $\mathcal{Z}$ vanishes. On the other hand, at the identity, the right- and left-invariant vector fields generated by an element of $\mathfrak{g}$ coincide. Hence $i_{\partial_{z}} \theta=-A$. The proposition follows. \(\square\)

Remark. If we instead take the curve in $\mathcal{Z}$ to be the projection under $\pi_{2}$ of $X \times\{g\}$ for some other constant element of $g$, then we obtain instead a twistor space for $g^{-1} \alpha g$.

In the irregular case, the twistor space is not unique: the one that arises in Proposition 1 is minimal in a sense that will be explained later.

\section*{Full twistor spaces}

The difference in structure between different twistor spaces of a system of ODEs can be understood by looking at the structure in a neighbourhood of a point of $a \in S$. By introducing a local coordinate $z$ that vanishes on $S$, we can choose a neighbourhood $U$ of the form $S \times D$, where $D \subset \mathbb{C}$ is, say, the unit disc, and the $X \cap U$ is $\{(a, z)\}, z \in D$.

Suppose that $\alpha=-A \mathrm{~d} z$ has a pole of order $r+1$ at $z=0$. Then corresponding system
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A y$$
has a singularity of rank $r$ at $z=0$.
Given a holomorphic vector field $V$ on $\mathcal{Z}$ tangent to $S$, we can construct a holomorphic family of copies $D_{t}$ of $D$ in $U$ by moving $D_{0}=X \cap U$ along $V$ (and if necessary restricting to a smaller neighbourhood of $a$ ): here $t$ is a complex parameter taking values in some neighbourhood of $t=0$. By restricting $\theta$ to each $X_{t}$, we get a one-parameter family of ODEs
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A(z, t) y,$$
each with a singularity of rank $r$ at $z=0$ (the singularity does not move with $t$ because $V$ is tangent to $S$ ). It follows from the flatness of $\mathrm{d}+\theta$ that
$$\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial z}-[A, \Omega],$$
where $\Omega=-i_{V} \theta$. This is the local deformation equation (see appendix).
At each fixed value of $t, \Omega$ is a function of the coordinate $z$. Introducing the notation
$$\nabla \Omega=\partial_{z} \Omega-[A, \Omega]$$
we have
$$\Omega=O\left(z^{-r-1}\right), \quad \nabla \Omega=O\left(z^{-r-1}\right) \quad \text { as } z \rightarrow 0 .$$
When the singularity is irregular, the various twistor spaces differ in the extent to which the converse holds: in the minimal construction, an $\Omega$ satisfying these conditions is of the form $i_{V} \theta$ for some holomorphic $V$ only if $\Omega-f(z) A$ is holomorphic at $z=0$ for some holomorphic function $f$.

Definition 2 A twistor space $\mathcal{Z}$ is full at $a \in S$ if for every $\Omega: D \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$ such that (5) holds, there is a holomorphic vector field $V$ on $U \subset \mathcal{Z}$ such that $\Omega=-\left.i_{V} \theta\right|_{X}$. The twistor space is full if it is full at every point of $S$.

When the system has irregular singularities, and $\operatorname{Rank}(G)>2$, the twistor space constructed in Proposition (1) is not full. We can see this by noting that, for
any holomorphic $V, i_{V} \theta$ has singular part at each pole that is proportional (by a holomorphic function) to a multiple of $A$, and cannot therefore give rise to the most general $\Omega$ satisfying (5). We shall put this more precisely below when we consider the normal bundle of $X$ in $\mathcal{Z}$.

A full twistor space generates not only the ODE itself, but also its isomonodromic deformations. We shall see that it is possible to construct a full twistor space in the generic case, but there are some rather special exceptions. A necessary condition for existence is that if $\Omega$ and $\Omega^{\prime}$ both satisfy (5), and if
$$\Omega=\frac{M}{z^{r+1}}+O\left(z^{-r}\right), \quad \Omega^{\prime}=\frac{M^{\prime}}{z^{r+1}}+O\left(z^{-r}\right),$$
as $z \rightarrow 0$, with $k>0$, then $\left[M, M^{\prime}\right]=0$. This fails in the following class of examples.

Example. Suppose that
$$A=z^{-2}\left(\begin{array}{ll}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{array}\right),$$
Then
$$\Omega=\frac{M}{z}, \quad \Omega^{\prime}=\frac{M^{\prime}}{z},$$
satisfy (5) for any constant $M, M^{\prime}$; but both cannot be generated from vector fields in the same twistor space if $\left[M, M^{\prime}\right] \neq 0$.

\section*{The singular hypersurface}

We now look in detail at the structure of a twistor space in a neighbourhood of $S$. The fullness condition at a point of $X \cap S$ is this: given $\Omega$ such that (5) holds, is $\phi(\Omega)$ holomorphic at $z=0$ ?

Let us write
$$A=\frac{p}{z^{r+1}}+H, \quad \Omega=\frac{\omega}{z^{r+1}}$$
where
$$p=p_{0}+p_{1} z+\cdots+p_{r} z^{r}, \quad \omega=\sum_{0}^{\infty} \omega_{i} z^{i}$$
and $H$ is holomorphic on $U$. If $\omega=O\left(z^{r+1}\right)$ as $z \rightarrow 0$, then $\Omega$ is holomorphic at $z=0$ and can be generated by the holomorphic vector field $\phi(\Omega)$ in any twistor space for $A$; so the source of any difficulty lies in the first $r$ terms in the Taylor expansion of $\omega$.

When we separate out the coefficients of $z^{-2(r+1)}, z^{-2 r-1}, \ldots z^{-(r+2)}$ in (5), we obtain for $r>0$
$$\begin{aligned}
{\left[p_{0}, \omega_{0}\right] } & =0 \\
{\left[p_{0}, \omega_{1}\right]+\left[p_{1}, \omega_{0}\right] } & =0
\end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned}
{\left[p_{0}, \omega_{r-1}\right]+\left[p_{1}, \omega_{r-2}\right]+\cdots+\left[p_{r-1}, \omega_{0}\right] } & =0 \\
{\left[p_{0}, \omega_{r}\right]+\left[p_{1}, \omega_{r-1}\right]+\cdots+\left[p_{r}, \omega_{0}\right]-(r+1) \omega_{0} } & =0,
\end{aligned}$$
or in the case $r=0$,
$$\left[p_{0}, \omega_{0}\right]=\omega_{0} .$$
The generic case is that one or other of the following hold:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] $r>0$ and the eigenvalues of $p_{0}$ are distinct; or
\item[(ii)] $r=0$, the eigenvalues of $p_{0}$ are distinct, and no pair differ by an integer. ${ }^{2}$
\end{itemize}

Lemma 1 If $A$ is generic, then $\Omega$ satisfies (5) if and only if $\omega_{0}=0$ and $[\omega, p]=$ $O\left(z^{r+1}\right)$ as $z \rightarrow 0$.

Proof. Under either of the conditions (i), (ii), the eigenvalues of $p$ can be assumed to be distinct, since they are distinct at $z=0$ and since we can, if necessary, replace $U$ by a smaller neighbourhood. So we can find a holomorphic gauge transformation $g: U \rightarrow G$ such that $g^{-1} p g$ is the sum of a diagonal polynomial and a term that vanishes to order $z^{r+1}$ at $z=0$, and so can be absorbed into $r$.

If we assume first that $p$ is actually diagonal, then we deduce successively that $\omega_{0}, \ldots \omega_{r-1}$ are diagonal (for $r>0$ ) and that
$$\left[p_{0}, \omega_{r}\right]=(r+1) \omega_{0} .$$
For $r>0$, this implies that $\omega_{0}=0$ since the diagonal terms on the left-hand side vanish, and hence that $\omega_{r}$ is also diagonal. For $r=0$, it gives $\omega_{0}=0$ since $p_{0}$ has no pair of eigenvalues differing by 1. Thus, whether or not $p$ is diagonal, we have that $\Omega$ is holomorphic at $z=0$ when $r=0$; and that when $r>0$,
$$\Omega=\frac{g q g^{-1}}{z^{r}}+O\left(z^{0}\right)$$
where $q$ is a diagonal polynomial of degree $r-1$. \(\square\)

Proposition 2 Let $\mathrm{d} y+\alpha y=0$ be a generic system, with $G=\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$. Then there exists a full twistor space.

\footnotetext{
${ }^{2}$ To prove Lemma 1 and Proposition 2, we only need that no pair should differ by 1; however the stronger condition here is needed to construct $g_{\mathrm{f}}$ (see Appendix), and is imposed here to avoid special cases in the presentation below.
}

Proof. Any twistor space is full at a singularity of rank $r=0$ since any $\Omega$ satisfying (5) is then holomorphic at $z=0$, and can therefore be generated by a holomorphic vector field in any twistor space. In the irregular case, we construct $\mathcal{Z}$ from the 'minimal' twistor space in Proposition 1, by cutting out and replacing a neighbourhood of each component of $S$ corresponding to an irregular singularity.

Suppose, to begin with, that the system has a singularity of rank $r$ at $z=0$ and that in a neighbourhood $D$ of $z=0$ we have $\alpha=-A \mathrm{~d} z$, where
$$A=\frac{p}{z^{r+1}}+H$$
with $p$ a diagonal polynomial of degree $r$ with distinct diagonal entries throughout $D$ and $H$ holomorphic. By making a diagonal gauge transformation, we can make $H$ off-diagonal.

Pick constant diagonal matrices $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{n-2}$ which, together with $p(0)$, form a basis for the diagonal subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$, and for each $i$ let $H_{i}$ be the off-diagonal matrix with entries
$$\left(H_{i}\right)_{a b}=\frac{z H_{a b}\left(q_{i a}-q_{i b}\right)}{p_{a}-p_{b}}, \quad a \neq b,$$
where $q_{i a}$ and $p_{a}, a=1, \ldots, n$, are the diagonal entries in $q_{i}$ and $p$. Thus
$$\left[p, H_{i}\right]=\left[q_{i}, z H\right], \quad\left[q_{i}, H_{j}\right]=\left[q_{j}, H_{i}\right] .$$
Now introduce evolution equations for the diagonal matrix $p(z)$ and the off-diagonal matrix $H(z)$ as functions of the complex variables $t_{1}, \ldots, t_{n-1}$ by putting
$$\partial_{i} p=-r q_{i} \quad \partial_{i} H=\partial_{z} H_{i}-\left[H, H_{i}\right],$$
where $\partial_{i}=\partial / \partial t_{i}, \partial_{z}=\partial / \partial z$. The integrability of this system is established by showing that
$$\partial_{i} H_{j}-\partial_{j} H_{i}=\left[H_{i}, H_{j}\right] .$$
Since both sides are off-diagonal, this follows from (9) and
$$\begin{aligned}
{\left[p, \partial_{i} H_{j}\right]-\left[p, \partial_{j} H_{i}\right] } & =z\left[q_{j}, \partial_{i} H\right]-z\left[q_{i}, \partial_{j} H\right] \\
& =z\left[q_{j}, \partial_{z} H_{i}\right]-z\left[q_{i}, \partial_{z} H_{j}\right]-z\left[q_{j},\left[H, H_{i}\right]\right]+z\left[q_{i},\left[H, H_{j}\right]\right] \\
& =z\left[H_{i},\left[q_{j}, H\right]\right]-z\left[H_{j},\left[q_{i}, H\right]\right] \\
& =\left[p,\left[H_{i}, H_{j}\right]\right] .
\end{aligned}$$
So the evolution equations extend $H$ and $p$ to functions of $\left(z, t_{1}, \ldots t_{n-2}\right)$ on a neighbourhood $W$ of the origin in $\mathbb{C}^{n-2}$.

It follows from the definitions that
$$\nabla=\mathrm{d}-\frac{p \mathrm{~d} z}{z^{r+1}}-H \mathrm{~d} z-\sum_{i}\left(\frac{q_{i} \mathrm{~d} t_{i}}{z^{r}}+H_{i} \mathrm{~d} t_{i}\right)$$
is flat meromorphic connection on the trivial bundle principal bundle $P=G \times W$.
Let $\mathcal{Q}$ denote the quotient of a neighbourhood of the identity section in $P$ by the horizontal foliation. The foliation extends holomorphically to $z=0$ since it is spanned by
$$z^{r+1} \partial_{z}-p-z^{r+1} H, \quad z^{r} \partial_{i}-q_{i}-z^{r} H_{i} \quad(i=1, \ldots, n-2),$$
where $p, q_{i}, H, H_{i}$ are interpreted as right-invariant vector fields on $G$.
The quotient is a 'local twistor space' for $A$ in the sense that it carries a holomorphic $\mathfrak{g}$-action, which is free and transitive except on the hypersurface $S^{\prime}=\{z=0\}$, and contains a copy of $D$ on which the induced system is $\alpha$. Moreover, the fullness condition holds at $S$ since $p_{0}$ and the $q_{i}$ s span the diagonal subalgebra (in the case $n=2, p_{0}$ on its own does that). Any generic $A$ can be reduced to the form (8) by a holomorphic gauge transformation $g(z)$; so more generally a local twistor space can be constructed by applying the same gauge transformation to ∇ .

By using the $\mathfrak{g}$ action, we can identify $\mathcal{Q} \backslash S^{\prime}$ with $V \backslash S$, where $V$ is a neighbourhood in $\mathcal{Z}$ of the $z=0$ intersection point of $S$ and $X$. Then the embedded copy of $D \backslash\{0\}$ is mapped onto a punctured neighbourhood of the singularity in $X$. The identification allows us to replace $V$ by $\mathcal{Q}$. By repeating this for the other irregular singularities, we obtain a full twistor space. \(\square\)

Given the choice of coordinate $z$ in a neighbourhood of an irregular singularity, $p_{0}$ is a well-defined map $S \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$. Up to scale, $p_{0}$ is independent of the choice of $z$. We thus have a natural map $\left[p_{0}\right]: S \rightarrow \mathbb{P} \mathfrak{g}$. It is equivariant with respect to the $\mathfrak{g}$ action on $S$ and the adjoint action on $\mathbb{P} \mathfrak{g}$. The following is immediate from the proof above.

Proposition 3 A twistor space for a generic system is full at an irregular singularity if and only if $\left[p_{0}\right]: S \rightarrow \mathbb{P} \mathfrak{g}$ is regular at $X \cap S$.

When the space is not full, $\left[p_{0}\right](S)$ has nonzero codimension in $\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{g})$. In the generic case, the full twistor space is locally unique in the sense of the following proposition.

Proposition 4 Suppose that $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$ and $\left(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}, X\right)$ are full twistor spaces for a generic linear system of ODEs on a Riemann surface $X$, with $G=\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$. Then there are neighbourhoods $U \supset X$ and $U^{\prime} \supset X$ in $\mathcal{Z}$ and $\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}$ and a $\mathfrak{g}$ -equivariant biholomorphic map $\rho: U \rightarrow U^{\prime}$ such that $\rho(X)=X$.

If we exclude the poles from $X$ and the corresponding hypersurfaces $S$ and $S^{\prime}$ from $\mathcal{Z}$ and $\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}$, then $\rho$ is determined in a straightforward way by the $\mathfrak{g}$ actions on $\mathcal{Z}$ and $\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}$. It is defined by choosing a (multivalued) solution $y$ to the system on $X$ and then extending $y$ to a (multivalued) map from a neighbourhood of $X \backslash S$
in $\mathcal{Z} \backslash S$ to $G$ such that $\mathrm{d} y+\theta y=0$. Similarly $y$ extends to $y^{\prime}$ on $\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}$. Then the required map is $\rho=y^{-1} \circ y$, where the branches are chosen so that $\rho$ is the identity on $X$ ( $\rho$ is well defined since $y$ and $y^{\prime}$ have the same holonomy). The fact that $\rho$ extends holomorphically to $S$ in the full case is a corollary of Proposition 6 below.

\section*{The structure of S}

Suppose that $G=\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ and that $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$ is full. We denote by $\Gamma$ the space of parametrized curves
$$D \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}: z \mapsto \gamma(z),$$
where $D \subset \mathbb{C}$ is the unit disc, $\gamma(D)$ meets some component of $S$ transversally at $z=0$, and $\gamma$ extends smoothly to $|z|=1$.

We shall now develop a picture in which $\theta_{\gamma}=\gamma^{*}(\theta)$ is regarded as an element of the dual of Lie algebra of the loop group $L G .{ }^{3}$ Different elements of $\Gamma$ give different points of an orbit in $L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$ of an affine action of $L G$. We shall construct a finite-dimensional complex symplectic manifold from the orbit which determines the singular part of $\alpha$ at $z=0$.

\section*{The Fuchsian case}

If the singularity at $z=0$ is regular and generic, then any twistor space is full at $z=0$ and any holomorphic map $z \rightarrow \Omega(z) \in \mathfrak{g}$ generates a local holomorphic vector field tangent $Y$ to $S$. Moreover we can write
$$\theta=p_{0} \mathrm{~d}(\log z)+\theta^{\prime}$$
where $z=0$ on $S$ and $\theta^{\prime}$ is holomorphic on $S .{ }^{4}$ Then $p_{0}$ is independent of the choice of the function $z$, and therefore determines a natural map $\mu: S \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}^{*}-$ the identification being given by the bilinear form $\operatorname{tr}\left(\xi_{1}, \xi_{2}\right), \xi_{i} \in \mathfrak{g}$. The image $\mu(S)$ is open subset of a coadjoint orbit.

By evaluating $\Omega$ and $Y$ at $z=0$, we obtain a natural identification
$$T_{a} S=\mathfrak{g} /\left[p_{0}(a)\right] \quad a \in S .$$
We can therefore define a 2-form $\sigma$ on $S$ by
$$\sigma_{a}\left(Y, Y^{\prime}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(p_{0}\left[\Omega, \Omega^{\prime}\right]\right) .$$
This is closed and presymplectic since it is the pull back to $S$ by $\mu$ of the symplectic form on $\mu(S)$.

\footnotetext{
${ }^{3}$ An element of loop algebra is a map $B: S^{1} \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$. We define $\left\langle\theta_{\gamma}, B\right\rangle$ by integrating $\operatorname{tr}\left(B \theta_{\gamma}\right)$ around the unit circle.
${ }^{4}$ Thus $\theta$ has a logarithmic pole in the sense of Malgrange (1982).
}

\section*{The irregular case}

In the irregular case, the analogous structure involves information from the higher formal neighbourhoods of $S$. It arises from the action on $\Gamma$ of the group $L_{+} G$ of holomorphic maps $g: D \rightarrow G$ that extend smoothly to $\bar{D}$ : if $g \in L_{+} G$ then $\gamma \in \Gamma$, then $(g \gamma)(z)=\gamma(z) g(z)$. ${ }^{5}$

A tangent vector $Y$ to $\Gamma$ at $[\gamma]$ is a section of $\left.T \mathcal{Z}\right|_{\gamma}$, tangent to $S$ at $z=0$. Put
$$\sigma_{\gamma}\left(Y, Y^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\Omega \nabla \Omega^{\prime}\right),$$
where $\Omega=i_{Y} \theta, \Omega^{\prime}=i_{Y^{\prime}} \theta, \nabla \Omega=\mathrm{d} \Omega+[\theta, \Omega]$ and the integral is along a loop surrounding $z=0$. This form is closed, but degenerate (its closure follows from the construction below). Its characteristic distribution is integrable, by closure, and contains the $Y$ s for which $\Omega=O\left(z^{r+1}\right)$ as $z \rightarrow 0$. These span the orbits of the normal subgroup $L_{r+1} G \subset L_{+} G$ of maps $g: D \rightarrow G$ such that $g=1+O\left(z^{r+1}\right)$ as $z \rightarrow 0$. Since $\Gamma / L_{r+1} G$ is finite-dimensional, the quotient $\Gamma_{r}$ of $\Gamma$ by the characteristic distribition is a finite-dimensional symplectic manifold.

Since $\mathcal{Z}$ is full, the tangent space to $\Gamma_{r}$ at $[\gamma]$ is the set of holomorphic maps
$$\Omega: D \backslash\{0\} \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$$
such that (5) holds, modulo maps with zeros of order $r$ at $z=0$.

\section*{Affine orbits}

Let $L G$ denote the loop group of smooth maps $S^{1} \rightarrow G$ (Pressley and Segal, 1986). Its Lie algebra is the space $L \mathfrak{g}$ of smooth maps $\Omega: S^{1} \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$.

A 1 -form $\alpha$ on $S^{1}$ with values in $\mathfrak{g}$ determines an element of $L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$ by
$$\langle\alpha, \Omega\rangle=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}(\Omega \alpha)$$
Let $\mathcal{A}_{r} \subset L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$ denote the subspace of smooth $\alpha$ s that extend meromorphically to $D$ with a pole at the origin of order at most $r+1$, and no other poles. Thus
$$L \mathfrak{g}^{*} \supset \mathcal{A}_{r} \supset \mathcal{A}_{r-1} \supset \cdots \supset \mathcal{A}_{0} .$$

Like any dual Lie algebra, $(L \mathfrak{g})^{*}$ carries the standard Kostant-Kirillov-Souriau Poisson structure, which is preserved by the coadjoint action of $L G$. The natural symplectic arena for the isomonodromy problem is not, however, that of the corresponding coadjoint orbits, but rather that of the orbits of the affine action of $L \mathfrak{g}$ on $L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$ given by the cocycle
$$c\left(\Omega, \Omega^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\Omega \mathrm{~d} \Omega^{\prime}\right) .$$

\footnotetext{
${ }^{5}$ Of course this is well-defined only for $g$ close to the identity; what follows has to be qualified in a similar way.
}

Each $\Omega \in L \mathfrak{g}$ determines a vector field on $L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$, its value at $A \in L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$ being given by
$$\langle\delta \alpha, \cdot\rangle=\langle\alpha,[\Omega, \cdot]\rangle-c(\Omega, \cdot) .$$
The first term on the right-hand side is the usual coadjoint action; the second is a translation introduced by Souriau (1970) (see also Woodhouse 1990). ${ }^{6}$ By integrating the flow on $L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$, we obtain the gauge action of $L G$ :
$$\alpha \mapsto g^{-1} \alpha g+g^{-1} \mathrm{~d} g .$$
The symplectic structure on the corresponding orbits is ${ }^{7}$
$$\sigma\left(Y, Y^{\prime}\right)=c\left(\Omega, \Omega^{\prime}\right)-\left\langle\alpha,\left[\Omega, \Omega^{\prime}\right]\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\Omega \nabla \Omega^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\Omega \delta^{\prime} \alpha\right),$$
where $Y, Y^{\prime}$ are the vector fields generating the actions of $\Omega, \Omega^{\prime} \in L \mathfrak{g}$,
$$\nabla \Omega=\mathrm{d} \Omega+[\alpha, \Omega],$$
and $\delta^{\prime} \alpha$ is the variation induced by $Y^{\prime}$. The flow of $\Omega \in L \mathfrak{g}$ is generated by
$$h(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}(\alpha \Omega) .$$
However, $\left[h_{A}, h_{B}\right]=h_{[A, B]}+c(A, B)$, so the action is not Hamiltonian: the cocycle is the obstruction to the existence of a moment map.

\section*{The symplectic structure of $M_{r}$}

Let $\mathcal{O}$ be the affine orbit of some generic element of $\mathcal{A}_{r}$; that is, an element of the form
$$g^{-1} \mathrm{~d} g-g^{-1}\left(\frac{p}{z^{r+1}}+H\right) g,$$
where $g \in L_{+} G, H \in L_{+} \mathfrak{g}$ are holomorphic on $D$, and $p$ is a polynomial in $z$ with distinct eigenvalues for $z \in D$.

\footnotetext{
${ }^{6}$ For a general Lie group, the affine action is symplectic, but not Hamiltonian when $c$ is not a coboundary. In fact the affine orbits are the models for the non-Hamiltonian transitive symplectic actions of the group in the same way that the coadjoint orbits are the models for the Hamiltonian actions. If $c$ is the obstruction to the existence of a moment map for some transitive symplectic action of the Lie algebra on a symplectic manifold $M$, then $M$ can be mapped equivariantly and symplectically to an affine orbit in the dual Lie algebra. The affine orbits have an alternative description in terms of the coadjoint orbits of the central extension determined by $c$, but this is less convenient for our purposes here. See Pressley and Segal (1986), p. 44.
${ }^{7}$ This is a good definition of $\sigma$ since the right-hand side vanishes whenever $\Omega_{1}$ fixes $A$, and therefore whenever $Y_{1}$ vanishes at $A$. We shall not need to consider the precise sense in which $\sigma$ is nondegenerate, since we shall deal only with finite-dimensional submanifolds.
}

A general element $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}$ is a smooth 1-form on $S^{1}$ with values in $\mathfrak{g}$. For any $\alpha, \alpha^{\prime} \in \mathcal{O}$, the two systems
$$\mathrm{d} y+\alpha y=0, \quad \mathrm{~d} y+\alpha^{\prime} y=0$$
on the circle have the same monodromy matrix $M$ up to conjugacy, since that is the condition that $y$ and $\hat{y}$ can be chosen so that $g=y(z) \hat{y}(z)^{-1}$ is single valued; $g$ is then the element of $L G$ that maps one system into the other.

Since $c$ vanishes on $L_{+} \mathfrak{g}$, the action of $L G$ on $\mathcal{O}$ is Hamiltonian when restricted to $L_{r+1} G$, the subgroup of loops of the form $1+z^{r+1} h$, where $h$ is holomorphic. We denote by $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ the Marsden-Weinstein reduction of $\mu_{r+1}^{-1}(0)$, where $\mu_{r+1}$ is the moment map. That is, $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ is the quotient of $\mathcal{A}_{r} \cap \mathcal{O}(A)$ by the action of $L_{r+1} G$. It a finite-dimensional complex symplectic manifold, with symplectic form that we shall again denote by $\sigma$. Its points are elements of $\mathcal{A}_{r} \cap \mathcal{O}(A)$, modulo gauge transformations of the form
$$\alpha \mapsto g^{-1} \mathrm{~d} g-g^{-1} \alpha g, \quad g \in L_{r+1} G .$$
A tangent vector $Y \in T_{\alpha} \mathcal{M}_{r}$ is of the form
$$\delta \alpha=\nabla \Omega=\mathrm{d} \Omega+[\alpha, \Omega]$$
where $\Omega$ satisfies (5); two such $\Omega$ s define the same tangent vector whenever their difference is in $L_{r+1} \mathfrak{g}$.

We can construct from $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}_{r}$ the following objects (see appendix).
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] The singularity data $(m, t)$, where $t$ is a diagonal polynomial of degree $r-1$ and $m$ is the exponent of formal monodromy.
\item[(ii)] The formal series $g_{\mathrm{f}}=\sum g_{i} z^{i}$.
\item[(iii)] The matrices $C_{i}$, defined as follows. For each $\alpha$, choose a solution $y$ to the corresponding system with fixed monodromy matrix $M$ and choose $2 r$ sectors $\mathcal{S}_{i}$ at $z=0$, as in (19). Then put $C_{i}=y_{i}(z)^{-1} y(z)$, where $y_{i}$ is the corresponding special solution and we continue $y$ in the positive sense around $z=0$ into the sector $\mathcal{S}_{i}$. If we put
$$y_{2 r+1}=y_{1} \mathrm{e}^{2 \pi \mathrm{i} m} \quad \mathcal{S}_{2 r+1}=\mathcal{S}_{1},$$
then $C_{2 r+1}=\mathrm{e}^{-2 \pi \mathrm{i} m} C_{1} M$ and we can define the Stokes' matrices by $S_{i}=C_{i} C_{i+1}^{-1}(i=1, \ldots 2 r)$.
\end{itemize}
These are not quite uniquely determined: we are free to make the replacement
$$g_{\mathrm{f}} \mapsto g_{\mathrm{f}} T, \quad C_{i} \mapsto T^{-1} C_{i}, \quad S_{i} \mapsto T^{-1} S_{i} T,$$
where T is diagonal and independent of $z$. We shall express the symplectic form on $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ in terms of these variables.

Given $t$ and the monodromy matrix $M$, the Stokes' matrices and the exponent of formal monodromy satisfy two constraints.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(C1)] Let $P_{i}$ be the matrix of the permutation that puts the real parts of diagonal entries in $z^{-r} t$ is increasing order in $\mathcal{S}_{i} \cap \mathcal{S}_{i+1}$. Then for each $i, P_{i}^{-1} S_{i} P_{i}$ is upper triangular and $P_{i} S_{i+1} P_{i}^{-1}$ is lower triangular, both with ones on the diagonal. This follows from the fact that
$$\exp \left(z^{-r} t+m \log z\right) S_{i} \exp \left(-z^{-r} t-m \log z\right) \rightarrow 1$$
faster than any power of $z$ as $z \rightarrow 0$ in $\mathcal{S}_{i} \cap \mathcal{S}_{i+1}$.
\item[(C2)] The product $\mathrm{e}^{-2 \pi \mathrm{i} m} S_{1} \ldots S_{2 r}$ is conjugate to $M^{-1}$.
\end{itemize}

Denote by $\mathcal{C}_{r}$ the set matrices $S_{i} \in \operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C}), m$ diagonal and trace-free, satisfying these two constraints. Given a point $\mathcal{C}_{r}$, we choose $C_{1}$ such that
$$\mathrm{e}^{-2 \pi \mathrm{i} m} S_{1} \ldots S_{2 r}=C_{1} M^{-1} C_{1}^{-1}$$
and define $C_{2}, \ldots, C_{2 r+1}$ by $C_{i+1}=S_{i}^{-1} C_{i}$. Put
$$\omega=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \sum_{1}^{2 r} \operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{~d} C_{i} C_{i}^{-1} \otimes \mathrm{~d} S_{i} S_{i}^{-1}\right)+\pi \mathrm{i} \operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{~d} m \otimes \mathrm{~d} m)-\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{d} C_{1} C_{1}^{-1} \otimes \mathrm{~d} m\right) .$$
It is shown in the appendix that $\omega$ is skew-symmetric, and in fact a symplectic form on $\mathcal{C}_{r}$.

For each point of $\mathcal{M}_{r}$, we pick a representative in $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}_{r}$. We then define 1-forms on $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ by
$$\Theta=\mathrm{d} g g^{-1}+\frac{g \mathrm{~d} t g^{-1}}{z^{r}} \quad \gamma=g_{0}^{-1} \mathrm{~d} g_{0},$$
where d is the exterior derivative on $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ and $g$ is the polynomial obtained by truncating the formal power series $g_{\mathrm{f}}$ at some large power of $r$. With this notation, the symplectic form on $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 5 The symplectic form on $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ is
$$\sigma=\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\Theta \wedge \nabla \Theta^{\prime}\right)+\operatorname{tr}(\gamma \wedge \mathrm{d} m)-\omega .$$
where $\nabla \Omega=\partial_{z} \Omega \mathrm{~d} z+[\alpha, \Omega]$.
The proof is by splitting the integral in the definition of $\sigma$ into sections lying in the various sectors, and then shrinking the contour to zero. The details are given in the appendix.

The formula for $\sigma$ is independent of the choices made in defining the variables on $\mathcal{M}_{r}$. In particular, it depends on the first $r$ terms in the formal series since the right-hand side of the formula is unchanged when $\Theta$ is replaced by $\mathrm{d} h h^{-1}+h \Theta h^{-1}$,
where $h=1+O\left(z^{r+1}\right)$. It is also unchanged when $g, C_{i}$ are replaced by $g T, T^{-1} C_{i}$ where $T$ is diagonal and indpendent of $z$.

We can see the local structure of $\mathcal{M}_{r}$ from the proposition. The submanifolds on which $g$ and $t$ are constant (up to the freedom 12) are symplectomorphic to $\mathcal{C}_{r}$. While, those on which $m$ and $S_{i}$ are constant (up to 12) are symplectomorphic to a fixed manifold $\mathcal{P}_{r}$; by mapping $[\gamma] \in \Gamma_{r}$ to $[\alpha]$, where $\alpha=\gamma^{*}(\theta)$, and by noting the coincidence of the formulas for the symplectic forms, we identify $\mathcal{P}_{r}$ with $\Gamma_{r}$.

\section*{Local uniqueness of the full twistor space}

Let $\mathcal{P}$ denote the subset of $\mathcal{O} \cap \mathcal{A}_{r}$ given by the fixing the values of the Stokes' matrices and exponent of formal monodromy. By using the actions of $L_{r+1} G$ on $\Gamma$ and $\mathcal{P}$ to pick represenetatives in $[\gamma]$ and $[\alpha]$, we can identify $\Gamma$ with an open neighbourhood in $\mathcal{P}$ so that $\gamma \in \Gamma$ corresponds to $\alpha \in \mathcal{P}$ such at $\alpha=\gamma^{*}(\theta)$. We then deduce the following proposition.

Proposition 6 Suppose that $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$ and $\left(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}, X\right)$ are full twistor spaces for a generic linear system of ODEs on a Riemann surface $X$, with $G=\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$. Let $a \in X$ be a pole of order $r+1>1$. Then there are neighbourhoods $U, U^{\prime}$ of a in $\mathcal{Z}$ and $\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}$ and a $\mathfrak{g}$-equivariant biholomorphic map $\rho: U \rightarrow U^{\prime}$ such that $\rho(a)=a$ and $\rho(X \cap U)=X \cap U^{\prime}$.

Proof. Choose a coordinate $z$ on a small disc in $X$ such that the pole is at $z=0$, and extend this to a neighbourhood of $a$ in $\mathcal{Z}$ so that $S$ is given by $z=0$. Then we can identify a neighbourhood of $a$ with $S \times D$, as before. For each $s \in S$, we have a holomorphic map $\gamma_{s}: D \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}$ and hence an element $\alpha_{s}$ of $\mathcal{P}$ such that $\gamma_{s}^{*}=\alpha_{s}$. Let $\gamma_{s}^{\prime}: D \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}^{\prime}$ be the corresponding map into the second twistor space. Then the required biholomorphic map is $\rho:(s, z) \mapsto \gamma_{s}^{\prime}(z)$. \(\square\)

Proposition 4 above is an immediate corollary, since Proposition 6 implies that the map $\rho$ constructed there extends to $S$.

\section*{Isomonodromic deformations}

We have shown that a generic $\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ system of the form (2) on a Riemann surface can be generated from a twistor space $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$, and that, if we require $\mathcal{Z}$ to be full, then it is unique, at least in a neighbourhood of $X$. In the case of Fuchsian equations on $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$, Hitchin (1995a) showed that the isomonodromic deformations of the system are given by the deformations of $X$ in $\mathcal{Z}$ (every twistor space being full in the Fuchsian case). This is also true more generally, as we shall now see.

By Kodaira's theorem, the deformations of a compact curve $X \subset \mathcal{Z}$ are determined by the properties of the normal bundle $N=\left.T \mathcal{Z}\right|_{X} / T X$. Put
$$d_{1}=\operatorname{dim} H^{1}(X, N) \quad \text { and } \quad d_{0}=\operatorname{dim} H^{0}(X, N) .$$

When $d_{1}=0$, the theorem implies that $X$ is one of a complete $d_{0}$-parameter holomorphic family of embedded curves. The tangent space to the parameter space at $X$ is naturally identified with $H^{0}(X, N)$ (Kodaira 1962).

For each curve $X$ in the family, we have a meromorphic 1-form $\left.\theta\right|_{X}$ and hence a system of differential equations of the form (2). As we vary the curve along a path $X_{t}$ in the family, $t \in[0,1]$, the tangent at $t$ is an element of $H^{0}\left(X_{t}, N\right)$. This we represent by local sections $Y_{i}$ of $\left.T \mathcal{Z}\right|_{X_{t}}$, chosen to be tangent to $S$ at the poles. Thus the $Y_{i}$ s are uniquely determined up to the addition local tangent vector fields to $X_{t}$ that vanish at $S \cap X_{t}$. Put $\Omega_{i}=i_{Y_{i}} \theta, \alpha_{t}=\left.\theta\right|_{X_{t}}$, and identify local neighbourhoods in the $X_{t} \mathrm{~s}$ along $Y_{i}$. Then $\Omega_{i}$ is meromorphic, with a pole of order $r$ at a singularity of rank $r$. By (3),
$$\partial_{t} \alpha_{t}=\nabla_{\alpha_{t}} \Omega_{i}=\mathrm{d} \Omega_{i}+\left[\alpha_{t}, \Omega_{i}\right] .$$
Moreover on the overlap of their domains, $\Omega_{i}-\Omega_{j}=i_{T_{i j}} \alpha_{t} \mathrm{~d} z$ for some tangent vector $T_{i j}$ to $X$, which must vanish at any poles in the overlap. By using the results in the appendix, we deduce the following proposition.

Proposition 7 Let $G=\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$, let $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$ be a twistor space, and let $X^{\prime}$ be a deformation of $X$. Then the linear system of ordinary differential equations on $X^{\prime}$ is an isomonodromic deformation of the linear system on $X$.

\section*{The minimal twistor space}

In the minimal case, we can find the normal bundle of $X$ as follows. For $x \in X$, put $L_{\alpha}(x)=\alpha\left(T_{x} X\right) \subset \mathfrak{g}$ when $x$ is not a pole; and
$$L_{\alpha}(x)=z^{r+1} \alpha\left(T_{x} X\right),$$
when $x$ is a pole of rank $r$ and $z=0$ at $x$. Then $L_{A} \rightarrow X_{0}$ is a holomorphic line bundle. Moreover $\alpha$ is a global meromorphic section of $L_{\alpha} \otimes K$ with a pole of order $r+1$ at a singularity of rank $r$ and, by assumption, no zeros. Therefore
$$L_{\alpha} \otimes K=-\sum\left(r_{i}+1\right) a_{\alpha}$$
and so $\operatorname{deg} L_{\alpha}=2-2 g-\sum\left(r_{i}+1\right)$, where the sum is over the singularities and $g$ is the genus.

Now a point $x \in X$ is the image of $(x, e) \in X \times G$ under the projection along $F$. So we have
$$N_{x}=T_{(x, e)}(X \times G) /\left(T_{x} \oplus L_{\alpha}\right) .$$
Thus there is a short exact sequence
$$0 \rightarrow L_{A} \rightarrow \mathfrak{g} \rightarrow N \rightarrow 0,$$
where $\mathfrak{g}$ is the trivial bundle $\mathfrak{g}$-bundle over $X$.

When $X=\mathbb{C P}_{1}$ and $\sum\left(r_{i}+1\right) \geq 4$, we have $H^{1}(\mathfrak{g})=0, H^{0}\left(L_{A}\right)=0$, and
$$\operatorname{dim} H^{1}\left(L_{A}\right)=-3+\sum\left(r_{i}+1\right) .$$
From the corresponding long exact sequence, therefore, $H^{1}(N)=0$, and
$$0 \rightarrow H^{0}(\mathfrak{g}) \rightarrow H^{0}(N) \rightarrow H^{1}\left(L_{A}\right) \rightarrow 0 .$$
It follows that
$$d_{1}=0, \quad d_{0}=\sum\left(r_{i}+1\right)-3+\operatorname{dim} G,$$
We conclude that $X$ is one of a $d_{0}$-parameter of embedded copies of $\mathbb{C} \mathbb{P}_{1}$. If all the singularities are regular, then $d_{0}$ is the dimension of the space of configurations of poles $\left(\sum\left(r_{i}+1\right)-3\right)$, plus the dimension of $G$. The deformations of $X$ are parametrized by the positions of the poles, modulo the action of $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$ on $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$, together with constant gauge transformations.

Example. The Schlesinger equations Suppose that $X=\mathbb{C P}_{1}$ and that all the singularities are regular. We choose the domains of the $\Omega_{i} \mathrm{~s}$ so that each pole lies in only one. The $\Omega_{i} \mathrm{~s}$ are holomorphic and the deformation is determined by the holomorphic tangent vector fields $T_{i j}$ on the overlaps of the domains. Since $H^{1}(X, T X)=0$, we have $T_{i j}=T_{i}-T_{j}$, where $T_{i}$ is holomorphic on the domain of $\Omega_{i}$ (but possibly non-zero at the corresponding pole). So if we put $Y=Y_{i}+T_{i}$, then $Y$ is a global section of $\left.T \mathcal{Z}\right|_{X}$.

Let $z$ be a global stereographic coordinate on $X$, with $z=\infty$ not one of the poles, and use $Y$ to transfer $z$ along the deformations of $X$. Then
$$\alpha=-\sum \frac{A_{i} \mathrm{~d} z}{z-a_{i}}$$
where the $a_{i}$ s are positions of the poles and the coefficients $A_{i}$ are independent of $z$ and satisfy
$$\sum_{i} A_{i}=0$$
(since there is no pole at infinity). Put $\Omega=i_{Y} \theta$. Then $\Omega$ is meromorphic with simple poles at the $a_{i}$ s and
$$\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial z}-[A, \Omega] .$$
In fact, since $\Omega_{i}$ is holomorphic at $a_{i}$ and $T_{i}\left(a_{i}\right)=\partial a_{i} / \partial t$, we have
$$\Omega=-\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial t} \frac{A_{i}}{z-a_{i}}+O\left(\left(z-a_{i}\right)^{0}\right) .$$
It follows that
$$\Omega=-\sum_{i} \frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial t} \frac{A_{i}}{z-a_{i}}+k$$
where $k$ is a matrix independent of $z$. Therefore
$$\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial t}=-\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial t} \frac{\left[A_{i}, A_{j}\right]}{a_{i}-a_{j}}+\left[k, A_{i}\right],$$
which is a form of the Schlesinger equations. The last term is simply a infinitesimal gauge transformation; the first gives the dependence of the $A_{i} \mathrm{~s}$ on the configuration of the poles.

\section*{The full case}

In a full twistor space, $d_{0}$ is generally larger than in the minimal construction. Here we can find $N$ in another way.

We suppose that all the singularities are generic. Then, by (5) and Lemma 1, a local section $V$ of $\left.T \mathcal{Z}\right|_{X}$ is a map $\Omega$ from a neighbourhood in $X$ to $\mathfrak{g}$ such that a singularity at of rank $r$,
$$\Omega=O\left(z^{-r}\right), \quad[\alpha, \Omega]=O\left(z^{-r-1}\right),$$
where $z$ is a local coordinate that vanishes at the singularity. That is, in a local gauge in which the singular part of $\alpha$ is diagonal, the diagonal entries in $\Omega$ have poles of at most order $r$, and the off-diagonal entries are holomorphic. Thus these algebraic conditions charaterize $\Omega$ as a local section of a holomorphic bundle $E \rightarrow X$ with fibre $\mathfrak{g}$ (and therefore rank $n^{2}-1$ ) and degree $\sum(n-1) r_{i}$. In the full case, therefore, we have that $T \mathcal{Z} \mid X=E$ can be constructed directly from the positions and ranks of the singularities of the ODE on $X$.

Put
$$L=T X \otimes \sum\left(-a_{i}\right),$$
so that a local holomorphic section of $L$ is a tangent vector field that vanishes at the poles. Then we have a short exact sequence
$$0 \rightarrow L \rightarrow E \rightarrow N \rightarrow 0$$
with the second map given by contraction with $\alpha$. Hence there is an exact sequence
$$0 \rightarrow H^{0}(L) \rightarrow H^{0}(E) \rightarrow H^{0}(N) \rightarrow H^{1}(L) \rightarrow H^{1}(E) \rightarrow H^{1}(N) \rightarrow 0 .$$
If the genus of $X$ is $g$, and if there are $k$ singularities in total, then
$$\operatorname{deg}(L)=2-2 g-k, \quad \operatorname{dim} H^{0}(E)-\operatorname{dim} H^{1}(E)=\left(n^{2}-1\right)(1-g)+(n-1) \sum r_{i},$$
(the latter identity coming from the Riemann-Roch theorem).

A global section of $E$ is a meromorphic map $\Omega: X \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}$ such that at a singularity of rank $r$,
$$\Omega=z^{-r} g_{\mathrm{f}} q g_{\mathrm{f}}^{-1}+O\left(z^{0}\right)$$
where $z$ is a local coordinate that vanishes at the singularity and $q$ is a diagonal polynomial of degree $r-1$. When $X=\mathbb{C P}_{1}, \Omega$ is determined as a global rational map by the $q \mathrm{~s}$ up to the addition of a constant element of $\mathfrak{g}$, and the $q \mathrm{~s}$ can be specified independently. In this case, therefore, $\operatorname{dim} H^{0}(E)=(n-1) \sum r_{i}+\operatorname{dim} G$, and $\operatorname{dim} H^{1}(E)=0$. Moreover if $k \geq 4$, then
$$\operatorname{dim} H^{0}(L)=0, \quad \operatorname{dim} H^{1}(L)=k-3 .$$
It follows that
$$d_{0}=H^{0}(N)=(n-1) \sum r_{i}+\operatorname{dim} G+k-3, \quad d_{1}=\operatorname{dim} H^{1}(N)=0 .$$
If either $n=2(G=\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbb{C}))$ or $\sum r_{i}=0$ (all singularities regular), then $d_{0}$ is the same as in the minimal case; in either of these cases, the minimal twistor space is full and, by Proposition 8 below, it gives all possible isomonodromic deformations. In general, however, there are more isomonodromic deformations than are given by the minimal construction: the additional parameters are the coefficients of the diagonal polynomials $t$ (of degree $r-1$ ) at the irregular singularities $(r \geq 1)$.

When $X$ has higher genus, $\operatorname{dim} H^{1}(E)=\operatorname{dim} H^{0}\left(E^{*} \otimes K\right)^{*}$ is generically zero whenever $(n-1) \sum r_{i}>n^{2} g+g-2$.

\section*{Twistor curves}

Let $\mathrm{d} y+\alpha y=0$ be a generic system on a compact Riemann surface $X$ and suppose that $H^{1}(E)=0$. Then we can construct a full twistor space $(\mathcal{Z}, X)$. Since $H^{1}(X, N)=0, X$ is one of a complete holomorphic family $\mathcal{K}$ of curves $X \subset \mathcal{Z}$.

Proposition 8 Let $\left(X_{t}, \alpha_{t}\right), t \in[0,1]$, be an isomonodromic deformation of $(X, \alpha)$. Then for small $t$, there is a path $X_{t}$ in $\mathcal{K}$ such that $\alpha_{t}=\left.\theta\right|_{X_{t}}$.

Proof. Let $y_{t}$ be solution to
$$\mathrm{d} y_{t}+\theta_{t} y_{t}=0$$
with constant monodromy, and with constant connection matrices $C_{i}$ to the special solutions at the poles.

Let $z, z^{\prime} \in X$ be nearby points (neither a pole) and let $g, g^{\prime} \in G$ be close to the identity. Then, by integrating the action of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathcal{Z}$, we have two points $z g$, $z^{\prime} g^{\prime}$ in $\mathcal{Z}$ near $X$. These are the same if
$$g g^{\prime-1}=y_{0}(z) y_{0}\left(z^{\prime}\right)^{-1} .$$

Let $z_{t} \in X_{t}$ vary continuously with $t$, and suppose that $z_{t}$ is not a pole for any small $t$. Put
$$\rho_{t}\left(z_{t}\right)=z_{0} y_{0}\left(z_{0}\right) y_{t}\left(z_{t}\right)^{-1} \in \mathcal{Z}$$
(the right-hand side is interpreted by regarding $z_{0} \in X$ as a point of $X \subset \mathcal{Z}$ and by using the local action of $G$ on $\mathcal{Z}$ ). This is independent of the choice of branch of $y_{t}$ and $y_{0}$ (so long as we make the choice of branch continuously) since $y_{0}$ and $y_{t}$ have the same monodromy. Moreover, $\rho_{t}\left(z_{t}\right)$ depends only on $z_{t}$, and not on the path, by (15). So if we exclude a small neighbourhood of each pole in $X_{t}$, then we can embed the complement in $\mathcal{Z}$ by $z_{t} \mapsto \rho_{t}\left(z_{t}\right)$. By fixing $z_{0}$ and moving $z_{t}$, we see from (16) that $\rho_{t}^{*} \theta=\alpha_{t}$.

It remains to show that $\rho_{t}$ extends holomorphically to the poles. Consider one of the poles (a point of $X_{t}$, varying continuously with $t$ ). We can choose a coordinate $z$ in a neighbourhood $D$ of the pole on each $X_{t}$ so that $D$ is the unit disc and the pole is at $z=0$. Then, for small $t$, since $\mathcal{Z}$ is full, there exists a holomorphic map $\gamma_{t}: D \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}$ such that $\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=\gamma_{t}^{*} \theta$ has the same singularity data at $z=0$ as $\alpha_{t}$ has at $z=0$. Since $\alpha_{t}$ is an isomonodromic deformation, $\alpha_{t}$ and $\alpha_{t}^{\prime}$ also have the same Stokes' matrices.

Let $y_{t}^{\prime}$ be a solution to
$$\mathrm{d} y_{t}^{\prime}+\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=0$$
with the same monodromy and connection matrices to the special solutions in the sectors at $z=0$ as $y_{0}$. Then
$$\gamma_{t}(z)=z y_{0}(z) y_{t}^{\prime}(z)^{-1} .$$
Further $y_{t}^{\prime} y_{t}^{-1}$ is holomorphic at $z=0$. This is because it is single-valued, since $y_{t}$ and $y_{t}^{\prime}$ have the same holonomy, and bounded since in any sector $\mathcal{S}$ at $z=0$
$$y_{t}^{\prime} y_{t}^{-1}=y_{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime} y_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1} \sim g_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime} g_{\mathrm{f}}^{-1}$$
where $y_{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}, y_{\mathcal{S}}$ are the corresponding special solutions and $g_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}, g_{\mathrm{f}}$ are the formal gauge transformations to diagonal form. So the embedding $\rho_{t}$ extends by mapping $z \in D \subset X_{t}$ to $\gamma_{t}(z) y_{t}^{\prime} y_{t}^{-1}$. \(\square\)

\section*{Isomonodromic flows for systems on the Riemann sphere}

The number of independent isomonodromic deformations (the dimension of $\mathcal{K}$ ) of a generic system on $X=\mathbb{C} \mathbb{P}_{1}$,
$$\operatorname{dim} H^{0}(X, N)=(n-1) \sum r_{i}+\operatorname{dim} G+k-3 .$$
We shall now show that the deformations are given by Hamiltonian flows on symplectic manifolds constructed from the affine orbits in $L \mathfrak{g}^{*}$.

In this case, the twistor curves in $\mathcal{Z}$ are copies of $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$, and they can be parametrized by a global stereographic coordinate $z \in \mathbb{C} \cup\{\infty\}$. We denote by $\hat{\mathcal{K}}$ the space of parametrized curves, which has dimension
$$\operatorname{dim} \hat{\mathcal{K}}=(n-1) \sum r_{i}+\operatorname{dim} G+k .$$
The points of $\hat{\mathcal{K}}$ can be labelled by the positions of the poles ( $k$ parameters), the polynomials $t$ at each pole $\left((n-1) \sum r_{i}\right.$ parameters $)$ and a choice of gauge ( $\operatorname{dim} G$ parameters).

An element of $\mathcal{K}$ is a mapping $\rho: \mathbb{C P}_{1} \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}$ from some fixed copy of the Riemann sphere. It determines a rational $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1 -form
$$\alpha=\rho^{*} \theta=-A \mathrm{~d} z,$$
where $A$ is rational, with poles of order $r_{i}+1$ at $k$ points $a_{1}, \ldots, a_{k}$ (we assume that none of the poles is at infinity, so $A=O\left(z^{-2}\right)$ as $z \rightarrow \infty$ ). In a neighbourhood of $a_{i}$, we put $z_{i}=z-a_{i}$ and assume, without of loss of generality, that no other pole lies in the closure of the disc $D_{i}=\left\{\left|z_{i}\right|<1\right\}$. Then $\alpha_{i}=\left.\alpha\right|_{D_{i}}$ determines a point of the symplectic manifold $\mathcal{M}_{r_{i}}$. Thus we have a map
$$\hat{\mathcal{K}} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{r_{1}} \times \mathcal{M}_{r_{2}} \times \cdots \times \mathcal{M}_{r_{k}} .$$
It is not surjective, since a given point of $\mathcal{M}$ is not, in general, given by the restrictions of a global 1-form $\alpha$. However $\alpha$ is uniquely determined by the positions of its poles and by its image in $\mathcal{M}$, since the difference between two $\alpha \mathrm{s}$ with the same pole positions, and determining the same point of $\mathcal{M}$, is a global holomorphic 1-form, and therefore vanishes.

Given $\left[\alpha_{i}\right] \in \mathcal{M}_{r_{i}}$ and the points $a_{i} \in \mathbb{C} \mathbb{P}_{1}$, we put $\alpha_{i}=-A_{i} \mathrm{~d} z_{i}$ and denote by $A_{i-}$ and $A_{i+}$ the negative and non-negative degree terms in the Laurent expansion of $A_{i}$ in powers of $z_{i}$ in a neighbourhood of $a_{i}$. Given also a diagonal polynomial $q_{i}$ of degree $r_{i}-1$, we put
$$\Omega_{q_{i}}=\left(z^{-r_{i}} g_{\mathrm{f}}^{-1} q_{i} g_{\mathrm{f}}\right)_{-}$$
where $g_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the formal gauge transformation to the diagonal form of $\alpha_{i}$ and again the minus subscript denotes the negative terms in the Laurent expansion in powers of $z_{i}$. Then $A_{i-}$ and $\Omega_{q_{i}}$ are global meromorphic functions on $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$ with values in $\mathfrak{g}$; they are holomorphic except at $a_{i}$, where they have poles of order $r_{i}+1$ and $r_{i}$, respectively. Moreover $A_{i-}$ and $\Omega_{i}$ are independent of the choice of representative in $\left[\alpha_{i}\right]$.

Proposition 9 The isomonodromic deformations of a generic $\operatorname{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ system on $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$ are generated by the Hamiltonians
$$h_{v}=\sum_{j} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{j} v\right), \quad h_{i}=\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{j} A_{i-}\right), \quad h_{q_{i}}=\sum_{j} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{j} \Omega_{q_{i}}\right),$$
on $\mathcal{M}$, where $v$ is a constant element of $\mathfrak{g}$ and the integrals are around small circles surrounding the poles.

The $h_{i}$ s are time-dependent Hamiltonians, the 'times' being the positions $a_{i}$ of the poles.

Proof. First we note that the Hamiltonians $h_{v}$ generate the constant gauge transformations. Consider next the flow generated by $h_{i}$. We shall find the value of the Hamiltonian vector field at a point of $\mathcal{M}$ constructed from a global meromorphic 1-form $\alpha$. To do this, we must we must evaluate the gradient of $h_{i}$ at such a point. We have
$$\begin{aligned}
\delta h_{i} & =\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\delta \alpha_{j} A_{i-}+\alpha \delta A_{i-}\right) \\
& =-\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{i} \delta A_{i-}\right)+\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\delta \alpha_{j} A_{i-}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
However
$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{i} \delta A_{i-}\right) & =\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{i+} \delta A_{i}\right) \\
& =\frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(A_{i+} \delta \alpha_{i}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
We conclude that the value of the Hamiltonian vector field at such a point is
$$\delta \alpha_{i}=-\nabla A_{i+}, \quad \delta \alpha_{j}=\nabla A_{i-} \quad j \neq i .$$

The claim is that this is tangent to an isomonodromic deformation. To see this, let $y$ be a solution to $\mathrm{d} y+\alpha y=0$, let $D$ be a disc containing $a_{i}$, but no other pole, and let $D^{\prime}$ be a second disc not containing $a_{i}$ such that $D, D^{\prime}$ is an open cover of $\mathbb{C P}_{1}$. For small $t$, put $F_{t}(z)=y(z-t) y(z)^{-1}$. Then $F: D \cap D^{\prime} \rightarrow G$ is single-valued, holomorphic, and equal to the identity when $t=0$. By Birkhoff's theorem, $F_{t}=h_{t}^{-1} h_{t}^{\prime}$ for some holomorphic maps $h_{t}: D \rightarrow G, h_{t}^{\prime}: D^{\prime} \rightarrow G$, with $h_{t}^{\prime}(\infty)=1$.

Put
$$y_{t}(z)= \begin{cases}h_{t}(z) y(z-t) & z \in D \\ h_{t}^{\prime}(z) y(t) & z \in D^{\prime}\end{cases}$$
and $\alpha_{t}=-\mathrm{d} y_{t} y_{t}^{-1}$. Then the definitions agree on $D \cap D^{\prime}$ and $\alpha_{t}$ is a global meromorphic 1-form with poles at $z=a_{j}(j \neq i)$ and $z=a_{i}+t$. Moreover $\partial_{t} y_{t}=\Omega_{t} y_{t}$, where
$$\Omega_{t}= \begin{cases}\partial_{t} h_{t} h_{t}^{-1}-h_{t} A_{i}(z-t) h_{t}^{-1} & \text { in } D \\ \partial_{t} h_{t}^{\prime} h_{t}^{\prime-1} & \text { in } D^{\prime} .\end{cases}$$

Since $h_{t}$ and $h_{t}^{\prime}$ are holomorphic in $D$ and $D^{\prime}$, it follows that the deformation is isomonodromic (see Proposition 11).

At $t=0$, we have $h_{t}=h_{t}^{\prime}=1$ and
$$\partial_{t} h_{t}^{\prime}=A_{i-} \quad \partial_{t} h_{t}=-A_{i+} ;$$
we also have at $t=0$ that $\delta \alpha_{i}=\nabla\left(\partial_{t} h_{t}\right), \delta \alpha_{j}=\nabla\left(\partial_{t} h_{t}^{\prime}\right)$ for $j \neq i$. So the tangent to the deformation is the Hamiltonian vector field constructed above: these deformations move the poles, but leave the singularity data unchanged.

Now consider the flow generated by $h_{q_{i}}$. Proceeding as before to calculate the value of the Hamiltonian vector field at a point given by a global 1-form $\alpha$, we have
$$\begin{aligned}
\delta h_{q_{i}} & =\sum_{j} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\delta \alpha_{j} \Omega_{q_{i}}+\alpha_{j} \delta \Omega_{q_{i}}\right) \\
& =\sum_{j} \frac{1}{2 \pi \mathrm{i}} \oint \operatorname{tr}\left(\alpha_{j} \delta \Omega_{q_{i}}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
So in this case, the value of the Hamiltonian vector field is
$$\delta \alpha_{i}=\nabla_{\alpha} \Omega_{q_{i}},$$
which is clearly isomonodromic. These deformations change the singularity data at $a_{i}$, leaving the position of the poles unchanged. \(\square\)

Remark. The Hamiltonians $H_{q_{i}}$ vanish on the orbit of a global 1-form $\alpha$, while
$$h_{i}=\frac{1}{\tau} \frac{\partial \tau}{\partial a_{i}},$$
where $\tau$ is as in Jimbo et al (1981a).
Example. In the case of a generic system with only regular singularities, $\mathcal{M}=$ $\mathcal{M}_{1} \times \cdots \times \mathcal{M}_{1}$. The tangent space is spanned $\mathcal{M}_{1}$ is spanned by the generators of constant gauge transformations. If we write
$$\alpha=-A \mathrm{~d} z=\sum \frac{A_{i} \mathrm{~d} z}{z-a_{i}}, \quad \alpha_{i}=-\frac{A_{i} \mathrm{~d} z_{i}}{z_{i}},$$
then we can identify $\mathcal{M}$ (as a symplectic manifold) with the product of the coadjoint orbits of the $A_{i}$ in $\mathfrak{g}^{*}$. In this case,
$$A_{i-}=\frac{A_{i}}{z-a_{i}} ;$$
there are no $h_{q_{i}} \mathrm{~s}$, while
$$h_{i}=\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{i} A_{j}\right)}{a_{i}-a_{j}} .$$

\section*{Appendix}

\section*{Singularities of systems of ODEs}

Let $A$ be a meromorphic map from some neighbourhood $D$ of the origin in $\mathbb{C}$ into $\mathfrak{g}$ (the Lie algebra $\operatorname{sl}(n, \mathbb{C}))$, with a pole of order $r+1$ at $z=0$. Then the system
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} y}{\mathrm{~d} z}=A y,$$
has a singularity of Poincaré rank $r$ at the origin. It is regular or Fuchsian if $r=0$, and irregular if $r>0$. In this paper, $y$ will always be a matrix fundamental solution-that is, it will take values in $G$.

\section*{Gauge and point transformations}

When we regard the system as a connection on a vector bundle, we must allow gauge transformations (changes of trivialization) of the form
$$A \mapsto g^{-1} A g-g^{1} \frac{\mathrm{~d} g}{\mathrm{~d} z}, \quad \nabla \mapsto g^{-1} \circ \nabla \circ g, \quad y \mapsto g y,$$
where $g: X \rightarrow G$ is holomorphic. When $g$ is constant, $A$ transforms by conjugation. We also admit transformations of the coordinate $z \mapsto \hat{z}$, under which
$$A \mapsto \hat{A}=A \frac{\mathrm{~d} z}{\mathrm{~d} \hat{z}} .$$
If this is to fix the singularity at the origin, then we must take $\hat{z}(0)=0$.

\section*{Generic irregular singularities}

An irregular singularity of rank $r$ is generic if the eigenvalues of
$$A_{-r-1}=\left.z^{r+1} A\right|_{z=0}$$
are distinct. In this case, we can assume, without loss of generality, that the eigenvalues of $z^{r+1} A$ are distinct throughout the neighbourhood. If we choose an ordering for the eigenvalues, then we can find a holomorphic map $g: D \rightarrow G$ such that $z^{r+1} g(z) A(z) g^{-1}(z)$ is holomorphic and diagonal, with the eigenvalues as diagonal entries.

It follows that we can find a holomorphic map $D \rightarrow G$ and a diagonal polynomial $p(z)$ of degree $r$ such that
$$A-g(z)^{-1} \frac{p(z)}{z^{r+1}} g(z)$$
is holomorphic at the origin. Therefore we have the normal form:
$$A \sim \mathrm{~d}\left(\frac{t}{z^{r}}\right)+\frac{m \mathrm{~d} z}{z}+R(z)$$
where ' $\sim$ ' denotes gauge equivalence, $t$ is a diagonal polynomial of degree $r-1$, $m$ is a constant diagonal matrix called the exponent of formal monodromy, and the remainder $R$ is holomorphic at $z=0$. Given the local coordinate $z$ and the ordering of the eigenvalues, $t$ and $m$ are uniquely determined by $A$, independently of the choice of gauge. We call them the singularity data at 0. If the ordering is changed, then the diagonal entries are permuted; if the coordinate is changed, then $m$ is unchanged, while $t \mapsto t^{\prime}$, where $t^{\prime}$ is obtained from $t$ by making the coordinate transformation and truncating the Taylor series in $z$.

If one looks for a gauge transformation $g(z)=g_{0}+g_{1} z+\cdots$ such that (18) holds with $R=0$, then the coefficients $g_{i}$ can be determined uniquely once a choice has been made for $g_{0}$ to diagonalize $A_{-r-1}$. For each such choice, one can therefore find a unique formal solution
$$y_{\mathrm{f}}=g_{\mathrm{f}}(z) \exp \left(T z^{-r}+m \log z\right) .$$
In general, the formal series does not converge. However, by truncating, one can make $R$ vanish to arbitrarily high order at $z=0$.
