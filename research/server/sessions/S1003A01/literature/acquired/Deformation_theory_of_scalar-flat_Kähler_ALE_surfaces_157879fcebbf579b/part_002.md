Define the operator $\mathfrak{N}$ as
$$\begin{aligned}
& C_{-2+\epsilon}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X) \times \mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \operatorname{End}_{a}(T X)\right) \times \mathbb{W} \rightarrow C_{-4+\epsilon}^{k-1, \alpha}(T X) \times \mathbb{R}^{m} \\
& (Y, \operatorname{Re}(t), Z) \mapsto \\
& \left\{\nabla_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}}^{*} E_{\Phi_{Y *} J_{0}}^{-1}\left(\Upsilon_{Z}^{*} J_{0}(\phi(t))\right), \int\left\langle E_{\Phi_{Y *} J_{0}}^{-1}\left(\Upsilon_{Z}^{*} J_{0}(\phi(t))\right), \Phi_{Y *} v_{1}\right\rangle_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}} d V_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}}\right. \\
& \left.\quad \cdots, \int\left\langle E_{\Phi_{Y *} J_{0}}^{-1}\left(\Upsilon_{Z}^{*} J_{0}(\phi(t))\right), \Phi_{Y *} v_{m}\right\rangle_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}} d V_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}}\right\}
\end{aligned}$$
where $0<\epsilon \ll 1, \nabla_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}}^{*}$ is the divergence operator associated to the metric $\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}, m=$ $\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{V}),\left\{v_{1}, \ldots, v_{m}\right\}$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{V}$. Note that the method of (5.15) is used here again to make the operator smooth. Also note that the map
$$(Y, \operatorname{Re}(t), Z) \mapsto \nabla_{\Phi_{Y *} g_{0}}^{*} E_{\Phi_{Y *} J_{0}}^{-1}\left(\Upsilon_{Z}^{*} J_{0}(\phi(t))\right)$$
is defined by choosing the first parameter of $\mathfrak{P}$ to be $\left(\Phi_{Y}\right)_{*} g_{0}-g_{0}$, choosing the second parameter to be $\left(\Phi_{Y}\right)_{*} J_{0}-J_{0}$, choosing the third parameter to be $\phi_{Z}(t)$ (defined in (6.11)), and letting $\delta^{\prime}=-3+\epsilon$. Since $\mathfrak{P}$ is smooth by Lemma 5.2, and since the mappings $Y \mapsto$ $\left(\Phi_{Y}\right)_{*} g_{0}-g_{0}$, and $Y \mapsto\left(\Phi_{Y}\right)_{*} J_{0}-J_{0}$ are smooth by Lemma 5.1, and since $\Upsilon^{*}$ is smooth by Lemma 6.3, we have that (6.26) is a smooth mapping. By applying the same argument to the inner products in $\mathfrak{N}$, we can show that $\mathfrak{N}$ is smooth in an open neighborhood of ( $0,0,0$ ).

At (0, 0, 0), the linearization of $\mathfrak{N}$ restricted to the tangent spaces of the first and third variables is:
$$\begin{aligned}
& D \mathfrak{N}_{0}: C_{-2+\epsilon}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X) \times \mathbb{W} \rightarrow C_{-4+\epsilon}^{k-1, \alpha}(T X) \times \mathbb{R}^{m} \\
&(Y, Z) \mapsto\left\{\nabla_{g_{0}}^{*} \mathfrak{L}_{Y} J_{0}=\square_{g_{0}} Y, \int\left\langle\frac{-1}{2} J_{0} \mathfrak{L}_{Z} J_{0}-\frac{1}{2} J_{0} \mathfrak{L}_{Y} J_{0}, v_{1}\right\rangle_{g_{0}} d V_{g_{0}}\right. \\
& \quad\left.\quad, \int\left\langle\frac{-1}{2} J_{0} \mathfrak{L}_{Z} J_{0}-\frac{1}{2} J_{0} \mathfrak{L}_{Y} J_{0}, v_{m}\right\rangle_{g_{0}} d V_{g_{0}}\right\} .
\end{aligned}$$
Restricting the domain and image, consider $D \mathfrak{N}_{0}$ mapping from $C_{-2+\epsilon}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X)$ to $C_{-4+\epsilon}^{k-1, \alpha}(T X)$. The adjoint of this restricted mapping, which we again denote by $D \mathfrak{N}_{0}$, is:
$$\begin{aligned}
\left(D \mathfrak{N}_{0}\right)^{*}: C_{-\epsilon}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X) & \rightarrow C_{-2-\epsilon}^{k-1, \alpha}(T X) \\
\eta & \mapsto\left(\square_{g_{0}} \eta\right)
\end{aligned}$$
The kernel of $\left(D \mathfrak{N}_{0}\right)^{*}$ consists of those $\eta \in C_{-\epsilon}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X)$ such that $\left(\square_{g_{0}} \eta\right)=0$, which implies that $\eta$ is the real part of a holomorphic vector field. From Proposition 3.3, $\eta=0$. Since the cokernel of $D \mathfrak{N}_{0}$ is trivial, $D \mathfrak{N}_{0}$ is surjective on the restricted domain and range.

It follows that the full mapping $D \mathfrak{N}_{0}$ is surjective, since $\mathbb{V}$ is generated by $\frac{-1}{2} J_{0} \mathfrak{L}_{Z} J_{0}$ for $Z \in \mathbb{W}$. Clearly then, in a small neighborhood of $(0,0,0), D \mathfrak{N}$ is surjective. Since the mapping $\mathfrak{N}$ is smooth, Condition (4.1) is satisfied, so the existence of diffeomorphism $\Phi$ then follows from Lemma 4.1. Finally, the estimate (6.24) follows from estimates above. \(\square\)

\section*{7. Stability of Kähler metrics}

In this section, we want to generalize Kodaira-Spencer's stability theory of Kähler metrics to ALE surfaces to the family of complex structures found above [KS60]. Specifically, we want to find a smooth family of Kähler forms on $\left(X, J_{t}\right)$ for $t$ small, where $J_{t}$ for $t \in B_{\epsilon_{1}} \subset$ $\mathcal{H}_{\text {ess }}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$ is the family of complex structures constructed in Section 4. In a slight abuse of notation, the notation $X_{t}$ will stand for $\left(X, J_{t}\right)$. All the norms below in this section are based on the central metric $g_{0}$.

Lemma 7.1. The integer-valued function $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{p, q}\right)\right)$ is upper semicontinuous at $t=$ 0 , for any $\tau \in(-2,0)$.

Proof. The Hodge Laplacian $\Delta_{d}=d d^{*}+d^{*} d$ is a Fredholm operator on $C_{\tau}^{k, \alpha}(-2<\tau<$ $0, k \geq 2$ ). The following is proved in [Bar86]: If $P$ is a pseudo-differential operator such that $\left\|P-\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{d}\right\|_{o p}<\epsilon$ for some $\epsilon$ sufficiently small, then $\operatorname{ker}(P) \leq \operatorname{ker}\left(\Delta_{d}\right)$, where $\|\cdot\|_{o p}$ is the norm of operators from $C_{\tau}^{k, \alpha}$ to $C_{\tau-2}^{k-2, \alpha}$, which is defined as:
$$\|P\|_{o p}=\sup \left\{\|P(u)\|_{C_{\tau-2}^{k-2, \alpha}}:\|u\|_{C_{\tau}^{k, \alpha}}=1\right\} .$$
We may apply this to the operator $P=\square_{t}=\bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} \bar{\partial}_{t}+\bar{\partial}_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t}^{*}$, since we have $\left\|\square_{t}-\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{d}\right\|_{o p}<\epsilon$ when $t \in \Delta_{\gamma}^{d_{1}}$ is small enough (when $t=0, \square_{0}=\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{d}$ by the Kähler identities). Then
$$\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{p, q}\right)\right) \leq \operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{p, q}\right)\right) .$$ \(\square\)

Let $e_{t}^{,^{,,}}$denote the dimension of the space of $\square_{t}$-harmonic forms with decay rate of $\tau$. Note that by a similar argument as in Proposition 3.5, any such harmonic form must decay like $O\left(r^{-3}\right)$ as $r \rightarrow \infty$. So by [Joy00, Sections 8.4 and 8.9], and Proposition 3.6, we have
$$b_{0}^{2,0}=b_{0}^{0,2}=b_{0}^{1,0}=b_{0}^{0,1}=0 .$$
From semi-continuity, it follows that
$$e_{t}^{2,0}=e_{t}^{0,2}=e_{t}^{1,0}=e_{t}^{0,1}=0 .$$
The proof of the following theorem is inspired by [BR15].
Theorem 7.2. By choosing $\epsilon_{1}$ small enough, for the family of deformation $\left(X, J_{t}\right), t \in B_{\epsilon_{1}}$, there exists a smooth family of Kähler forms $\omega_{t}$ on $X_{t}$, and
$$\left\|\omega_{t}-\omega_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C \cdot\|t\|_{C_{-3}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C \cdot \epsilon_{1}$$
for some constant $C$, and some $k \geq 3, \delta \in(-2,-1)$.
Furthermore, letting $\mathfrak{G}$ denote the group of holomorphic isometries of ( $X, g_{0}, J_{0}$ ), the mapping
$$\begin{aligned}
\aleph: B_{\epsilon_{1}} & \rightarrow \Lambda^{2}\left(T^{*} X\right) \\
t & \mapsto \omega_{t}
\end{aligned}$$
can be chosen equivariantly with respect to the action of $\mathfrak{G}$. That is, $\aleph\left(\iota^{*} t\right)=\iota^{*} \aleph(t)$, for all $\iota \in \mathfrak{G}$.

Proof. Let $g_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\left(g+g \circ J_{t}\right)$ be the Hermitian metric on $X_{t}$, where $g$ is the Riemannian metric of the central fiber and $J_{t}$ is the complex structure of $X_{t}$. Let $\square_{t}=\bar{\partial}_{t}{ }^{*} \bar{\partial}_{t}+\bar{\partial}_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t}{ }^{*}$ be the $\bar{\partial}_{t}$-Laplacian defined with respect to $g_{t}$. Let $\omega_{t}^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2} g_{t}\left(J_{t},\right)$ be the corresponding (1, 1)-form. We want to perturb $\omega_{t}^{\prime}$ to acquire a Kähler form $\omega_{t}$. We have $\omega_{t}^{\prime}=\omega_{t}^{h}+\phi_{t}$, where $\omega_{t}^{h}$ is the $\square_{t}$-harmonic part of $\omega_{t}^{\prime}$, which is asymptotic to $d z_{t} \wedge d \bar{z}_{t}$ at the rate of $-\delta$, with $\left\|\phi_{t}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\square_{t} \omega_{t}^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\delta-2}^{k-2, \alpha}}$. We next show that this claimed decomposition is valid. Consider the following Fredholm operator
$$\square_{t}: C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right) \rightarrow C_{\delta-2}^{k-2, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right) .$$
The kernel ker $\square_{t}^{*} \subset C_{-2-\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$ is of finite dimension. For any $\sigma \in \operatorname{ker} \square_{t}^{*}, \sigma$ admits an expansion
$$\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{2} \frac{a_{k}}{r^{2}} d z_{t} \wedge d \bar{z}_{t}+O\left(r^{-3}\right)$$
as $r \rightarrow \infty$, where $a_{k}$ are constants. Since
$$\begin{aligned}
0=\int_{X}\left\langle\sigma, \square_{t} \sigma\right\rangle_{g_{t}} d V_{g_{t}}=\lim _{R \rightarrow \infty} & \left\{\int_{B(R)}\left(\langle\bar{\partial} \sigma, \bar{\partial} \sigma\rangle_{g_{t}}+\left\langle\bar{\partial}^{*} \sigma, \bar{\partial}^{*} \sigma\right\rangle_{g_{t}}\right) d V_{g_{t}}\right. \\
& \left.\left.\left.+\int_{S(R)}(\langle\sigma, \bar{\partial} r\lrcorner \bar{\partial} \sigma\rangle_{g_{t}}+\left\langle\bar{\partial}^{*} \sigma, \bar{\partial} r\right\lrcorner \sigma\right\rangle_{g_{t}}\right) d A_{g_{t}}\right\} \\
= & \int_{X}\left(\langle\bar{\partial} \sigma, \bar{\partial} \sigma\rangle_{g_{t}}+\left\langle\bar{\partial}^{*} \sigma, \bar{\partial}^{*} \sigma\right\rangle_{g_{t}}\right) d V_{g_{t}}
\end{aligned}$$

This shows that $\bar{\partial} \sigma=0, \bar{\partial}^{*} \sigma=0$. Since also $\bar{\partial}_{t}-\bar{\partial}_{E u c}=O\left(r^{-3}\right)$, we have $\bar{\partial}_{E u c} \sigma=O\left(r^{-5}\right)$. Then $a_{k}=0$ and $\sigma=O\left(r^{-3}\right)$. This implies that $\square_{t} \omega_{t}^{\prime} \in\left(\operatorname{ker} \square_{t}^{*}\right)^{\perp} \cap C_{\delta-2}^{k-2, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$, which is the $L^{2}$-complement of $\operatorname{ker} \square_{t}^{*}$ in $C_{\delta-2}^{k-2, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$. Then there exists a $\phi_{t} \in C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$ such that $\square_{t} \phi_{t}=\square_{t} \omega_{t}^{\prime}$ and $\left\|\phi_{t}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\square_{t} \omega_{t}^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\delta-2}^{k-2, \alpha}}$. Then we can let $\omega_{t}^{h}=\omega_{t}^{\prime}-\phi_{t}$ which is $\square_{t}$-harmonic.
Proposition 7.3. If $\square_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=0$, then $\bar{\partial}_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=0$.
Proof. $\bar{\partial}_{t} \square_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=\square_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=0$, then $\bar{\partial}_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}$ is $\square_{t}$-harmonic, with decay rate of $-(\delta-1)$. However, $e_{t}^{2,1}=e_{t}^{0,1}=0$ by the upper semi-continuity and since the conjugate Hodge-star operator maps harmonic forms to harmonic forms. Then $\bar{\partial}_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=0$. \(\square\)

We want to find $\omega_{t}$ such that $d \omega_{t}=0$; for this we need a lemma.
Lemma 7.4. We have $\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=\bar{\partial}_{t} a_{t}$, where $a_{t} \in C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,0}\right), k \geq 3$. Furthermore,
$$\left\|a_{t}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}\right\|_{C_{\delta-1}^{k-1, \alpha}} \leq C^{\prime}\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}$$
Proof. First note that
$$\bar{\partial}_{t} \partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=-\partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=0 .$$
Then
$$\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h} \in C_{\delta-1}^{k-1, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right)=\mathcal{H}_{\delta-1}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right) \oplus \square_{t} C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right) .$$
(This decomposition follows since $\square_{t}$ is Fredholm on $C_{\delta+1}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right)$.) Then
$$\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=h_{t}+\left(\bar{\partial}_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t}^{*}+\bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} \bar{\partial}_{t}\right) f_{t}$$
with $f_{t} \in C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right)$, where $h_{t}$ is the harmonic part. Since $\bar{\partial}_{t} \partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{H}=0$, it follows that $\square_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} f_{t}=0$. From injectivity of $\square_{t}$ on $C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}$, we have $\bar{\partial}_{t} f_{t}=0$. Then
$$\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=h_{t}+\bar{\partial}_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} f_{t}=h_{t}+\bar{\partial}_{t} a_{t},$$
where $a_{t}=\bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} f_{t} \in C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,0}\right)$. Since $e_{t}^{2,1}=0$, then $h_{t}=0$, and $\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}=\bar{\partial}_{t} a_{t}$. Since $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta+1}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right)\right)=0$,
$$\square_{t}: C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right) \rightarrow C_{\delta-1}^{k-1, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,1}\right)$$
is an isomorphism. Then $\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}\right\|_{C_{\delta-1}^{k-1, \alpha}}$ implies that
$$\|a\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}\right\|_{C_{\delta-1}^{k-1, \alpha}} \leq C^{\prime}\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}},$$ \(\square\)

Now we have $a_{t} \in C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,0}\right)$ and $\bar{\partial}_{t} \overline{a_{t}}=0$. Let $\delta+2$ not be a indicial root of $\square_{t}$, then
$$\square_{t}: C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right) \rightarrow C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right)$$
is Fredholm. Since $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{-4-\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right)\right)=0$, it follows that $\square_{t}$ is surjective. We can therefore choose $f_{t} \in C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right)$ such that $\overline{a_{t}}=\square_{t} f_{t}=\bar{\partial}_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} f_{t}$ with
$$\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\overline{a_{t}}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C^{\prime}\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} .$$
Let $\bar{\beta}_{t}=\bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} f_{t}$, and let $\omega_{t}=\omega_{t}^{h}+\bar{\partial}_{t} \beta_{t}$. Then $d \omega_{t}=\partial_{t} \omega_{t}^{h}-\bar{\partial}_{t} a_{t}=0$. Choose $\epsilon_{1}$ to be small enough, then $\omega_{t}$ is a closed positive (1,1)-form. \(\square\)

\section*{8. Deformations of scalar-flat Kähler metrics}

Notice that the previous sections did not use the scalar-flat assumption; this section is where we start using it.
8.1. The linearized operator. In previous sections, we have shown that there exists a family of decaying deformation of complex structure with dimension equal to $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\text {ess }}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes\right.\right.$ $\Theta)$ ), and there exists a smooth family of Kähler forms along the deformation. In this section, we will show that near an initial scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric $\omega_{0}$, there is a smooth family of scalar-flat Kähler ALE metrics under these deformations. The arguments in this section are inspired by [LS93, LS94].

Denote $S\left(\omega_{0}+\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} f\right)$ as the scalar curvature of $X$ with metric $\omega_{0}+\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} f$. We now consider $S$ as mapping between weighted Hölder spaces, with some $0<\epsilon \ll 1$
$$\begin{aligned}
S: C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}(X) & \rightarrow C_{\epsilon-4}^{k-4, \alpha}(X) \\
f & \mapsto S\left(\omega_{0}+\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} f\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
Its linearization and adjoint are maps between:
$$\begin{aligned}
& L: C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}(X) \rightarrow C_{\epsilon-4}^{k-4, \alpha}(X) \\
& L^{*}: C_{-\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}(X) \rightarrow C_{-4-\epsilon}^{k-4, \alpha}(X) .
\end{aligned}$$
By direct calculation,
$$L(f):=D S\left(\omega_{0}+\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} f\right)=-\left(\Delta^{2} f+R_{i, \bar{j}} \nabla^{i} \nabla^{\bar{j}} f\right) .$$
Define $\bar{\partial}^{\#} f=g_{0}^{i, \bar{j}} \bar{\partial}_{j} f$. Also by direct calculation,
$$\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right)^{*}\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right) f=\Delta^{2} f+R_{i, \bar{j}} \nabla^{i} \nabla^{\bar{j}} f+\nabla_{k} S \nabla^{k} f .$$
Clearly then, when $\omega_{0}$ is scalar-flat, $L(f)=-\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right)^{*}\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right)(f)$, see [LS94].
The following lemma shows that the linearized mapping is surjective. This can also be interpreted as the nondegeneracy of the Futaki invariant, see [Fut88] and [LS94, Section 3.2].

Lemma 8.1. For $0<\epsilon \ll 1, k \geq 4, L$ is surjective.
Proof. Obviously, $L$ is an elliptic operator, and the indicial roots of $L$ are the integers. Consequently, by standard weighted space theory, $L$ is Fredholm since $\delta$ is non-integral [LM85].

Let $f \in \operatorname{ker}\left(L^{*}\right)$, then $f=O\left(r^{-\epsilon}\right)$ and $\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right)^{*}\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}{ }^{\#}\right) f=0$. Since
$$f\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right)^{*}\left(\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{\#}\right) f=o\left(r^{-4}\right),$$
integrating by parts, It follows that $\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}{ }^{\#} f=0$, so $\bar{\partial}^{\#} f$ is a holomorphic vector field on $X$ which has a decay rate of $-(\epsilon-1)$, and Proposition 3.3 then implies that $\bar{\partial}^{\#} f=0$. Since $f$ is a real-valued function, $f$ must be constant; but since $f=O\left(r^{-\epsilon}\right)$, we have $f \equiv 0$. We have shown that $\operatorname{ker}\left(L^{*}\right)=\{0\}$, so $L$ is surjective. \(\square\) \(\square\)

Proposition 8.2. For $0<\epsilon \ll 1$ and $k \geq 4, \operatorname{ker}(L)$ is spanned by the constants.
Proof. The operator $L$ admits an expansion near infinity of the form $L=\Delta_{\text {Euc }}^{2}+Q$, where $Q$ represents lower order terms. Let
$$N(L, \tau)=\operatorname{Index}\left(L: C_{\tau}^{k, \alpha}(X) \rightarrow C_{\tau-4}^{k-4, \alpha}(X)\right) .$$

Then, by the relative index theorem of Lockhart-McOwen,
$$N(L, \epsilon)-N(L,-\epsilon)=N\left(\Delta_{E u c}^{2}, \epsilon\right)-N\left(\Delta_{E u c}^{2},-\epsilon\right)=2 .$$
see [LM85]. The above argument shows that $\operatorname{Ker}(L) \cap C_{-\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}(X)=\{0\}$, which implies that
$$\operatorname{ker}(L) \cap C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}(X)=\{\text { constants }\} .$$ \(\square\)
8.2. Construction of $\mathfrak{F}$. Now we start the construction of the class $\mathfrak{F}$. First, we show a weighted version $\partial \bar{\partial}$-lemma on ALE surfaces. This is similar to Joy00, Theorem 8.4.4], but with slightly different assumptions.

Lemma 8.3. Consider the Kähler ALE surface $\left(X, J_{0}, g_{0}\right)$. Let $\zeta \in C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$ be a real $(1,1)$ form, for some $k \geq 2$ and $\delta \in(-2,-1)$, satisfying $d \zeta=0$. Then there exists $\theta \in C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}(X)$ such that $\zeta=\zeta_{h}+\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} \theta$, and $\|\theta\|_{C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}} \leq C \cdot\left\|\zeta-\zeta_{h}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}$, where $\zeta_{h} \in$ $\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$.

Proof. Since $\zeta$ is closed, can be decomposed as $\zeta=\zeta_{h}+\partial \eta+\bar{\partial} \xi$, where $\zeta_{h} \in \mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$, $\eta \in C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1}\right)$, and $\xi \in C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{1,0}\right)$. Since $\zeta$ is a real form, we can assume that $\eta=\bar{\xi}$. Consider the operator □ and its Fredholm adjoint $\square^{*}$ :
$$\begin{aligned}
\square: C_{\delta+3}^{k+3, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1}\right) & \rightarrow C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1}\right) \\
\square^{*}: C_{-3-\delta}^{k+3, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1}\right) & \rightarrow C_{-\delta-5}^{k+1, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1}\right)
\end{aligned}$$
This implies that □ has finite-dimensional cokernel. Then we have
$$\eta=\eta_{h}+\square \gamma=\eta_{h}+\bar{\partial} \bar{\partial}^{*} \gamma+\bar{\partial}^{*} \bar{\partial} \gamma,$$
where $\gamma \in C_{\delta+3}^{k+3, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1}\right)$ and $\eta_{h}$ is the □-harmonic part. Without loss of generality, assume $\eta_{h}=0$. Since $g_{0}$ is Kähler, $\partial \bar{\partial}^{*}(\bar{\partial} \gamma)=-\bar{\partial} \partial^{*}(\bar{\partial} \gamma)=0$, so we can assume that $\bar{\partial} \gamma=0$. Then $\zeta=\zeta_{h}+\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} \theta^{\prime}$, where $\theta^{\prime}=2 \operatorname{Im}\left(\bar{\partial}^{*} \gamma\right) \in C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}(X)$.

Now consider the Fredholm operator
$$F=\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial}: C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}(X) \rightarrow C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$$
Let $\left(\operatorname{ker} F^{*}\right)^{\perp}$ be the $L^{2}$-complement of $\operatorname{ker} F^{*}$ in $C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}\left(X, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$. The argument above shows that $\operatorname{ker} F^{*}=\operatorname{ker} \square^{*}$. Then $\zeta-\zeta_{h} \in\left(\operatorname{ker} F^{*}\right)^{\perp}$. By the same argument as in the proof of Theorem 7.2, we can show that there exists a $\theta \in C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}(X)$, such that $\sqrt{-1} \partial \bar{\partial} \theta=\zeta-\zeta_{h}$, and $\|\theta\|_{C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}} \leq C\left\|\zeta-\zeta_{h}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}$ for some constant $C$. \(\square\)

Theorem 8.4. Let $\left(X, J_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ be a scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric on a surface $X$. Then there is family $\mathfrak{F}$ of scalar-flat Kähler metrics near $g_{0}$, parametrized by $B$, that is, there is a differentiable mapping
$$F: B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}(X),$$
into the space of smooth Riemannian metrics $\mathcal{M}(X)$ with $\mathfrak{F}=F\left(B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}\right)$. Furthermore, letting $\mathfrak{G}$ denote the group of holomorphic isometries of $\left(X, J_{0}, g_{0}\right)$, the mapping $F$ is equivariant with respect to the action of $\mathfrak{G}$. That is, for $\iota \in \mathfrak{G}$, and $(t, \rho) \in B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$, we have $F\left(\iota^{*} t, \iota^{*} \rho\right)=\iota^{*} F(t, \rho)$.

Proof. Recall that
$$B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1}=B_{\epsilon_{1}}(0) \subset \mathcal{H}_{e s s}\left(X_{0}, \operatorname{End}_{a}\left(T X_{0}\right)\right) ; B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}=B_{\epsilon_{2}}(0) \subset \mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right) \simeq H^{1,1}\left(X_{0}\right) .$$
Let $t$ denote the parameter of $B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1}$. By Section 7, $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{p, q}\right)\right)$ (using the metric determined by $\omega_{t}$ ) is upper semicontinuous, and
$$\begin{aligned}
& \operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{1,1}\left(X_{0}\right)\right) \\
& \operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{2,0}\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right)\right)=0 .
\end{aligned}$$
So for $\epsilon_{1}$ sufficiently small,
$$\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,0}\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right)\right)=0,$$
and also
$$\begin{gathered}
\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{2,0}\right)\right) \geq \operatorname{dim}\left(H^{2,0}\left(X_{t}\right)\right) \\
\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{0,2}\right)\right) \geq \operatorname{dim}\left(H^{0,2}\left(X_{t}\right)\right) .
\end{gathered}$$
This implies that $\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{2}\left(X_{t}\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{1,1}\left(X_{t}\right)\right)$ which is a topological invariant. Then
$$\begin{array}{r}
\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{2}\left(X_{t}\right)\right) \leq \operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)\right) \leq \operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)\right)= \\
=\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{2}\left(X_{0}\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{2}\left(X_{t}\right)\right) .
\end{array}$$
This implies that $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{2}\left(X_{t}\right)\right)$ is constant, so choose a smooth family of isomorphisms $\psi_{t}$ which map $\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$ to $\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$ for $t$ sufficiently small. Note that $\mathfrak{G}$ acts on $\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$, and from Theorem 7.2, $\mathfrak{G}$ also acts on $\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{t}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$. Clearly we can choose $\psi_{t}$ to be equivariant with respect to these actions.

It has been shown in Section 7 that there is a smooth family of Kähler forms $\omega_{t}$. Let $\rho \in B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$, define
$$\omega(t, \rho)=\omega_{t}+\psi_{t}(\rho)$$
Now consider the mapping
$$\mathcal{S}: B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2} \times C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}(X) \rightarrow C_{\epsilon-4}^{k-4, \alpha}(X),$$
defined by
$$\mathcal{S}:(t, \rho, f) \mapsto S\left(\omega(t, \rho)+\sqrt{-1} \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} f\right) .$$
We endow the domain with the product norm, where $B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1}$ and $B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$ have the $L^{2}$-norm. By direct calculation, the linearization of $\mathcal{S}$ at 0 is:
$$D \mathcal{S}=\left(*, \quad-\text { Ric }_{h}, \quad L\right)$$
where $R i c_{h}$ is the harmonic part of Ricci form. Since $L$ is surjective as shown in Lemma 8.3, $D \mathcal{S}$ is surjective by the lemma above. Next, we recall the expansion of the curvature tensor
$$R m\left(g_{0}+h\right)=R m\left(g_{0}\right)+\left(g_{0}+h\right)^{-1} * \nabla^{2} h+\left(g_{0}+h\right)^{-2} * \nabla h * \nabla h,$$
where $*$ denotes a various tensor contractions, and $h$ is a symmetric tensor such that $g_{0}+h$ is a Riemannian metric [GV16, Formula 3.40]. In our case, $h$ can be written as
$$h(,)=\left(\omega(t, \rho)+\sqrt{-1} \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} f\right)\left(-J_{t},\right)-\omega(0,0)\left(-J_{0},\right) .$$

Next, by (7.5), we have the estimates
$$\begin{aligned}
|h| & \leq C\left(|t|+|\rho|+\left|\nabla^{2} f\right|\right) \leq C\left(r^{\delta}\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+r^{\delta}\|\rho\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+r^{-2+\epsilon}\|f\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right) \\
|\nabla h| & \leq C\left(|\nabla t|+|\nabla \rho|+\left|\nabla^{3} f\right|\right) \leq C\left(r^{-1+\delta}\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+r^{-1+\delta}\|\rho\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+r^{-3+\epsilon}\|f\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right) \\
\left|\nabla^{2} h\right| & \leq C\left(\left|\nabla^{2} t\right|+\left|\nabla^{2} \rho\right|+\left|\nabla^{4} f\right|\right) \leq C\left(r^{-2+\delta}\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+r^{-2+\delta}\|\rho\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+r^{-4+\epsilon}\|f\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
Then by (8.23), we have
$$\begin{aligned}
S\left(g_{0}+h\right)-D S \cdot h=C_{0} & \left(g_{0}, h\right) * R m\left(g_{0}\right) * h * h \\
& \quad+C_{1}\left(g_{0}, h\right) * h * \nabla^{2} h+C_{2}\left(g_{0}, h\right) * \nabla h * \nabla h
\end{aligned}$$
where $C_{0}\left(g_{0}, h\right), C_{1}\left(g_{0}, h\right)$, and $C_{2}\left(g_{0}, h\right)$ are bounded functions, when $h$ is sufficiently small. Without loss of generality, we can assume that $-2+\epsilon<\delta$. Then
$$\begin{aligned}
\left|S\left(g_{0}+h\right)-D S \cdot h\right| & \leq C\left\{r^{-2}|h|^{2}+|h| \cdot\left|\nabla^{2} h\right|+|\nabla h| \cdot|\nabla h|\right\} \\
& \leq C \cdot r^{-2+2 \delta} \cdot\left(\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\|\rho\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\|f\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right)^{2}
\end{aligned}$$
which implies that
$$\left\|S\left(g_{0}+h\right)-D S \cdot h\right\|_{C_{-4+\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C\left(\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\|\rho\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\|f\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right)^{2} .$$
Furthermore,
$$\begin{aligned}
\| S\left(g_{0}+h\right) & -S\left(g_{0}+h^{\prime}\right)-D S \cdot\left(h-h^{\prime}\right) \|_{C_{-4+\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}} \leq \\
C \cdot & \left(\left\|t-t^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\left\|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\left\|f-f^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right) \\
\cdot & \left(\|t\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\|\rho\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\|f\|_{C_{1}^{k, \alpha}}+\left\|t^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\left\|\rho^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C_{\epsilon}^{k, \alpha}}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
Since $B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1}$ and $B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$ are finite-dimensional, we can replace the corresponding norms by the $L^{2}$-norm. This shows that $\mathcal{S}$ satisfies the condition (4.1). Then by Lemma 4.1, the zero set of $\mathcal{S}$ is in one-to-one correspondence with the kernel of $\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(0)$. Since any solution of the nonlinear equation can be written as a kernel element plus a unique element in the image of a bounded right inverse for the linearized operator, the zero set of $\mathcal{S}$ can also be written as a graph over $B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$. That is, for any $(t, \rho) \in B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$, there exists a unique $f(t, \rho)$, up to constants, of
$$\mathcal{S}(t, \rho, f(t, \rho))=0,$$
so a family of scalar-flat Kähler metrics over $B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$ can be constructed as
$$F:(t, \rho) \mapsto g(t, \rho)=\left(\omega(t, \rho)+\sqrt{-1} \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} f(t, \rho)\right)\left(-J_{t} \cdot, \cdot\right),$$
where $g(0,0)=g_{0}$. It is a straightforward consequence of the implicit function theorem that the mapping $F$ is differentiable. Then the image of $F$ gives us the family of scalar-flat Kähler metrics $\mathfrak{F}$, and the construction is clearly equivariant with respect to the action of $\mathfrak{G}$.

To finish the proof, we show that metrics in $\mathfrak{F}$ are smooth. By the result of [TV05a], any Kähler constant scalar curvature metric satisfies an equation of the form
$$\Delta R i c=R m * R i c,$$
where the right hand side denotes quadratic curvature contractions involving the full curvature tensor Rm and the Ricci tensor. By a Moser iteration method and regularity bootstrap argument in harmonic coordinates, it follows that $g$ is smooth, see [TV05b, Theorem 6.4]. \(\square\)

\section*{9. Versality and uniqueness of the moduli space}

In this section, all norms are defined based on the initial Kähler metric $g_{0}$. We let $\left(g_{1}, J_{1}\right)$ be any scalar-flat Kähler metric satisfying $\left\|g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}<\epsilon_{3}$, (for some $k \geq 4, \delta \in(-2,-1)$, and $\epsilon_{3}$ will be determined later). We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 9.1. For $\epsilon_{3}$ sufficiently small, if $\left\|g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}<\epsilon_{3}$, then there exists a diffeomorphism $\Phi_{0}: X \rightarrow X$ and constants $C_{1}, C_{2}$ depending upon $g_{0}, J_{0}$ so that
$$\begin{aligned}
\left\|\Phi_{0}^{*} g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} & <C_{1} \epsilon_{3} \\
\left\|\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}-J_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} & <C_{2} \epsilon_{3} .
\end{aligned}$$
Proof. Let $\nabla_{0}$ denote the covariant derivative of $g_{0}$, and $\Gamma_{0}, \Gamma_{1}$ denote the Christoffel symbols of $g_{0}, g_{1}$, respectively. Then $\left\|\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta-1}^{k-1, \alpha}}<C \epsilon_{3}$, so in particular $\left|\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{0}\right|<\frac{C \epsilon_{3}}{(1+r)^{-\delta+1}}$ as $r \rightarrow \infty$.

Since $\left(g_{i}, J_{i}\right)$ is Kähler, $\nabla_{i} J_{i}=0$ for $i=0,1$. We now estimate $\left|J_{1}-J_{0}\right|(p)$ along any geodesic ray $\gamma$ starting at $p_{0}$. From [HL16, Lemma 1.1], both $J_{1}$ and $J_{0}$ have a finite limit along $\gamma$ as $t \rightarrow \infty$, and furthermore
$$J_{i}=\left(J_{i}\right)_{0}+O\left(r^{-2}\right),$$
where $\left(J_{i}\right)_{0}$ is a constant complex structure on $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ for $i=0,1$. We are assuming that $\left(J_{0}\right)_{0}=J_{E u c}$. Clearly, there exists a diffeomorphism $\Phi_{0}: X \rightarrow X$ so that
$$\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}=J_{E u c}+O\left(r^{-2}\right),$$
as $r \rightarrow \infty$ such that (9.1) is satisfied. This can be done, for example, by connecting a rotation defined on the complement of a large ball $X \backslash B\left(p_{0}, 2 R\right)$ to the identity transformation on $B\left(p_{0}, R\right)$ by smooth path of rotations of each sphere on the annulus $A(R, 2 R)$. We then have
$$\begin{aligned}
\left|\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}-J_{0}\right|(p) & =\left|\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}-J_{0}\right|(p)-\lim _{t \rightarrow \infty}\left|\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}-J_{0}\right|(\gamma(t)) \\
& \leq \int_{s=r}^{\infty}\left|\nabla_{0}\left(\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}-J_{0}\right)(\gamma(s))\right| d s \\
& \leq \int_{s=r}^{\infty}\left|\nabla_{0} \Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}(\gamma(s))\right| d s \\
& <\int_{s=r}^{\infty} \frac{C \cdot \epsilon_{3}}{(1+s)^{-\delta+1}} d s \leq \frac{C^{\prime} \cdot \epsilon_{3}}{(1+r)^{-\delta}} .
\end{aligned}$$
Since this estimate is true along any ray, it follows that $\left\|\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}-J_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{0}}<C^{\prime} \epsilon_{3}$. We can estimate the higher regularities in the same way, and (9.2) follows. \(\square\)

We next prove the "versality" of our family $\mathfrak{F}$. That is, for $g_{1}$ as above, there exists a diffeomorphism $\Phi$ such that $\Phi^{*} g_{1}$ is in the class $\mathfrak{F}$.

Theorem 9.2. Let $-2<\delta<-1$ be fixed. There exists an $\epsilon_{3}>0$ such that for any scalar-flat Kähler metric $\left(g_{1}, J_{1}\right)$ satisfying $\left\|g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq \epsilon_{3}$, there exists a diffeomorphism $\Phi: X \rightarrow X$ of the form $\Phi_{0} \circ \Phi_{Y_{1}} \circ \Upsilon_{Z} \circ \Phi_{Y_{2}}$ where $\Phi_{0}$ is as in Lemma 9.1, $Y_{1}, Y_{2} \in C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X)$, and $Z \in \mathbb{W}$, such that $\Phi^{*} g_{1} \in \mathfrak{F}$. Furthermore, there exists a constant $C$ so that
$$\left\|\Phi^{*} g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C \epsilon_{3} .$$
Proof. Let $\Phi_{0}$ denote the diffeomorphism from Lemma 9.1. Then $\Phi_{0}^{*} J_{1}$ satisfies the assumptions of Theorem 6.4, so there exists a diffeomorphism $\tilde{\Phi}$ satisyfing the properties stated in that theorem. In particular, $\tilde{\Phi}$ is of the form $\tilde{\Phi}=\Phi_{Y_{1}} \circ \Upsilon_{Z} \circ \Phi_{Y_{2}}$, where $Y_{1}, Y_{2} \in C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X)$, and $Z \in \mathbb{W}$. The estimate (9.6) for $\Phi_{0} \circ \tilde{\Phi}$ is then proved as follows. For $\Phi_{Y_{1}}$, we estimate
$$\left\|\Phi_{Y_{1}}^{*} \Phi_{0}^{*} g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq\left\|\Phi_{Y_{1}}^{*} \Phi_{0}^{*} g_{1}-\Phi_{0}^{*} g_{1}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}+\left\|\Phi_{0}^{*} g_{1}-g_{0}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}} \leq C \epsilon_{3} .$$
This estimate holds since $\Phi_{0}^{*} g_{1}$ is also a scalar-flat Kähler metric which is smooth by the last observation in the proof of Theorem 8.4, so when $\epsilon_{3}$ is sufficiently small, Lemma 5.1 holds for $g_{1}$. The same argument applies to $\Phi_{Y_{2}}$. The estimate (9.6) then follows from (9.7), (6.9) and Lemma 9.1.

Let $\omega_{1}$ denote the Kähler form of $\Phi^{*} g_{1}$. It was shown in Section 8 that $\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{1,1}\left(X_{t}\right)\right)$ is locally constant, which implies $H^{1,1}\left(X_{0}\right) \simeq H^{1,1}\left(X_{t}\right)$ for any $t \in B^{1}$. Then there exists a $\rho \in \mathcal{H}_{\delta}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{1,1}\right)$ such that $\left[\omega\left(\phi_{\text {ess }}, \rho\right)\right]=\left[\omega_{1}\right] \in H^{1,1}\left(X_{t}\right)$, where $\omega\left(\phi_{\text {ess }}, \rho\right)$ is defined in (8.19). Let $g^{\prime}=F\left(\phi_{\text {ess }}, \rho\right)$, and let $\omega^{\prime}$ denote the corresponding Kähler form. Then by Lemma 8.3, $\omega^{\prime}-\omega_{1}=\sqrt{-1} \bar{\partial}_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t}^{*} f$ for some potential function $f \in C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}(X)$, and $\|f\|_{C_{\delta+2}^{k+2, \alpha}} \leq$ $C\left\|\omega^{\prime}-\omega_{1}\right\|_{C_{\delta}^{k, \alpha}}$.

As shown in the proof of Theorem 8.4, in a neighborhood of $g_{0}$, a scalar-flat Kähler metric is uniquely determined by the Kähler class and complex structure. This implies that $g^{\prime}=g_{1}$. \(\square\)

This completes the proof of Theorem 1.4. Next, we will complete the proof of Theorem 1.7. From Theorem 8.4, the mapping $F$ is $\mathfrak{G}$-equivariant. That is, for $\iota \in \mathfrak{G}$, and $(t, \rho) \in B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}$, we have $F\left(\iota^{*} t, \iota^{*} \rho\right)=\iota^{*} F(t, \rho)$. This clearly implies that two elements in the same orbit of $\mathfrak{G}$ are isometric, which proves the first statement in Theorem 1.7.

For the second statement in Theorem 1.7, we need the following lemma, which says that in the non-hyperkähler case, the dimension of $\mathfrak{F}$ is the same as the dimension of the parameter space.

Lemma 9.3. Let $(X, g, J)$ be as above. If $g$ is not hyperkähler then $F$ is injective, so $\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{F})=d=\operatorname{dim}\left(B_{\epsilon_{1}}^{1} \times B_{\epsilon_{2}}^{2}\right)$.

Proof. If $F\left(\phi_{1}, \rho_{1}\right)=F\left(\phi_{2}, \rho_{2}\right)=\tilde{g}$, then the metric $\tilde{g}$ is Kähler with respect to the two complex structures $J_{1}$ and $J_{2}$, corresponding to $\phi_{1}$ and $\phi_{2}$, respectively.

We have $J_{i} \in \operatorname{End}(T X)(i=1,2)$ such that $J_{i}^{2}=-1$. Locally, $J_{i}$ can be considered as an purely imaginary Hamiltonian number. Define
$$J_{3}=\frac{J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}}{\left\|J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}\right\|},$$
where for $p \in X$,
$$\left\|J_{i}(p)\right\|=\sup _{0 \neq v \in T_{p} X}\left\{\frac{\left\|J_{i}(v)\right\|_{h}}{\|v\|_{h}}\right\} .$$
Then $J_{3} \in \operatorname{End}(T X)$. We claim that $J_{3}$ is well-defined, $J_{3}^{2}=-1, J_{1}, J_{2}, J_{3}$ are linearly independent. Clearly,
$$\left(J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}\right)\left(J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}\right)=-2+\left(J_{1} J_{2} J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1} J_{2} J_{1}\right) .$$
Since $J_{1} J_{2} J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1} J_{2} J_{1}$ is real and has norm which is $<2$, we have that
$$\left(J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}\right)\left(J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}\right) \neq 0,$$
so $J_{3}$ is well-defined, and $J_{3} \cdot J_{3}=-1$. If we write $J_{1}, J_{2}$ locally as Hamiltonian numbers $a_{1} I+b_{1} J+c_{1} K, a_{2} I+b_{2} J+c_{2} K$ (where $a_{i}, b_{i}, c_{i} \in \mathbb{R}, I^{2}=J^{2}=K^{2}=-1$ ), then
$$J_{1} J_{2}-J_{2} J_{1}=\left(b_{0} c_{1}-b_{1} c_{0}\right) I+\left(c_{0} a_{1}-c_{1} a_{0}\right) J+\left(a_{0} c_{1}-a_{1} c_{0}\right) K,$$
which is linearly independent with $J_{1}$ and $J_{2}$. Then $J_{1}, J_{2}, J_{3}$ are linearly independent. As a result, $\left\{J_{1}, J_{2}, J_{3}\right\}$ gives a hyperkähler structure. Then we have proved that, if $\Gamma \not \subset \operatorname{SU}(2)$, then $J_{1}=J_{2}$, then by the proof of Theorem 8.4, $F$ is injective. \(\square\)

The next proposition immediately implies the second part of Theorem 1.7.
Proposition 9.4. For any two elements $g_{1}, g_{2} \in \mathfrak{F}$ associated to complex structures $J_{1}, J_{2}$ such that $E_{J_{0}}^{-1}\left(J_{1}\right), E_{J_{0}}^{-1}\left(J_{2}\right)$ are divergence-free, if there exists a small diffeomorphism $\Phi_{Y}$ which is induced by the exponential map of a vector field $Y \in C_{\delta+1}^{k+1, \alpha}(T X)$, such that $g_{1}=$ $\Phi_{Y}^{*} g_{2}$, then $g_{1}, g_{2}$ are the same.

Proof. First, assume $X$ is non-hyperkähler. Since $\Phi_{Y}^{*} g_{2}=g_{1}$, by Lemma 9.3, $J_{1}=\Phi_{Y}^{*} J_{2}$. However by Lemma 5.3, there is a unique small diffeomorphism that gauges $E_{J_{0}}^{-1}\left(J_{2}\right)$ to be divergence-free. Then $\Phi_{Y}=I d, g_{1}=g_{2}$.

When $X$ is hyperkähler, then by rotating the hyperkähler sphere, there exists a $J_{1}^{\prime}$ such that $J_{1}^{\prime}$ is compatible with $g_{1}$ and $\left(X, \Phi_{Y}^{*} J_{2}\right)$ is biholomorphic to $\left(X, J_{1}^{\prime}\right)$. Then by the same argument above, $g_{1}=g_{2}$. \(\square\)

Since $\mathfrak{F}$ is of finite dimension, and $\mathfrak{G}$ is a compact group action on $\mathfrak{F}$, the dimension $m$ of $\mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{F} / \mathfrak{G}$ is well-defined. In the non-hyperkähler case, by Lemma 9.3,
$$m=d-\text { (the dimension of a maximal orbit of } \mathfrak{G} \text { ). }$$
(For the hyperkähler case, recall Remark 1.11.)

\section*{10. Deformations of the minimal resolution}

In this section, we prove Theorems 1.10 and 1.12.
10.1. Harmonic representation of $H^{1}(X, \Theta)$. Let $X$ denote the minimal resolution of $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Gamma$, where $\Gamma$ is a finite subgroup of U(2) without complex reflections. In the following, we want to construct a weighted version of Hodge theory, that links the sheaf cohomology with the decaying harmonic forms.

The divisor $E=\cup_{i} E_{i}$ is a union of irreducible components which are rational curves, with only normal crossing singularities. Let $\operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)$ denote the sheaf dual to logarithmic 1-forms along $E$ (see [Kaw78]). We note that $\operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)$ is a locally free sheaf of rank 2, see [Wah75]. Away from $E$, this is clear. If $p \in E_{i}$, we can choose a holomorphic coordinate chart $\left\{z_{1}, z_{2}\right\}$ such that near $p, E_{i}=\left\{z_{1}=0\right\}$. Then local sections of $\operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)$ are generated by $\left\{z_{1} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}\right\}$.

The short exact sequence
$$0 \rightarrow \operatorname{Der}_{E}(X) \rightarrow \Theta_{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{O}_{E}(E) \rightarrow 0,$$
induces an exact sequence of cohomologies
$$0 \rightarrow H^{1}\left(X, \operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)\right) \rightarrow H^{1}(X, \Theta) \rightarrow H^{1}\left(E, \mathcal{O}_{E}(E)\right) \rightarrow H^{2}\left(X, \operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)\right) .$$
Since $E$ is composed of rational curves whose self-intersection numbers are negative, we have $H^{0}\left(E, \mathcal{O}_{E}(E)\right)=0$.

Proposition 10.1. We have the vanishing result: $H^{1}\left(X, \operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)\right)=0$.
Proof. This is a result which follows from work of [BKR88, Bri68, Lau73, Wah75]. \(\square\)

By Siu's vanishing theorem ([Siu69]), since $X$ is a non-compact $\sigma$-compact complex manifold, for any coherent analytic sheaf $\mathscr{F}$ on $X$, the top degree sheaf cohomology $H^{2}(X, \mathscr{F})$ is trivial. Consequently, $H^{2}\left(X, \operatorname{Der}_{E}(X)\right)=0$, which gives us an isomorphism $H^{1}\left(\Theta_{X}\right)=$ $H^{1}\left(O_{E}(E)\right)$. Let $-e_{j}$ be the self-intersection number of each rational curve $E_{j} \subset E$, and let $k_{\Gamma}$ be the number of rational curves (which is equal to $b_{2}$ ). Then
$$\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{1}(X, \Theta)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\Gamma}}\left(e_{j}-1\right) .$$

Theorem 10.2. Let $(X, g, J)$ denote the minimal resolution of $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Gamma$ with any $A L E$ Kähler metric $g$ of order $\tau>1$. Then
$$H^{1}(X, \Theta) \cong \mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right) \cong \mathcal{H}_{e s s}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$$
Proof. First, we consider the case on $\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\}$, and we compute $H^{1}\left(\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\}, \Theta\right)$. The domain $\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\}$ can be covered by two charts: $U_{1}=\left\{z_{1} \neq 0\right\}$ and $U_{2}=\left\{z_{2} \neq 0\right\}$. Note that $U_{1}, U_{2}$ are each isomorphic to $\mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}^{*}$, and $U_{1} \cap U_{2}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{*} \times \mathbb{C}^{*}$. Then $\theta_{1} \in \Gamma\left(U_{1}, \Theta\right)$ can be expanded into a Laurent series
$$\theta_{1}=\sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}, j \in \mathbb{Z}} a_{i, j}^{1} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+b_{i, j}^{1} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}},$$
$\theta_{2} \in \Gamma\left(U_{2}, \Theta\right)$ can be expanded into
$$\theta_{2}=\sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}, j \in \mathbb{Z} \geq 0} a_{i, j}^{2} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+b_{i, j}^{2} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}},$$
$\theta_{1,2} \in \Gamma\left(U_{1} \cap U_{2}, \Theta\right)$ can be expanded into
$$\theta_{1,2}=\sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}, j \in \mathbb{Z}} a_{i, j}^{1,2} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+b_{i, j}^{1,2} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}} .$$
Then any $\theta_{1,2} \in \Gamma\left(U_{1} \cap U_{2}, \Theta\right)$ which cannot be represented as $\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}$ must be of the form
$$\theta_{1,2}=\sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_{-}, j \in \mathbb{Z}_{-}} a_{i, j}^{1,2} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+b_{i, j}^{1,2} z_{1}^{i} z_{2}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}} .$$

By Dolbeault's lemma, we have $H^{1}(X, \Theta) \simeq H^{0}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$ ([GH94]). Using this, we transform the Čech cohomology element (10.8) to an element of Dolbeault cohomology $\phi \in$ $\Gamma\left(\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$. Let $\rho_{1}, \rho_{2}$ be a partition of unity, where $\rho_{1}$ is supported in $U_{1}$ and $\rho_{1}=1$ for $\left|z_{1}\right|>1 ; \rho_{2}$ is supported in $U_{2}$ and $\rho_{2}=1$ for $\left|z_{2}\right|>1 ; \rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=1$. Then define $\phi \in \Gamma\left(\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$ as
$$\phi=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
\bar{\partial}\left(\rho_{2} \cdot \theta_{1,2}\right) & \text { on } U_{1} \\
-\bar{\partial}\left(\rho_{1} \cdot \theta_{1,2}\right) & \text { on } U_{2}
\end{array} .\right.$$
The form $\phi$ is well-defined since $\bar{\partial}\left(\rho_{2} \cdot \theta_{1,2}\right)+\bar{\partial}\left(\rho_{1} \cdot \theta_{1,2}\right)=\bar{\partial}\left(\theta_{1,2}\right)=0$ on $U_{1} \cap U_{2}$. Furthermore, $\phi$ is decaying at infinity, since the degrees appearing in (10.8) are all negative.

Now for any closed form $\phi \in H^{0}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$, since $X-E$ is biholomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\} / \Gamma$, $\phi$ corresponds with a $\Gamma$-equivariant form $\phi^{\prime} \in H^{0}\left(\mathbb{C}^{2} \backslash\{0\}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$. Then $\phi^{\prime}=\bar{\partial} \tau+\psi$, with $\psi$ a closed form and decaying at infinity by the argument above. Since $\Gamma$ is a finite group, by averaging $\tau$ by the group action, we can assume that $\tau$ is $\Gamma$-invariant. Let $\rho$ be a cutoff function on $X$ which equals to 0 in $B(R)$ and 1 outside of $B(2 R)$. Then $\phi-\bar{\partial}(\rho \cdot \tau)$ is a closed decaying form, which is in the same class as $\phi$.

This shows that the natural mapping
$$\mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right) \rightarrow H^{1}(X, \Theta)$$
is surjective. We next show that this mapping is injective.
Let $\phi=\bar{\partial} \eta \in \mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$, where $\eta \in \Gamma(X, \Theta)$. Then $[\phi]=0 \in H^{1}(X, \Theta)$. We want to prove $\phi=0$ by showing that $\phi=\bar{\partial} \xi$ for some $\xi=O\left(r^{-2}\right)$. Let $\chi$ be a smooth cutoff function defined on $X$ with compact support that contains $E$. Then $\phi=\bar{\partial}(\chi \eta)+\bar{\partial}((1-\chi) \eta)$. Since $\pi$ is biholomorphic on $X \backslash E,(1-\chi) \eta$ can be pushed down by $\pi$ and extended to $\zeta$ on $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ such that $\zeta=0$ in a neighborhood of the origin. Also $\bar{\partial} \zeta=O\left(r^{-3}\right)$ on $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ by the decaying rate of $\phi$. Exactly as in (3.9) above, by the Poincaré lemma, there exists a $\sigma \in C_{-2}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}, \Theta\right)$ such that $\bar{\partial} \sigma=\bar{\partial} \zeta$. Average $\sigma$ by the group action of $\Gamma$ such that $\sigma$ is $\Gamma$-invariant, then $\sigma(0)=0$. Note that $\sigma$ is holomorphic in a neighborhood of 0 . By standard argument, we can lift up $\sigma$ to $\tilde{\sigma} \in \Gamma(X, \Theta)$, where $\tilde{\sigma}=O\left(|z|^{-2}\right)$. Then we have $\phi=\bar{\partial}(\chi \eta+\tilde{\sigma})$. Since $\chi \eta+\tilde{\sigma}=O\left(r^{-2}\right), \phi=0$.

Finally, we are going to prove that
$$\mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right) \simeq \mathcal{H}_{e s s}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right),$$
It is clear that $\mathcal{H}_{\text {ess }}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right) \subset \mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$. To show the isomorphism, we need to show that $\mathbb{V}=\{0\}$. Let $Y \in \mathbb{W}$, by definition, $Y \in \mathcal{H}_{1}(X, \Theta)$. Then $Y$ has the following
expansion as $r \rightarrow \infty$ :
$$Y=\sum_{i, j} a_{i, j} z_{i} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}+\sum_{k} b_{k} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}+O\left(r^{-3+\epsilon}\right)$$
where $a_{i, j}, b_{k}$ are constants. The decaying rate of $O\left(r^{-3+\epsilon}\right)$ comes from the fact that □ has no indicial roots between -2 and 0, and there is no $\bar{\partial}$-closed kernel element corresponding to the root of -2.

If $\Gamma$ is nontrivial, then $b_{k}=0$. Denote
$$\pi: X \mapsto \mathbb{C}^{2} / \Gamma$$
as the minimal resolution of $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Gamma$. Since $Z=\sum_{i, j} a_{i, j} z_{i} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}$ is a holomorphic vector field on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Gamma \backslash\{0\}$ and $\lim _{r \rightarrow 0} Z=0$, by standard argument, $Z$ can be lifted up to a holomorphic vector field $\tilde{Z}$ on $X$. Then $Y-\tilde{Z} \in \mathcal{H}_{-3}(X, \Theta)$. By using integration by parts, $Y-\tilde{Z}$ is holomorphic, then $Y$ is holomorphic, $\bar{\partial} Y=0$, and $V=0$. Then $\mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right) \simeq$ $\mathcal{H}_{\text {ess }}\left(X, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)$.

Finally, if $\Gamma$ is trivial, then $X$ is biholomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, and the coordinate vector fields extend to global holomorphic vector fields, so these do not give any non-trivial elements of $\mathbb{V}$. \(\square\)

\subsection*{10.2. Proof of Theorem 1.12.}

Proof of Theorem 1.12. In the following, $X_{t}$ will stand for the triple $\left(X, g_{t}, J_{t}\right)$. Since the index of a strongly continuous family of Fredholm operators is constant, the index $P(t)$ of the operator $P_{t}$ defined in Lemma 4.2 is locally constant along a path of ALE Kähler metrics. Consequently, $P(t)$ is constant along the path $X_{t}$. By the same argument as in Lemma 4.2, the cokernel of $P_{t}$ is trivial for all $t \in[0,1]$. Therefore $P_{t}$ are all surjective Fredholm operators, with $\operatorname{dim}\left(\operatorname{ker} P_{t}\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\operatorname{ker} P_{0}\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\operatorname{ker} P_{1}\right)$. Therefore if there exists a path that connects $X_{1}$ with a minimal resolution $X_{0}$, then $\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X_{1}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)\right)$. Since $\mathfrak{G}\left(X_{1}\right)=\{e\}$,
$$\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{e s s}\left(X_{1}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)\right)=\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{-3}\left(X_{0}, \Lambda^{0,1} \otimes \Theta\right)\right)-\operatorname{dim}\left(\mathfrak{G}\left(X_{0}\right)\right)=j_{\Gamma} .$$
In the non-hyperkähler case, Lemma 9.3 implies that
$$m\left(X_{1}\right)=j_{\Gamma}+b_{2}(X)=m_{\Gamma},$$
and Theorem 1.7 implies that the local moduli space of scalar-flat Kähler ALE metrics near $g_{1}$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $m_{\Gamma}$. As remarked above, the hyperkähler moduli space is constructed globally by Kronheimer, and $m_{\Gamma}=3 k-3$. \(\square\)
