\title{
THE TOPOLOGY OF THE EISENSTEIN-PICARD MODULAR SURFACE
}

\author{
JIMING MA AND BAOHUA XIE
}

\begin{abstract}
The Eisenstein-Picard modular surface $M$ is the quotient space of the complex hyperbolic plane by the modular group $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$. We determine the global topology of $M$ as a 4-orbifold.
\end{abstract}

\section*{1. Introduction}

Complex hyperbolic $n$-space $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}$ is the unique complete simply connected Kähler $n$-manifold with all holomorphic sectional curvatures -1. The holomorphic isometry group of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}$ is PU(n, 1). The Riemannian sectional curvatures of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}$ vary between -1 and -1/4. It turns out that complex hyperbolic manifolds are rather difficult to study.

Due to the extremal properties of ratios of their Chern numbers, complex hyperbolic manifolds are also of special interest in complex geometry. One of the most important questions in complex hyperbolic geometry is the existence of (infinitely many commensurable classes of) non-arithmetic complex hyperbolic lattices, but we know very few examples of them [2, 5, 7, 8, 18, 13] now.

Even through it is well-known there are infinitely many commensurable classes of arithmetic complex hyperbolic manifolds in any complex dimension at least two, but the explicit algebraic, geometric and topological information of them are still very mysterious. For example, Picard modular surfaces $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} / \mathrm{PU}\left(2,1 ; \mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)$ are the simplest complex hyperbolic lattices. But the geometries and algebras of them are achieved until recently only for very few $d$ [9, 11, 12, 17]. Here $\mathcal{O}_{d}$ is the ring of integers in the imaginary quadratic number field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$. For the algebras of fake complex projective planes, a tiny portions of the second type arithmetic complex hyperbolic lattices, see [1, 22]. We still need

In this paper, we begin the study of the topologies of complex hyperbolic lattices. We consider a complex dimension one example before outline our result.
1.1. A toy example. The 2-disk $\mathbb{D}^{2}$ can be identified as
$$\{z \in \mathbb{C}:|z| \leqslant 1\} .$$
For each $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{+}$, there is a smooth $\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\langle f\rangle$-action on $\mathbb{D}^{2}$, namely
$$f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2 \pi \mathrm{i}}{n}} \cdot z .$$
The quotient space of $\mathbb{D}^{2}$ by this $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$-action is a 2-orbifold $\mathcal{F}_{n}$, there is a $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$-coned (or $2 \pi / n$-coned) point $c_{n}$ in it.

Consider the well-known modular surface $\Sigma$, where $\Sigma$ is the quotient space of the complex hyperbolic line $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}$ by the modular group $\operatorname{PSL}(2 ; \mathbb{Z})$. Recall that PSL(2; $\mathbb{Z}$ ) is generated by $a$ and $b$, with $a^{2}=$ $b^{3}=i d$ and $a b$ is parabolic. So $\Sigma$ is a $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}$-orbifold, with a $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-coned point, a $\mathbb{Z}_{3}$-coned point, and a cusp. Take a simple closed curve $C$ in $\Sigma$ surrounding the cusp. Then $\Sigma-C \times(0, \infty)$ is a compact orbifold, the interior of it is topologically homeomorphic to $\Sigma$, so it is a compact core of $\Sigma$. We can understand the topology of $\Sigma$ by understanding the topology of $\Sigma-C \times(0, \infty)$.
$\Sigma-C \times(0, \infty)$ can be obtained with
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] first we take two 2-orbifolds $\mathcal{F}_{2}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{3}$. Which are small closed neighborhoods of the $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-coned point and $\mathbb{Z}_{3}$-coned point in $\Sigma$ respectively;
\item[-] then we glue a disk [0, 1] × [0, 1] to $\mathcal{F}_{2} \cup \mathcal{F}_{3}$ to get a connected orientable 2-orbifold: $\{0\} \times[0,1]$ is identified with an arc in the boundary of $\mathcal{F}_{2}$, and $\{1\} \times[0,1]$ is identified with an arc in the boundary of $\mathcal{F}_{3}$.
\end{itemize}
So we view $\mathcal{F}_{2}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{3}$ as orbifold 0-handles, the glued disk [0, 1] $\times[0,1]$ as a 1-handle. Then we give $\Sigma-C \times(0, \infty)$ a topological handle structure. See Figure 1 for the process to get a handle structure of $\Sigma-C \times(0, \infty)$. Where the left subfigure of Figure 1 is the Ford domain of $\operatorname{PSL}(2 ; \mathbb{Z})$ on $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}$ (in the upper half space model). The boundary of the Ford domain consists of arcs of Euclidean circles with radius 1 centered at integers. The reader may find it is helpful to keep this example in mind.

In this paper, we will consider a similar problem for the more complicated $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$-orbifolds.

1.2. Main result of the paper. We determine the global topology of the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface as a 4-orbifold in this paper.

Let $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega]) \subset \operatorname{PU}(2,1)$ be the Eisenstein-Picard modular group, where $\omega=(-1+\mathrm{i} \sqrt{3}) / 2$ is a cube roots of unity. The group $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$ is a generalization of $\operatorname{PSL}(2 ; \mathbb{Z})$ into complex dimension two. We denote the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface by $M$, which is the quotient space of the complex hyperbolic plane $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ by the group $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$.

Consider a compact core $N$ of $M$. That is, $N$ is a compact 4-orbifold with boundary a 3-orbifold, such that the interior of $N$ is homeomorphic to $M$. Our main result is

Theorem 1.1. Let $M$ be the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface, and $N$ a compact core of $M$. Then $N$ can be obtained topologically as:
\begin{itemize}
\item[] Step 1. Describe the topologies of four orbifold 0-handles $h\left(w_{3}\right), h\left(w_{4}\right)$, $h\left(w_{12}\right)$ and $h\left(z_{0}\right)$.
\item[] Step 2. Attach a 1-handle $h\left(\left[w_{3}, w_{4}\right]\right)$ and an orbifold 1-handle $h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{12}\right]\right)$.
\item[] Step 3. Attach an object $h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}, w_{12}\right]\right)$, the product of a pair of pants and the 2-orbifold $\mathcal{F}_{2}$, to get a 4-orbifold with 0-dimensional and 2-dimensional singular sets.
\item[] Step 4. Attach two 2-handles
\end{itemize}
$$h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}, w_{3}, J\left(w_{4}\right)\right]\right) \quad \text { and } \quad h\left(\left[z_{0}, J\left(w_{4}\right), z_{1}, w_{12}\right]\right) .$$

Step 5. Attach a 3-handle
$$h\left(\left[w_{12}, z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2},\left(w_{3}, w_{4}, J\left(w_{4}\right), P J\left(w_{4}\right)\right)\right]\right) .$$

Any one of the notations in Theorem 1.1, such as $h\left(z_{i}\right), h\left(w_{j}\right)$ and $h\left(\left[z_{0}, J\left(w_{4}\right), z_{1}, w_{12}\right]\right)$ etc., has a geometrical meaning. Which are related to cells of a fundamental domain $D^{*}$ of $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$, see Subsection 4.5 for the details. For more on orbifold $k$-handles, see Subsection 4.1.

Handle theory is a standard tool for the study of topologies of manifolds. By the well-known Selberg's Lemma, there is a finite cover $M^{\prime}$ of $M$ such that $M^{\prime}$ is a 4-manifold (so without singular set). But there is an order 72 torsion subgroup of $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$, so the covering degree $M^{\prime} \rightarrow M$ is at least 72. In turn the topology of $M^{\prime}$ seems complicated (to the authors). So it seems better to study the topology of $M$ instead of $M^{\prime}$. Then we introduce the terms orbifold $k$-handles for $k=0,1,2$, which are mild (or even trivial) generalizations of $k$-handles. Moreover, we believe orbifold $k$-handles are natural and convenient tools to study the topologies of 4-orbifolds. We hope this will not plague the reader.

The procedure to get the topology of $N$ in Theorem 1.1 stage by stage is sketched in Figure 2.

In Step 1, each of $w_{3}, w_{4}, w_{12}$ and $z_{0}$ is the fixed point of an isotropy group of order 3, 4, 12 and 72 respectively. The associated orbifold 0-handles are just small closed neighborhoods of them in $M$. See Subsections 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4 for the topologies of these orbifold 0-handles.

In Step 2, the 1-handle $h\left(\left[w_{3}, w_{4}\right]\right)$ is simple, we just attach $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathbb{D}^{3}$ to orbifold 0-handles $h\left(w_{3}\right), h\left(w_{4}\right)$ along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathbb{D}^{3}$. The orbifold 1-handle $h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{12}\right]\right)$ is a little tricky, which is $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{6}\right)$. So there is $\mathbb{Z}_{6}$-coned singular set in it. Moreover, the $\mathbb{Z}_{6}$-coned singular sets of $\partial \mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{6}\right)$ are glued to (subsets of) $\mathbb{Z}_{6}$-coned singular sets of the boundaries of $h\left(w_{4}\right)$ and $h\left(w_{12}\right)$ respectively.

In Step 3, $h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}, w_{12}\right]\right)$ is the product of a pair of pants and the 2 -orbifold $\mathcal{F}_{2}$, so it is a $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-coned 4-orbifold. Three copies of $\mathbb{S}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{2}$ are gluing regions of $h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}, w_{12}\right]\right)$, which are glued to $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-coned singular sets of the boundaries of $h\left(w_{4}\right), h\left(w_{12}\right)$ and $h\left(z_{0}\right)$ respectively.

In Step 4, the 2-handles $h\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}, w_{3}, J\left(w_{4}\right)\right]\right)$ and $h\left(\left[z_{0}, J\left(w_{4}\right), z_{1}, w_{12}\right]\right)$ are attached along two well-chosen curves on the 4-orbifold obtained after Step 3. These information can be formulated in the fundamental domain $D^{*}$ (in Section 4 ) of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} / \mathrm{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ ).

The 3-handle in Step 5 does not affect the fundamental group of the 4-orbifold after step 4.

Moreover, there are some papers which relating some $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$-lattices to other (in some sense simple) complex surfaces (and curve arrangements in complex surfaces) [3, 4, 16]. To our knowledge, Theorem 1.1 is one of the very few results (if not the first one) about explicit topology structures of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$-lattices.

Our proof of Theorem 1.1 depends essentially on the fundamental domain $D$ of $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$ 's action on $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ by Falbel-Parker [12]. The fundamental domain $D$ by Falbel-Parker, combinatorically a 4-simplex, is extremely simple. But at the same time, it misleads the complexity of the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface. There are three isolated points of isotropy groups, namely $w_{3}, w_{4}$ and $w_{12}$, hiding in the edges and ridges of the 4-simplex $D$. So we subdivide $D$ into a new fundamental domain $D^{*}$. With the help of $D^{*}$, we can get Theorem 1.1.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we give well known background material on complex hyperbolic geometry and some information about the fundamental domain $D$ constructed in [12]. In Section 3, we consider local topologies of isolated points of isotropy groups in $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$. In Section 4 we give the handle structure of the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface based on the cells of the new fundamental domain $D^{*}$, so we prove Theorem 1.1.

\section*{2. Preliminary}

The purpose of this section is to introduce briefly complex hyperbolic geometry. One can refer to Goldman's book [14] for more details. We also sketch some properties of $\operatorname{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$ after Falbel-Parker [12].
2.1. Complex hyperbolic plane. Let $\langle\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{w}\rangle=\mathbf{w}^{*} H \mathbf{z}$ be the Hermitian form on $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ associated to $H$, where $H$ is the Hermitian matrix
$$H=\left[\begin{array}{lll}
0 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 0 & 0
\end{array}\right]$$
of signature (2, 1). Then $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ is the union of the negative cone $V_{-}$, null cone $V_{0}$ and positive cone $V_{+}$, where
$$\begin{aligned}
V_{-} & =\left\{\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{3} \backslash\{0\}:\langle\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}\rangle<0\right\}, \\
V_{0} & =\left\{\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{3} \backslash\{0\}:\langle\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}\rangle=0\right\}, \\
V_{+} & =\left\{\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{C}^{3} \backslash\{0\}:\langle\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}\rangle>0\right\} .
\end{aligned}$$

Definition 2.1. Let $P: \mathbb{C}^{3} \backslash\{0\} \rightarrow \mathbb{C} P^{2}$ be the projectivization map. Then the complex hyperbolic plane $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ is defined to be $P\left(V_{-}\right)$, and its
boundary $\partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ is defined to be $P\left(V_{0}\right)$. The Bergman metric on the complex hyperbolic plane is given by the distance formula
$$\cosh ^{2}\left(\frac{d(u, v)}{2}\right)=\frac{\langle\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v}\rangle},$$
where $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{C}^{3}$ are lifts of $u, v$. Here $d(u, v)$ is the distance between two points $u, v \in \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$.

The standard lift
$$\left[\begin{array}{c}
z_{1} \\
z_{2} \\
1
\end{array}\right]$$
of $z=\left(z_{1}, z_{2}\right) \in \mathbb{C}^{2}$ is negative if and only if
$$z_{1}+\left|z_{2}\right|^{2}+\bar{z}_{1}=2 \operatorname{Re}\left(z_{1}\right)+\left|z_{2}\right|^{2}<0 .$$
Thus $\mathbb{P}\left(V_{-}\right)$is a paraboloid in $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, called the Siegel domain. In these coordinates, the boundary $\mathbb{P}\left(V_{0}\right)$ is given by
$$2 \operatorname{Re}\left(z_{1}\right)+\left|z_{2}\right|^{2}=0 .$$

Let $\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{R}$ be the Heisenberg group with product
$$[z, t] \cdot[\zeta, \nu]=[z+\zeta, t+\nu-2 \operatorname{Im}(\bar{z} \zeta)] .$$
We write $q=[z, t] \in \mathcal{N}$ for $z \in \mathbb{C}$ and $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Then the boundary of the complex hyperbolic plane $\partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ can be identified with the union $\mathcal{N} \cup\left\{q_{\infty}\right\}$, where $q_{\infty}$ is the point at infinity. The standard lift of $q_{\infty}$ and $q=[z, t] \in \mathcal{N}$ in $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ are
$$\mathbf{q}_{\infty}=\left[\begin{array}{l}
1 \\
0 \\
0
\end{array}\right] \quad \text { and } \quad \mathbf{q}=\left[\begin{array}{c}
\frac{-|z|^{2}+i t}{2} \\
z \\
1
\end{array}\right] .$$

Complex hyperbolic plane and its boundary $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \cup \partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ can be identified with $\mathcal{N} \times \mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0} \cup q_{\infty}$. Any point $q=(z, t, u) \in \mathcal{N} \times \mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}$ has the standard lift
$$\mathbf{q}=\left[\begin{array}{c}
\frac{-|z|^{2}-u+i t}{2} \\
z \\
1
\end{array}\right] .$$
Here $(z, t, u)$ is called the horospherical coordinates of $\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \cup \partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$. The natural projection $\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ is called the vertical projection.

For $v>0$, the horoball $H_{>v}$ of height $v$ is the open subspace of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ in horospherical coordinates $(z, t, u)$ with $u>v$. The boundary of $H_{>v}$, that is, the subspace of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ in horospherical coordinates $(z, t, v)$, is the horosphere of height $v$.
2.2. The isometries. The complex hyperbolic plane is a Kähler manifold of constant holomorphic sectional curvature -1. We denote by U $(2,1)$ the Lie group of $\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle$ preserving complex linear transformations and by $\operatorname{PU}(2,1)$ the group modulo scalar matrices. The group of holomorphic isometries of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ is exactly PU(2, 1). It is sometimes convenient to work with $\operatorname{SU}(2,1)$, which is a 3-fold cover of $\operatorname{PU}(2,1)$.

Elements of $\operatorname{SU}(2,1)$ fall into three types, according to the number and types of the fixed points of the corresponding isometry. Namely, an isometry is loxodromic (resp. parabolic) if it has exactly two fixed points (resp. one fixed point) on $\partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$. It is called elliptic when it has (at least) one fixed point inside $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$. An elliptic $A \in \operatorname{SU}(2,1)$ is called regular elliptic whenever it has three distinct eigenvalues, and special elliptic if it has a repeated eigenvalue.

Any unipotent element of $\operatorname{SU}(2,1)$ is conjugate in SU(2, 1) to one fixing $q_{\infty}$ given by:
$$T_{[z, t]}=\left(\begin{array}{ccc}
1 & -\bar{z} & \frac{-|z|^{2}+i t}{2} \\
0 & 1 & z \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right) .$$
Note that applying $T_{[z, t]}$ to $[w, s]$ amounts to doing the Heisenberg left multiplication by $[z, t]$. For that reason $T_{[z, t]}$ is called a Heisenberg translation. A Heisenberg translation by $[0, t]$ is called a vertical translation by $t$.

The full stabilizer of $q_{\infty}$ is generated by the above unipotent group, together with the isometries of the forms
$$\left(\begin{array}{ccc}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & e^{i \theta} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right) \quad \text { and } \quad\left(\begin{array}{ccc}
\lambda & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 / \lambda
\end{array}\right),$$
where $\theta, \lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\lambda \neq 0$. The first acts on $\partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \backslash\left\{q_{\infty}\right\}=\mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{R}$ as a rotation with vertical axis:
$$(z, t) \mapsto\left(e^{i \theta} z, t\right),$$
whereas the second one acts as
$$(z, t) \mapsto\left(\lambda z, \lambda^{2} t\right) .$$
Note that the parabolic isometries fixing $q_{\infty}$ are Cygan isometries, see [14].

\subsection*{2.3. Totally geodesic submanifolds and complex reflections.}

There are two kinds of totally geodesic submanifolds of real dimension 2 in $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ : complex lines in $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ are complex geodesics (represented by $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1} \subset \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ ) and Lagrangian planes in $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ are totally real geodesic

2-planes (represented by $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2} \subset \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ ). Since the Riemannian sectional curvature of the complex hyperbolic plane is nonconstant, there are no totally geodesic hypersurfaces. A polar vector of a complex line $L$ is the unique vector (up to scalar multiplication) perpendicular to this complex line with respect to the Hermitian form. A polar vector belongs to $V_{+}$and each vector in $V_{+}$corresponds to a complex line.

There is a special class of elliptic elements in $\operatorname{PU}(2,1)$.
Definition 2.2. The complex reflection of order $k$ about the complex line $C$ with polar vector n is given by the following formula:
$$I_{C}(\mathbf{z})=-\mathbf{z}+\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2 \pi \mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{k}}}\right) \frac{2\langle\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{n}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{n}\rangle} \mathbf{n} .$$
Then $I_{C}$ is a holomorphic isometry fixing the complex line $C$ pointwisely.

\subsection*{2.4. Isometric spheres, spinal coordinates and Ford polyhedron.}

Definition 2.3. The (extended) Cygan metric on $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ is given by
$$d_{\mathrm{Cyg}}(p, q)=\left||z-w|^{2}+|u-v|-i(t-s+2 \operatorname{Im}(z \bar{w}))\right|^{1 / 2},$$
where $p=(z, t, u)$ and $q=(w, s, v)$ are in horospherical coordinates.
Suppose that $g=\left(g_{i j}\right)_{i, j=1}^{3} \in \mathrm{PU}(2,1)$ does not fix $q_{\infty}$. This implies that $g_{31} \neq 0$, see Lemma 4.1 of [21].

Definition 2.4. The isometric sphere of $g$, denoted by $I(g)$, is the set
$$I(g)=\left\{p \in \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \cup \partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, g^{-1}\left(\mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right)\right\rangle\right|\right\} .$$

The interior of $I(g)$ is the set
$$\left\{p \in \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \cup \partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right\rangle\right|>\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, g^{-1}\left(\mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right)\right\rangle\right|\right\} .$$
The exterior of $I(g)$ is the set
$$\left\{p \in \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \cup \partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right\rangle\right|<\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, g^{-1}\left(\mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right)\right\rangle\right|\right\} .$$

The isometric sphere $I(g)$ is the Cygan sphere with center
$$g^{-1}\left(\mathbf{q}_{\infty}\right)=\left[\frac{\overline{g_{32}}}{\overline{g_{31}}}, 2 \operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{\overline{g_{33}}}{\overline{g_{31}}}\right)\right]$$
and radius $r_{g}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\left|g_{31}\right|}}$.
Definition 2.5. The Ford domain $D_{\Gamma}$ for a discrete group $\Gamma<\mathbf{P U}(2,1)$ centered at $q_{\infty}$ is the intersection of the (closures of the) exteriors of
all isometric spheres of elements in $\Gamma$ not fixing $q_{\infty}$. That is, $D_{\Gamma}$ is the set
$$\left\{p \in \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2} \cup \partial \mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, q_{\infty}\right\rangle\right| \leqslant\left|\left\langle\mathbf{p}, G^{-1}\left(q_{\infty}\right)\right\rangle\right| \forall G \in \Gamma \text { with } G\left(q_{\infty}\right) \neq q_{\infty}\right\} .$$

The Ford domain is preserved by $\Gamma_{\infty}$, the stabilizer of $q_{\infty}$ in $\Gamma$. In this case, $D_{\Gamma}$ is only a fundamental domain modulo the action of $\Gamma_{\infty}$. In other words, the intersection of the Ford domain with a fundamental domain for $\Gamma_{\infty}$ is a fundamental domain for $\Gamma$. Facets of co-dimension one, two, three and four in $D_{\Gamma}$ will be called sides, ridges, edges and vertices, respectively.

Once there is a guessed Ford domain for a group $\Gamma$, then one can check it is in fact the Ford domain using the Poincaré polyhedron theorem. Moreover, the side-pairings and the Poincaré polyhedron theorem give an abstract presentation of $\Gamma$, see [7].

\section*{4. Topology of Eisenstein-Picard modular surface $M$}

In this section, after some preparations on orbifold handles, we subdivide Falbel-Parker's fundamental domain $D$ into a new one $D^{*}$. Based on side-pairings on $D^{*}$, we prove Theorem 1.1 in the last.
4.1. An introduction to handle theory. We give a sketch of handle theory following [15]. From now on, we assume all 4-orbifolds and 4-manifolds are orientable.

Definition 4.1. For $0 \leqslant k \leqslant 4$, a 4-dimensional $k$-handle $h$ is a copy of $\mathbb{D}^{k} \times \mathbb{D}^{4-k}$, attached to the boundary of an orientable smooth 4-manifold $Y$ along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{k} \times \mathbb{D}^{4-k}$ by an embedding $\phi: \partial \mathbb{D}^{k} \times \mathbb{D}^{4-k} \rightarrow \partial Y$, to get an orientable smooth 4-manifold $Y \cup_{\phi} h$.

For a 4-dimensional $k$-handle $h$ :
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] $\mathbb{D}^{k} \times\{0\}$ is called the core;
\item[-] $\{0\} \times \mathbb{D}^{4-k}$ is the cocore;
\item[-] $\phi$ is the attaching map;
\item[-] $\partial \mathbb{D}^{k} \times\{0\}$ (or its image) is the attaching sphere;
\item[-] $\{0\} \times \partial \mathbb{D}^{n-k}$ is the belt sphere;
\item[-] The number $k$ is called the index of the handle.
\end{itemize}
Since there is a deformation retraction of $Y \cup_{\phi} h$ onto $Y \cup_{\phi_{\left.\right|^{\mathbb{R}^{k} \times\{0\}}}} \mathbb{D}^{k} \times\{0\}$, we have that up to homotopy, attaching a $k$-handle is the same as attaching a $k$-cell.

A 4-dimensional 0-handle is attached to $\partial Y$ along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{0} \times \mathbb{D}^{4}=\emptyset$, so attaching a 0-handle to a 4-manifold is the same as taking the disjoint union of $Y$ with $\mathbb{D}^{4}$.

The attaching sphere of a 1-handle is $\partial \mathbb{D}^{1} \times\{0\}$, which is a pair of points. If $\partial Y$ is connected and nonempty, then there is a unique isotopy class of $\phi: \partial \mathbb{D}^{0} \times\{0\} \rightarrow \partial X$ since we assume all the manifolds are orientable. If $\partial Y$ is disconnected, a 1-handle attaching to two component of $\partial Y$ is just the boundary sum along two components of $\partial Y$.

Since $\pi_{2}(O(1))$ is the trivial group, there is a unique way to attach a 3-handle $\mathbb{D}^{3} \times \mathbb{D}^{1}$ along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{3} \times \mathbb{D}^{1}$ to the boundary of a 4-manifold $Y$.

A 4-handle $h$ is the same as $\mathbb{D}^{4}$ attached along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{4}=\mathbb{S}^{3}$.
As above, 4-dimensional $k$-handles are easy to understand, for $k=$ 0, 1, 3 and 4. However, 2-handles are more complicated. For a 2-handle $\mathbb{D}^{2} \times \mathbb{D}^{2}$, it is determined by an embedded circle in $\partial Y$ and an integer (so called "framing"), they together corresponds to a Dehn surgery from $\partial Y$ to $\partial\left(Y \cup_{\phi} \mathbb{D}^{2} \times \mathbb{D}^{2}\right)$, see Part II of [15].

We give a mild/trivial generalization of $k$-handles into orbifold $k$ -handles.

First, let $L$ be a closed orientable 3-orbifold with finite orbifold-fundamental group $G$. If the singular set $\mathcal{S}$ of $L$ is non-empty, we assume the singular set $\mathcal{S}$ is a disjoint union of circles (this is the case
we will use in this paper). So $L$ has $\mathbb{S}^{3}$ as the universal cover, $L$ is a $\mathbb{S}^{3}$ -orbifold. The cone on $L$ is a 4-orbifold $Y$, which can also be obtained from $\mathbb{D}^{4}$ by $G$-action with exactly one global fixed point. Moreover, $Y$ has 2-dimensional singular set if and only if the singular set $\mathcal{S}$ of $L$ is non-empty.

Definition 4.2. The cone $Y$ based on a $\mathbb{S}^{3}$-orbifold $L$ is a 4-dimensional orbifold 0-handle.

Recall the 2-orbifold $\mathcal{F}_{n}$ with the cone point $c_{n}$ in the introduction, which is just the quotient space of $\mathbb{D}^{2}$ by $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$. So the product $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}$ is a 3-orbifold, with underlying space a 3-ball and there is a $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$-coned proper arc it. Then $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)$ can be viewed as a 4-orbifold with 2-dimensional singular set $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left\{c_{n}\right\}$.

Definition 4.3. A 4-dimensional orbifold 1-handle $h$ is a copy of $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times$ $\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)$, attached to the boundary of an orientable smooth 4-orbifold $Y$ along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)$ by an embedding $\phi: \partial \mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}\right) \rightarrow \partial Y$, such that $\partial \mathbb{D}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)$ are identified with two copies of $\mathbb{D}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}$ in the boundary of $Y$, to get an orientable smooth 4-orbifold $Y \cup_{\phi} h$.

Definition 4.4. A 4-dimensional orbifold 2-handle $h$ is a copy of $\mathbb{D}^{2} \times$ $\mathcal{F}_{n}$, attached to the boundary of an orientable smooth 4-orbifold $Y$ along $\partial \mathbb{D}^{2} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}$ by an embedding $\phi: \partial \mathbb{D}^{2} \times \mathcal{F}_{n} \rightarrow \partial Y$, such that $\partial \mathbb{D}^{2} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}$ is identified with a copy of $\mathbb{S}^{1} \times \mathcal{F}_{n}$ in the boundary of $Y$, to get an orientable smooth 4-orbifold $Y \cup_{\phi} h$.
4.2. Equivalent classes of faces of the fundamental polytope $D$. Since the fundamental polytope $D$ constructed by Falbel-Parker is a 4-simplex, we will denote a $k$-face of $D$ by its $(k+1)$ vertices. It is not difficult to get the equivalent classes of faces of $D$ under the action of $\Gamma=\mathrm{PU}(2,1 ; \mathbb{Z}[\omega])$.

4.5. A handle structure of the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface $M$. We now consider the global topology of the Eisenstein-Picard modular surface $M$.

We take a horosphere $H_{u}$ in $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$ with $u$ large enough, $H_{>u}$ is the associated horoball. Then $\Gamma$ acts on
$$\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}-\cup_{\gamma \in \Gamma} \gamma \cdot H_{>u}$$
proper discontinuously, the quotient space is denoted by $M_{\leqslant u}$. We can view $M_{\leqslant u}$ as the space as truncating the cusp of $M$. So $M_{\leqslant u}$ is a compact 4-orbifold with non-empty boundary, and $M$ is homeomorphic to the interior of $M_{\leqslant u}$. Then $M_{\leqslant u}$ is a compact core of $M$.

On the other hand, $M$ is the quotient space of
$$\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}, q_{\infty}\right]-q_{\infty}$$
by side-parings. Topologically, $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}, q_{\infty}\right]-q_{\infty}$ is homeomophic to $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}\right] \times[0, \infty)$, we fix such a homeomorphism. In particular, $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}, q_{\infty}\right]-q_{\infty}$ is homeomophic to its subset $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}\right] \times[0, t)$ for any $t>0$. Moreover, the homeomorphism is $\Gamma$-invariant. In particular, if we consider side-parings on $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}\right] \times[0, t]$, which are restrictions of side-parings on $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}, q_{\infty}\right]-q_{\infty}$. So $M_{\leqslant u}$ is home-omophic to the quotient space of $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}\right] \times[0, t]$ by side-parings for any $t>0$.

Consider the tetrahedron $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}\right]$ in $\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}$, the quotient space of it is a 3-dimensional CW-complex $Y$ in $M$. We should note some 2-cells of $Y$ are totally geodesic in $M$, which are crucial for the topology of $M$. We take a closed neighborhood $N$ of $Y$ in $M$, which is the quotient space of $\left[z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2}, z_{3}\right] \times[0, \epsilon]$ for $\epsilon>0$ small enough. So $M_{\leqslant u}$ is homeomorphic to $N$, and then $N$ is a compact core of $M$.

We now consider the topology of $N$. We first sketch the process, then we will give more details later. Since $N$ can be viewed as the union of small closed neighborhoods $N(c)$ in $M$ for each cell $c$ in Table 1 without $q_{\infty}$ as one of its vertices. That is, we may take $c$ be any of
$$\begin{gathered}
{\left[z_{0}\right],\left[w_{3}\right],\left[w_{4}\right],\left[w_{12}\right],} \\
{\left[z_{0}, w_{12}\right],\left[z_{1}, w_{12}\right],\left[z_{0}, w_{4}\right],\left[w_{3}, w_{4}\right]} \\
{\left[z_{0}, w_{4}, w_{3}, J\left(w_{4}\right)\right],\left[z_{0}, J\left(w_{4}\right), z_{1}, w_{12}\right],\left[z_{1}, w_{12}, z_{2}, w_{4}\right]}
\end{gathered}$$
and
$$\left[w_{12}, z_{0}, z_{1}, z_{2},\left(w_{3}, w_{4}, J\left(w_{4}\right), P J\left(w_{4}\right)\right)\right] .$$
By this we mean we first take small closed neighborhoods
$$N\left(\left[z_{0}\right]\right), N\left(\left[w_{3}\right]\right), N\left(\left[w_{4}\right]\right), N\left(\left[w_{12}\right]\right),$$
in $M$. We will view them as orbifold 0-handles.

Then we take small closed neighborhoods
$$N\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{12}\right]\right), N\left(\left[z_{1}, w_{12}\right]\right), N\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}\right]\right), N\left(\left[w_{3}, w_{4}\right]\right)$$
of edges $\left[z_{0}, w_{12}\right],\left[z_{1}, w_{12}\right],\left[z_{0}, w_{4}\right],\left[w_{3}, w_{4}\right]$ in $M$. But for technical reasons, what we really need are
$$\begin{aligned}

N\left(\left[z_{1}, w_{12}\right]\right) & -\operatorname{int}\left(N\left(\left[z_{1}\right]\right) \cup N\left(\left[w_{12}\right]\right)\right), \\
N\left(\left[z_{0}, w_{4}\right]\right) & -\operatorname{int}\left(N\left(\left[z_{0}\right]\right) \cup N\left(\left[w_{4}\right]\right)\right),
\end{aligned}$$
and
$$N\left(\left[w_{3}, w_{4}\right]\right)-\operatorname{int}\left(N\left(\left[w_{3}\right]\right) \cup N\left(\left[w_{4}\right]\right)\right)$$
