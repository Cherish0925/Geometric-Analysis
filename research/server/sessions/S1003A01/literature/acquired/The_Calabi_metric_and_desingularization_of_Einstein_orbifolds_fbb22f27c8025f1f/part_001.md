\title{
THE CALABI METRIC AND DESINGULARIZATION OF EINSTEIN ORBIFOLDS
}

\author{
PEYMAN MORTEZA AND JEFF A. VIACLOVSKY
}

\begin{abstract}
Consider an Einstein orbifold $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ of real dimension $2 n$ having a singularity with orbifold group the cyclic group of order $n$ in $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ which is generated by an $n$th root of unity times the identity. Existence of a Ricci-flat Kähler ALE metric with this group at infinity was shown by Calabi. There is a natural "approximate" Einstein metric on the desingularization of $M_{0}$ obtained by replacing a small neighborhood of the singular point of the orbifold with a scaled and truncated Calabi metric. In this paper, we identify the first obstruction to perturbing this approximate Einstein metric to an actual Einstein metric. If $M_{0}$ is compact, we can use this to produce examples of Einstein orbifolds which do not admit any Einstein metric in a neighborhood of the natural approximate Einstein metric on the desingularization. In the case that $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ is asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein and non-degenerate, we show that if the first obstruction vanishes, then there does in fact exist an asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein metric on the desingularization. We also obtain a non-existence result in the asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein case, provided that the obstruction does not vanish. This work extends a construction of Biquard in the case $n=2$, in which case the Calabi metric is also known as the Eguchi-Hanson metric, but there are some key points for which the higher-dimensional case differs.
\end{abstract}

\section*{1. Introduction}

Let $\Gamma_{n}=\left\{e^{\frac{2 k \pi i}{n}}: k=0, \ldots, n-1\right\}$ and consider the action of $\Gamma_{n}$ on $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ by
$$e^{\frac{2 k \pi i}{n}} \cdot\left(z^{1}, \ldots, z^{n}\right)=\left(e^{\frac{2 k \pi i}{n}} z^{1}, \ldots, e^{\frac{2 k \pi i}{n}} z^{n}\right) .$$
The Calabi metric $g_{\text {cal }}$ is a Ricci-flat Kähler asymptotically locally Euclidean (ALE) metric on the total space $X$ of the line bundle $\mathcal{O}(-n) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, which is $\mathrm{U}(n)$-invariant. The group at infinity is $\Gamma_{n} \subset \operatorname{SU}(n)$.

Let $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ denote an Einstein orbifold of real dimension $m=2 n$ satisfying
$$\operatorname{Ric}\left(g_{0}\right)=\Lambda g_{0},$$
and which has a singular point with group $\Gamma_{n}$. One can desingularize the orbifold metric by gluing on a Calabi metric in the following manner.

Since $g_{c a l}$ is Ricci-flat and ALE, there exists a coordinate system $\left\{x^{i}\right\}, i=1 \ldots 2 n$, and a diffeomorphism
$$\Psi:\left(\mathbb{R}^{2 n} \backslash B(0, R)\right) / \Gamma_{n} \rightarrow X \backslash K,$$
where $B(0, R)$ is a ball of radius $R$, and $K$ is a compact subset of $X$ so that
$$\begin{aligned}
\Psi^{*} g_{c a l} & =g_{e u c}+O\left(r^{-2 n}\right), \\
\partial^{\alpha} \Psi^{*} g_{c a l} & =O\left(r^{-2 n-|\alpha|}\right),
\end{aligned}$$
where $\alpha$ is a multi-index of order $|\alpha|, r=|x|$, and $g_{\text {euc }}$ denotes the Euclidean metric. In the following we extend $r$ to a globally defined function on $X$ which satisfies $0<r<R$ on the interior of the compact subset $K$.

Similarly, since $p_{0}$ is a smooth orbifold point of $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$, there exists a coordinate system $\left\{z^{i}\right\}, i=1 \ldots 2 n$, and a diffeomorphism
$$\tilde{\Psi}: B(0, \epsilon) \backslash\{0\} \rightarrow B\left(\widetilde{\left.p_{0}, \epsilon\right) \backslash\left\{p_{0}\right\}}\right.$$
such that
$$\tilde{\Psi}^{*} \pi^{*} g_{0}=g_{e u c}+O\left(|z|^{2}\right),$$
where $\pi: \widetilde{B\left(p_{0}, \epsilon\right)} \rightarrow B\left(p_{0}, \epsilon\right)$ is the universal covering mapping, and such that $\tilde{\Psi}^{*} \pi^{*}(g)$ extends to a smooth metric on $B(0, \epsilon)$.

Let $t>0$ be small, and consider the following regions
$$\begin{aligned}
X^{t} & =\left\{x \in X: r<2 t^{-1 / 4}\right\} \\
M_{0}^{t} & =M \backslash B\left(p_{0},(1 / 2) t^{1 / 4}\right)
\end{aligned}$$
Define a mapping
$$\phi_{t}:\left\{x \in X \mid(1 / 2) t^{-1 / 4}<r<2 t^{-1 / 4}\right\} \rightarrow\left\{m \in M_{0}\left|(1 / 2) t^{1 / 4}<|z|<2 t^{1 / 4}\right\}\right.$$
by $\phi(x)=\sqrt{t} x$. Define $M^{t}$ to be the union of $X^{t}$ and $M_{0}^{t}$ along the attaching map $\phi_{t}$.

Lemma 1.1. If $A \in \mathrm{SO}(2 n)$ satisfies $A \gamma=\gamma A$ for $\gamma$ a generator of $\Gamma_{n}$, then $A \in$ $\mathrm{U}(n)$, if $n \geq 3$.

This lemma is elementary, and the proof is omitted. It illustrates a key difference between the case $n=2$ and the higher-dimensional cases: if $n=2$, then one can modify the attaching map $\phi$ by 2 nontrivial rotational parameters, but there is no rotational freedom if $n>2$ (since the Calabi metric is $\mathrm{U}(n)$-invariant). Another consequence of this lemma is that there is a well-defined almost complex structure $J$ at $p_{0}$. The corresponding Kähler form at $p_{0}$, will be denoted by $\omega_{p_{0}}$.

An approximate Einstein metric on $M^{t}$ is defined as follows. Let $\chi$ be a smooth function satisfying $0 \leq \chi \leq 1$,
$$\chi(s)=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
1 & s \leq 1 / 2 \\
0 & s \geq 2
\end{array},\right.$$
and define $\chi_{t}(s) \equiv \chi\left(t^{\frac{1}{4}} s\right)$. Then
$$g_{t}= \begin{cases}g_{0} & M_{0} \backslash B\left(p_{0}, 2 t^{1 / 4}\right) \\ \left(1-\chi_{t}(r)\right) \phi_{t}^{*} g_{0}+\chi_{t}(r) t \cdot g_{\text {cal }} & (1 / 2) t^{-1 / 4}<r<2 t^{-1 / 4} \\ t \cdot g_{\text {cal }} & r<(1 / 2) t^{-1 / 4}\end{cases}$$
is a natural "approximate" Einstein metric on $M^{t}$.

We define a weight function on $M^{t}$ as follows.
$$w=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
1 & z \in M_{0} \backslash B\left(p_{0}, i_{g_{0}}\right) \\
|z| & \frac{1}{2} t^{\frac{1}{4}}<|z|<\frac{1}{2} i_{g_{0}} \\
r t^{\frac{1}{2}} & 2<r<2 t^{-\frac{1}{4}} \\
t^{\frac{1}{2}} & r \leq 1
\end{array},\right.$$
where $i_{g_{0}}$ is the injectivity radius of $g_{0}$, and such that $w$ is increasing in $r$ for $1<r<2$, and increasing in $|z|$ for $\frac{1}{2} i_{g_{0}}<|z|<i_{g_{0}}$.

Our first result is in the case of a compact Einstein orbifold, which is an extension of [Biq13, Theorem 1.2]. We define
$$\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle\left(p_{0}\right)=\sum_{i, j, k, l} R_{i j k l}\left(p_{0}\right)\left(\omega_{p_{0}}\right)_{i j}\left(\omega_{p_{0}}\right)_{k l},$$
and let $R\left(p_{0}\right)$ denote the scalar curvature at $p_{0}$.
Theorem 1.1. Let $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ be a compact Einstein orbifold having a singular point $p_{0}$ with orbifold group $\Gamma_{n} \subset \operatorname{SU}(n)$ with $n \geq 3$, and assume that
$$n\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle\left(p_{0}\right)+2(n-2) R\left(p_{0}\right) \neq 0 .$$
Then there exist constants $\epsilon>0$ and $\delta_{0}>0$ small, such that there does not exist any Einstein metric $g_{E}$ near $g_{t}$ satisfying
$$\left\|\nabla^{\ell}\left(g_{t}-g_{E}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}\left(g_{t}\right)}<\epsilon w^{-\delta_{0}-\ell},$$
for $0 \leq \ell \leq 3$, and sufficiently small gluing parameter $t$.
This theorem does give some new understanding of possible degenerations of Einstein metrics, but we do not obtain an existence result in the compact case because this gluing problem is obstructed, and the criterion in (1.15) is only the first obstruction. To find an Einstein metric in this manner, one would have to show the vanishing of infinitely many obstructions (both of a local and global nature). This would be difficult, if not impossible, to check in any given case. However, it is possible that this procedure could work in a specific example, an example of which is the following (and is a generalization of the well-known Kummer construction in complex dimension 2).

Example 1.2. This example is due to Peng Lu [Lu99] (and is proved using a gluing argument for Kähler-Einstein metrics). Let $\Sigma$ denote the elliptic curve with a nontrivial automorphism of order 3. Consider the 6-torus $\Sigma \times \Sigma \times \Sigma$. Let $\mathbb{Z}_{3}$ act diagonally on the product. Then $T^{6} / \mathbb{Z}^{3}$ has 27 singular points of type $\Gamma_{3}$. The flat metric on the quotient can be resolved to a Calabi-Yau metric on the resolution by attaching a Calabi metric at each of the 27 singular points. Clearly, the obstruction (1.15) is satisfied at the singular points, since the orbifold metric is flat.

Theorem 1.1 implies several non-existence results. The first "non-example" is the following.

Non-example 1.3. Let $(M, g)$ be a compact Kähler-Einstein orbifold with Einstein constant $\Lambda \neq 0$, and singular point $p_{0}$ of type $\Gamma_{n}$ and $n \geq 3$. In the Kähler-Einstein case, $\langle\mathcal{R} \omega, \omega\rangle=2 R$ (see [Bes08, page 77]), so the obstruction in (3.29) is equal to $4(n-1) R\left(p_{0}\right)$. Consequently, if $\Lambda \neq 0$, then there does not exist any Einstein metric near the approximate metric $g_{t}$, for $t$ sufficiently small. We note that it follows from the adjunction formula that there is no Kähler-Einstein metric near $g_{t}$ for $t$ sufficiently small, so the main point is that these examples cannot be desingularized by a non-Kähler Einstein metric either.

This "non-example" should be contrasted with the case of $n=2$, in which case there are known examples of sequences of positive Kähler-Einstein metrics limiting to orbifolds with Eguchi-Hanson metrics bubbling off [Tia90, OSS16]. The "explanation" is that for $n=2$, the rotational parameters come into play, and the obstruction can be made to vanish upon rotating the complex structure, but this phenomenon does not happen in higher dimensions.

Non-example 1.4. The football metric $g_{S}$ on $S^{2 n} / \Gamma$ is defined as the quotient of the round metric on $S^{2 n}$ by $\Gamma$, where $\Gamma$ acts in the above manner on the first $2 n$ coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^{2 n+1}$. Since $g_{S}$ has constant curvature equal to 1, the obstruction (1.15) does not vanish at either of the singular points. Consequently, there does not exist any Einstein metric near the approximate metric $g_{t}$, for $t$ sufficiently small.

We emphasize that Theorem 1.1 only can be used to rule out an Einstein metric near the specific approximate metric $g_{t}$ defined above. Of course, if the first obstruction in (1.15) is non-zero, it is possible that there could be some other procedure to desingularize the orbifold points which could produce an Einstein metric, but the above approximate metric seems to be the most "natural".
1.1. Asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein metrics. We next consider a class of complete, non-compact Riemannian metrics.

Definition 1.5. Let $\bar{M}=M \cup \partial M$ be a smooth, compact manifold with boundary, and let $g$ be a Riemannian metric on $M$. We say that $g$ is conformally compact if there exists a function $x \in C^{\infty}(\bar{M}, \mathbb{R})$ with $x>0$ on $M$ such that
$$\partial M=\{p \in \bar{M}: x(p)=0\},$$
and $\bar{g}=x^{2} g$ extends to a Riemannian metric on $\bar{M}$ of class $C^{2}$ with $d x \neq 0$ on $\partial M$.
A function $x$ with the above properties is called a boundary defining function. Note that $\left.\bar{g}\right|_{\partial M}$ is not well-defined since there might be another candidate for a boundary defining function $x$ but its conformal class $\left[\left.\bar{g}\right|_{\partial M}\right]$ is well-defined and we call it the conformal infinity of $g$.

Definition 1.6. Let $(M, \partial M, g, x)$ be a smooth, conformally compact manifold. We say that $(M, \partial M, g, x)$ is asymptotically hyperbolic $(A H)$ if $|d x|_{\bar{g}}=1$, where $x$ is a boundary defining function as above. If moreover $g$ is Einstein with $\operatorname{Ric}(g)=$ $-(m-1) g$, then $g$ is called asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein (AHE).

Note that the AH condition is independent of the defining function $x$. Furthermore, if $m=\operatorname{dim}(M)$ is even, the metric $\bar{g}$ extends smoothly to $\bar{M}$ [CDLS05]. For more background on the theory of AHE metrics, we refer the reader to [Biq06, GL91, Lee06, Maz91b]. We say that $g$ is non-degenerate if the space of $L^{2}$ infinitesimal Einstein deformations is trivial (see Definition 2.2).

Our main existence result is the following, which is an extension of [Biq13, Theorem 1.1] to the higher-dimensional case.

Theorem 1.2. Let $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ be an asymptotically hyberbolic Einstein orbifold having a singular point $p_{0}$ with orbifold group $\Gamma_{n} \subset \operatorname{SU}(n)$, with conformal infinity $\left[g_{\infty}\right]$ and $n \geq 3$. If $g_{0}$ is non-degenerate and
$$n\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle\left(p_{0}\right)+2(n-2) R\left(p_{0}\right)=0,$$
then there exists an AHE metric $\hat{g}_{t}$ on $M^{t}$ for all $t$ sufficiently small. The conformal infinity $\left[\hat{g}_{\infty, t}\right]$ of the Einstein metric is a small perturbation of $\left[g_{\infty}\right]$, in the sense that
$$\left\|\hat{g}_{\infty, t}-g_{\infty}\right\|_{C^{2}(\partial M)}<C t .$$
for some constant $C$.
The Einstein metric $\hat{g}_{t}$ is close, in a weighted norm, to a certain perturbation of $g_{t}$ which changes the conformal infinity. For the precise statement, see Section 9. The only "explicit" example of this phenomenon that we know of is the following.

Example 1.7. In [PP87], Page and Pope wrote down an explicit 1-parameter family of $\mathrm{U}(n)$-invariant Einstein metrics $\mathcal{O}(-n) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, see also [BB82]. In [MS07], it was pointed out by Mazzeo and Singer that at one end of the parameter space, the Page-Pope family degenerates to an Einstein orbifold, with a Ricci-flat ALE metric bubbling off (which is in fact the Calabi metric). It can be shown that the limiting orbifold is the quotient of a Page-Pope metric on $\mathbb{D}^{2 n}$, which also comes in a 1-dimensional family, and the parameter is exactly that for which the obstruction (1.18) is satisfied. Of course, Theorem 1.2 could be used to produce the Page-Pope metrics on $\mathcal{O}(-n)$, but since these metrics are already known explicitly, it is not necessary to analyze this example in more detail here.

We note that the boundary conformal class in the previous example is that of a Berger sphere. The boundary Berger sphere conformal classes in the Page-Pope family on $\mathcal{O}(-n)$ are those with smaller length of the Hopf fiber than the one for which the obstruction vanishes. The ones with longer fiber length probably do not have any AHE filling, but the proof of this would require a more detailed analysis than given here (in particular, it would require a computation of the second obstruction).

There is also a corresponding non-existence result in the AHE case, provided that the obstruction in (1.18) does not vanish. But to state this properly, we need the following. Use the boundary defining function $x$ to identify a neighborhood of the boundary, call it $U_{2 \epsilon}$ with $(0,2 \epsilon) \times \partial M_{0}$, and if necessary, we modify the defining function $x$ to a new defining function $\tilde{x}$ satisfying
$$\tilde{x}(z)= \begin{cases}x & z \in U_{2 \epsilon} \\ 2 \epsilon \leq \tilde{x} \leq 1 & z \in M_{0} \backslash\left\{U_{2 \epsilon}\right\} .\end{cases}$$
Given a boundary metric $\tilde{g}_{\infty}$ close to $g_{\infty}$, using the identification above we extend $\tilde{g}_{\infty}$ to an asymptotically hyperbolic metric on $M_{0}$ by
$$\tilde{g}=g+\chi\left(x \epsilon^{-1}\right)\left(\tilde{g}_{\infty}-g_{\infty}\right) .$$
Our non-existence result in the AHE case is the following.

Theorem 1.3. Let $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ be an asymptotically hyberbolic Einstein orbifold having a singular point $p_{0}$ with orbifold group $\Gamma_{n} \subset \operatorname{SU}(n)$, with conformal infinity $\left[g_{\infty}\right]$ and $n \geq 3$. Assume that
$$n\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle\left(p_{0}\right)+2(n-2) R\left(p_{0}\right) \neq 0 .$$
Fix $\delta_{0}>0$ and $\delta_{\infty}>0$ small. Then given $\epsilon>0$, there exists a constant $\delta>0$ such that if
$$\left\|\tilde{g}_{\infty}-g_{\infty}\right\|_{C^{2}(\partial M)}<\delta,$$
then there is no AHE metric $g_{E}$ near $\tilde{g}_{t}$ satisfying
$$\left\|\nabla^{\ell}\left(\tilde{x}^{-\delta_{\infty}}\left(\tilde{g}_{t}-g_{E}\right)\right)\right\|_{C^{0}\left(g_{t}\right)}<\epsilon w^{-\delta_{0}-\ell}$$
for $0 \leq \ell \leq 3$, and sufficiently small gluing parameter $t$.
Using this result, we have the following "non-example".
Non-example 1.8. Let $\left(\mathbb{D}^{2 n}, g_{H}\right)$ denote the hyperbolic space of real dimension $2 n$. This is an AHE metric, with conformal infinity the round sphere $S^{2 n-1}$. The group $\Gamma_{n}$ acts on $\mathbb{D}^{2 n}$, and the hyperbolic metric descends to the quotient. Since $g_{H}$ has constant curvature equal to -1, the obstruction (1.22) does not vanish at the singular point. Therefore, there exists no AHE metric near the approximate metric $\tilde{g}_{t}$, with boundary conformal class near the round metric.

The obstruction can be interpreted as defining a "wall" in the space of boundary conformal classes. That is, in the space of conformal infinities which have orbifold Einstein fillings, the ones which have the obstruction (1.18) vanishing at the orbifold point should generically define a hypersurface. It is expected that only the conformal classes on one side of this "wall" have smooth fillings. This has been completely analyzed by Biquard in the 4-dimensional case [Biq16]. However, due to space limitations, we will not go into more detail about the wall-crossing in the higher-dimensional case in this paper.

We end the introduction with an outline of the paper. In Section 2 we will recall some basic details about the Calabi metric, then we will show that the space of decaying infinitesimal Einstein deformations is one-dimensional. In Section 3 we will identify the obstruction for finding a solution of the gauged linearized Einstein equation with prescribed quadratic leading term, and also give a useful reformulation of the obstruction. In Section 4 we will define the "refined" approximate metric which is an improvement of the "naïve" approximate metric which was defined above. Then in Section 5 we will present the main Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction which yields a solution of the Einstein equation modulo the space of obstructions, which we will use to prove Theorem 1.1.

The remainder of the paper will then focus on the asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein case. In Section 6, we will give some background on the theory of AHE Einstein metrics, and in particular compute the indicial roots at infinity for several important operators. In Section 7 we will define a mapping from germs to boundary tensors,
which considers solutions which blow-up at $p_{0}$ at a certain rate with prescribed leading term. This will be used in Section 8, together with a duality argument, to prove that there is a solution of the linearized equation with a certain prescribed quadratic leading term at $p_{0}$, and which is bounded at infinity (in the interior metric). This solution will then be used in Section 9 to prove the main existence theorem, Theorem 1.2. Finally, in Section 9, the non-existence result Theorem 1.3 will be proved.

\section*{2. Infinitesimal Einstein deformations of the Calabi metric}

We begin with a brief description of the Calabi metric.
2.1. The Calabi metric. The cone $\mathbb{C}^{n} / \Gamma_{n}$ admits a crepant resolution $X$ which is the total space of the bundle $\mathcal{O}(-n) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$. In [Cal79], Calabi proved the following.

Theorem 2.1. The space $X$ admits a $\mathrm{U}(n)$-invariant Ricci-flat Kähler ALE metric denoted by $g_{\text {cal }}$.

Proof. This metric is well-known, so we just give here a formula for the Calabi metric in coordinates following [KR97, Sal89]. To do this, we need to define some 1-forms and vector fields which will also be used often later in the paper. Consider the following 1-forms
$$d r, J(d r),$$
where $d r$ is the radial form and $J$ is the standard complex structure. We denote the dual vectors (with respect to the Euclidean metric) by
$$\partial_{r}, J\left(\partial_{r}\right) .$$
Let $\theta=-J\left(\frac{d r}{r}\right)$ and $\partial_{\theta}$ denote the dual of $\theta$ (with respect to the Euclidean metric). It follows that
$$J\left(r \partial_{r}\right)=\partial_{\theta} .$$
The Calabi metric in radial coordinates is given by ${ }^{1}$
$$g_{c a l}=r^{2(n-1)}\left(1+r^{2 n}\right)^{\frac{1-n}{n}} d r^{2}+\left(1+r^{2 n}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}} g_{F S}+r^{2 n}\left(1+r^{2 n}\right)^{\frac{1-n}{n}} \theta^{2},$$
where $g_{F S}$ denotes the Fubini-Study metric on $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$. There is an apparent singularity at $r=0$, which can be resolved by replacing the origin with the $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ as the zero section of $\mathcal{O}(-n) \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, and the metric then extends smoothly to the resolution. \(\square\)

\footnotetext{
${ }^{1}$ There is a typo in [KR97] for $g_{c a l}$ in radial form.
}

We will next set some notation which will be used below. Let $\omega$ denote the FubiniStudy 2-form on $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$. Recall that on $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ we have
$$d \theta=2 \omega, d \omega=0$$
where $\theta$ is viewed as a connection form for the Hopf fibration $S^{2 n-1} \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$. Next, we define
$$A(r)=\frac{r^{n-1}}{\left(1+r^{2 n}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2 n}}}, B(r)=\left(1+r^{2 n}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}, C(r)=\left(1+r^{2 n}\right)^{\frac{1-n}{2 n}} r^{n}$$
so that
$$\|A d r\|_{g_{c a l}}=\|C \theta\|_{g_{c a l}}=1,\|B \omega\|_{g_{c a l}}^{2}=n-1 .$$
We also define
$$d \bar{r} \equiv A d r, \bar{\theta} \equiv C \theta, \bar{\omega} \equiv B \omega, \overline{g_{F S}} \equiv B g_{F S},$$
so that the Calabi metric may be written as
$$g_{c a l}=d \bar{r} \otimes d \bar{r}+\overline{g_{F S}}+\bar{\theta} \otimes \bar{\theta} .$$
2.2. Infinitesimal Einstein deformations. In this section we prove that the space of decaying infinitesimal Einstein deformations of $\left(X, g_{\text {cal }}\right)$ is one-dimensional and we explicitly identify a generator. We first define the operators that we need. Let $\left(M^{m}, g\right)$ be a Riemannian manifold of real dimension $m$. For a 1-form $\omega$,
$$\begin{array}{ll}
\delta_{g}: \Omega^{1}(M) \rightarrow \Omega^{0}(M) & \delta_{g}(\omega)=-g^{i j} \nabla_{i} \omega_{j} \\
\delta_{g}^{*}: \Omega^{1}(M) \rightarrow S^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right) & \delta_{g}^{*}(\omega)_{i j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{i} \omega_{j}+\nabla_{j} \omega_{i}\right) \\
\mathcal{K}_{g}: \Omega^{1}(M) \rightarrow S_{0}^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right) & \mathcal{K}_{g}(\omega)=\delta_{g}^{*} \omega+\frac{1}{m} \delta_{g}(\omega) g .
\end{array}$$
For a symmetric 2-tensor $h$,
$$\begin{array}{ll}
\delta_{g}: S^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right) \rightarrow \Omega^{1}(M) & \left(\delta_{g}(h)\right)_{i}=-g^{j k} \nabla_{j} h_{k i} \\
B_{g}: S^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right) \rightarrow \Omega^{1}(M) & B_{g}(h)=\delta_{g}(h)+\frac{1}{2} d\left(t r_{g} h\right) \\
P_{g}: S^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right) \rightarrow S^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right) & P_{g}(h)=\frac{1}{2} \nabla^{*} \nabla h-R(h),
\end{array}$$
where $\nabla^{*}$ is the formal $L^{2}$-adjoint of ∇ , and $\stackrel{\circ}{R}$ is the action of curvature tensor on symmetric two tensors defined by
$$\stackrel{\circ}{R}(h)_{i j}=g^{k p} g^{l q} R_{i k j l} h_{p q} .$$
We note that the operator $\nabla^{*} \nabla=-\Delta$, is the negative rough Laplacian of $h$. For simplicity of notation we will often omit the metric $g$ when it is clear from context. We also recall the following definition from [Bes08].

Definition 2.2. An infinitesimal Einstein deformation of an Einstein metric $g$ is a symmetric 2-tensor field $h$ such that
$$\delta_{g} h=0, P_{g}(h)=0, \operatorname{tr}_{g}(h)=0 .$$

We say that a tensor $h$ is decaying if $h=O\left(r^{-\epsilon}\right)$ as $r \rightarrow \infty$ for some $\epsilon>0$.

Theorem 2.1. The space of decaying infinitesimal Einstein deformations of the Calabi metric is one-dimensional, and is spanned by the element
$$o=\frac{1}{1+r^{2 n}}\left(-\overline{g_{F S}}+(n-1) d \bar{r} \otimes d \bar{r}+(n-1) \bar{\theta} \otimes \bar{\theta}\right) .$$

To prove this, we will first characterize the decaying harmonic (1,1)-forms on the Calabi metric, which will then be used to characterize the decaying infinitesimal Einstein deformations. Recall that $n$ denotes the complex dimension, while $m=2 n$ denotes the real dimension.

Proposition 2.3. On $\left(X, g_{\text {cal }}\right)$, the space of decaying harmonic $(1,1)$-forms is onedimensional and is spanned by the element
$$\Omega=\frac{1-n}{1+r^{2 n}} d \bar{r} \wedge \bar{\theta}+\frac{1}{1+r^{2 n}} \bar{\omega}$$
Proof. Define
$$\mathcal{H}^{p, q}=\left\{\eta \in C^{\infty}\left(\Lambda^{p, q} X\right) \mid \eta=O\left(r^{1-2 n}\right), d \eta=0, \delta \eta=0\right\}$$
and let $H^{p, q}(X)$ denote the image of $\mathcal{H}^{p, q}(X)$ in $H^{p+q}(X, \mathbb{C})$ under the natural mapping $\eta \mapsto[\eta]$. By [Joy00, Theorem 8.4.2], the decomposition
$$H^{2}(X, \mathbb{C})=H^{2,0} \oplus H^{1,1} \oplus H^{0,2}$$
holds, which implies that
$$\operatorname{dim}\left(H^{1,1}\right)=1 .$$
By a standard argument (see for example [HV16, Section 3]), if $\eta \in C^{\infty}\left(\Lambda^{p, q} X\right)$ satisfies $\eta=O\left(r^{-\epsilon}\right)$ for some $\epsilon>0$, then $\eta=O\left(r^{-2 n+1}\right)$ as $r \rightarrow \infty$.

The remainder of the proof is therefore to show that the form in (2.13) is both closed and co-closed, since it is obviously a (1, 1)-form. Using (2.5) it is easy to see that $\Omega$ is closed. To prove co-closedness, we recall that for a Kähler manifold $\left(M^{n}, g_{0}, J_{0}, \omega_{0}\right)$ ( $n$ is complex dimension) we have the following ( see [MK06])
$$d V_{g_{0}}=\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!} .$$
Using (2.17) it follows that
$$\begin{aligned}
* \omega & =\frac{1}{(n-2)!} B^{n-3} \omega^{n-2} \wedge d \bar{r} \wedge \bar{\theta} \\
d V_{g} & =\frac{B^{n-1} \omega^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} \wedge d \bar{r} \wedge \bar{\theta}
\end{aligned}$$
Now straightforward calculation using (2.5) and (2.18) shows that
$$\delta \Omega=-* d * \Omega=0 .$$
Finally, it is easy to check that this form extends across the zero section to define a smooth form on $X$. \(\square\)

Next, we complete the proof of Theorem 2.1. If $h$ is an infinitesimal Einstein deformation, then write
$$h=h_{+}+h_{-},$$
where $h_{+}$is the $J$-invariant part, and $h_{-}$is the $J$-anti-invariant part. It is proved in [Bes08] that
$$\begin{array}{r}
\Delta \Omega=0, \\
\Delta I=0,
\end{array}$$
where $\Omega$ is the (1, 1)-form associated to $h_{+}$, and $I$ is the section of $\Lambda^{0,1} \otimes T^{1,0}$ associated to $h_{-}$. Since the canonical bundle is trivial, the conjugate Hodge star operator applied to $I$ is a harmonic $(1, n-1)$-form. By [Joy00, Theorem 8.4.2], this implies that $h_{-} \equiv 0$.

By Proposition 2.3, the space of decaying infinitesimal Einstein deformations is spanned by the symmetric 2-tensor
$$o(., .)=\Omega(I(.), .) \text {. }$$
Noting that $I\left(\overline{\partial_{r}}\right)=\overline{\partial_{\theta}}$, where $\overline{\partial_{\theta}}, \overline{\partial_{r}}$ are the duals of $\bar{\theta}, d \bar{r}$ with respect to the Calabi metric, we obtain
$$o\left(\overline{\partial_{\theta}}, \overline{\partial_{\theta}}\right)=o\left(\overline{\partial_{r}}, \overline{\partial_{r}}\right)=\frac{n-1}{1+r^{2 n}} .$$
Also recall that $\omega$ is the Fubini-Study form, and that $I$ agrees with standard complex structure on $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, so
$$\omega(., .)=g_{F S}(I(.), .) \Longrightarrow \omega(I(.), .)=-g_{F S}(., .) \Longrightarrow \bar{\omega}(I(.), .)=-\overline{g_{F S}}(., .)$$
(for $\bar{\omega}$ we have $\langle\bar{\omega}, \bar{\omega}\rangle_{\text {cal }}=n-1$ ) and $o$ becomes
$$o=\frac{1}{1+r^{2 n}}\left(-\bar{g}_{F S}+(n-1) d \bar{r} \otimes d \bar{r}+(n-1) \bar{\theta} \otimes \bar{\theta}\right),$$
which is clearly traceless. Now observe that we can rewrite $o$ as
$$o=\frac{1}{1+r^{2 n}}\left(-g_{c a l}+n(d \bar{r} \otimes d \bar{r})+n(\bar{\theta} \otimes \bar{\theta})\right) .$$
Also since $\Omega$ is divergence-free ( $\Omega$ is co-closed) and $I$ is parallel, it follows that $o$ is divergence-free, so $o$ is both traceless and divergence-free, as required. This completes the proof of Theorem 2.1.

We end this section with a slightly modified statement of Theorem 2.1.
Theorem 2.2. On $\left(X, g_{\text {cal }}\right)$, the space of decaying solutions of
$$\begin{aligned}
P_{g} h & =0 \\
t r_{g} h & =0,
\end{aligned}$$
is one-dimensional, and is spanned by the element $o$.

Proof. The only thing we need to prove is that a solution of $P_{\text {cal }} h=0$ which is decaying must also be divergence-free. To see this, since Calabi metric is Ricci-flat we get the following for linearized operator
$$d_{\mathrm{cal}} R i c(h)=\frac{1}{2} \nabla_{\mathrm{cal}}^{*} \nabla_{\mathrm{cal}}(h)-\stackrel{\circ}{R}(h)-\delta_{\mathrm{cal}}^{*} B_{\mathrm{cal}}(h) .$$
Applying the divergence operator to the equation $P_{\text {cal }} h=0$ yields the equation
$$P_{1} \delta_{g} h=0,$$
where $P_{1} \equiv B \delta^{*}$. Since $g_{\text {cal }}$ is Ricci-flat, this implies that
$$\Delta_{H}\left(\delta_{g} h\right)=0,$$
where $\Delta_{H}$ is the Hodge Laplacian. By [Joy00, Theorem 8.4.1], there are no decaying harmonic 1-forms on $X$, which implies that $\delta_{g} h=0$. \(\square\)

\section*{3. Linearized equation on the Calabi metric}

In this section we apply a technique in [Biq13] to find the obstruction for solving linearized equation with prescribed leading quadratic term. Let
$$H=H_{i j k l} x^{i} x^{j} d x^{k} \otimes d x^{l}$$
be a given quadratic tensor defined on $\mathbb{R}^{2 n}$ which is symmetric in the first two and last two indices. We need the following basic lemma.

Lemma 3.1. Let $v$ be a symmetric 2-tensor with $\|v\|_{g_{\text {cal }}}=O\left(r^{\delta}\right)$ for some $\delta \in$ $(2-2 n, 0)$. Then there exists $u$ with $\|u\|_{g_{\text {cal }}}=O\left(r^{2+\delta}\right)$ and $P_{g_{\text {cal }}}(u)=v$ if and only if
$$\langle v, o\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}=0 .$$
Proof. This follows from standard theory of elliptic operators on weighted spaces (see [Bar86] for example). \(\square\)

Next, we have the following proposition.
Proposition 3.2. Given $(H, \Lambda)$ with $H$ a $\Gamma_{n}$-invariant symmetric 2-tensor on $\mathbb{R}^{2 n}$ satisfying
$$\begin{aligned}
-\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{e u c} H & =\Lambda g_{e u c} \\
B_{e u c} H & =0
\end{aligned}$$
there exists a solution $(h, \lambda)$ to
$$\begin{aligned}
P_{g_{c a l}}(h) & =\Lambda g_{c a l}+\lambda o \\
h & =H+O\left(r^{-2 n+2+\epsilon}\right) \\
B_{g_{c a l}}(h) & =0,
\end{aligned}$$
if and only if
$$\lambda=-\frac{1}{\|o\|_{L^{2}}^{2}} \lim _{r \rightarrow \infty} \int_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} \frac{n+1}{r}\langle H, o\rangle d S_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} .$$

Proof. Let $(h, \lambda)$ be a solution to (3.5). Let $R$ be a fixed positive real number and consider the following cut-off function
$$\chi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
1 & |x|>2 R \\
0 & |x|<R
\end{array} .\right.$$
Let $h^{\prime}=h-\chi H$, then $h^{\prime}=O\left(r^{-2 n+2+\epsilon}\right)$. We have
$$P_{g_{c a l}}\left(h^{\prime}\right)=-P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H)+\Lambda g_{c a l}+\lambda o .$$
Next, we claim that $\Lambda g_{\text {cal }}-P_{g_{\text {cal }}}(\chi H)=O\left(r^{-2 n}\right)$. To see this,
$$P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H)=-\frac{1}{2} \Delta(\chi H)-\stackrel{\circ}{R}(H)=\Lambda g_{e u c}+O\left(r^{-2 n}\right),$$
where we used that $H_{i i k l}=\Lambda \delta_{k l}$. Using the fact that $o$ is traceless,
$$\left\langle P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H)-\Lambda g_{c a l}, o\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H), o\right\rangle .$$
Integrating by parts yields
$$\int_{r \leq r_{0}}\left\langle P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H), o\right\rangle d V_{g_{c a l}}=\frac{1}{2} \int_{r=r_{0}}\left(\left\langle\chi H, \nabla_{\vec{n}} o\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla_{\vec{n}}(\chi H), o\right\rangle\right) d S_{S_{r_{0} / \Gamma_{n}}} .$$
Notice that we have the following asymptotic relations
$$\begin{aligned}
& \nabla_{\vec{n}} O=\frac{-2 n}{r} o+O\left(r^{-2 n-2}\right) \\
& \nabla_{\vec{n}}(\chi H)=\frac{2}{r} H+O(1),
\end{aligned}$$
as $r \rightarrow \infty$, so taking the limit as $r_{0} \rightarrow \infty$ we obtain
$$\int_{X}\left\langle P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H), o\right\rangle d V_{g_{c a l}}=-\lim _{r \rightarrow \infty} \int_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} \frac{n+1}{r}\langle H, o\rangle d S_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} .$$
Now applying Lemma 3.1 we should have
$$\left\langle-P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H)+\Lambda g_{c a l}+\lambda o, o\right\rangle=0$$
which implies
$$\lambda=-\frac{1}{\|o\|_{L^{2}}^{2}} \lim _{r \rightarrow \infty} \int_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} \frac{n+1}{r}\langle H, o\rangle d S_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} .$$
Conversely, assume that (3.6) is satisfied. This implies that (3.15) is satisfied and by Lemma 3.1 there exist a solution $h^{\prime}$ for the following problem:
$$-P_{g_{c a l}}(\chi H)+\Lambda g_{c a l}+\lambda o=P\left(h^{\prime}\right) .$$
So $h=h^{\prime}+\chi H$ is the desired solution. Next, we show that $h$ is in Bianchi gauge. Since the Calabi metric is Ricci-flat, we have
$$0=B_{g_{c a l}} P_{g_{c a l}} h=\frac{1}{2} \nabla^{*} \nabla B_{g_{c a l}} h .$$
Also,
$$h=H+O\left(r^{-2 n+2+\epsilon}\right) \Longrightarrow B_{g_{c a l}} h=O\left(r^{-2 n+1+\epsilon}\right) .$$

Let $\omega=B_{g_{\text {cal }}} h$. Then integrating by parts implies
$$0=\int_{B_{r} / \Gamma_{n}}\langle\omega, \Delta \omega\rangle d V_{g_{c a l}}=\int_{B_{r} / \Gamma_{n}}|\nabla \omega|^{2} d V_{g_{c a l}}-\int_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}}\left\langle\omega, \nabla_{\vec{n}} \omega\right\rangle d S$$
and taking the limit as $r \rightarrow \infty$ shows that $\nabla \omega=0$ and the latter implies that $\omega=0$. \(\square\)
3.1. Reformulation of the obstruction. We begin with some integral identities.

Lemma 3.3. On $\mathbb{R}^{2 n}$ with coordinates $\left(x^{1}, \ldots, x^{2 n}\right)$ we have the following:
$$\begin{aligned}
\int_{S^{2 n-1}} x^{i} x^{j} d S & =\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{2 n} \delta_{i j} \\
\int_{S^{2 n-1}} x^{i} x^{j} x^{k} x^{l} d S & =\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{2 n(2 n+2)}\left(\delta_{k l} \delta_{i j}+\delta_{k i} \delta_{l j}+\delta_{k j} \delta_{i l}\right)
\end{aligned}$$
Proof. This is proved, for example, in [Bre08]. \(\square\)

Let $g$ be a Riemannian metric on $\mathbb{R}^{2 n}$. The Taylor expansion of the metric in normal coordinates has the form
$$g_{k l}(z)=\delta_{k l}-\frac{1}{3} R_{i k j l} z^{i} z^{j}+O\left(|z|^{3}\right)$$
as $|z| \rightarrow 0$. The quadratic term is not in Bianchi gauge, so we use the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4. Assume that $g_{0}$ is Einstein of dimension $2 n$, with $\operatorname{Ric}\left(g_{0}\right)=\Lambda g_{0}$. Then there exists a coordinate system near the origin such that
$$g_{k l}(z)=\delta_{k l}(0)+H_{i j k l} z^{i} z^{j}+O\left(|z|^{3}\right),$$
as $|x| \rightarrow 0$, where
$$H_{i j k l} z^{i} z^{j}=-\frac{1}{3} R_{i k j l} z^{i} z^{j}-\frac{\Lambda}{3(n+1)}\left(\delta_{k l}|z|^{2}+2 z^{k} z^{l}\right) .$$
Proof. It is easy to verify that
$$B_{e u c}\left(H_{i j k l} z^{i} z^{j}\right)=0 .$$
Finally, we note that
$$\delta_{e u c}^{*}\left(|z|^{2} z^{i}\right)=\delta_{i j}|z|^{2}+2 z^{i} z^{j},$$
so the added term is a Lie derivative of a cubic vector field. A standard argument, see for example [AV12, Section 7], implies that there is a coordinate system with the stated property. \(\square\) \(\square\)

Proposition 3.5. Let $H=H_{i j k l} x^{i} x^{j} d x^{k} d x^{l}$ be the symmetric 2-tensor with
$$H_{i j k l}=-\frac{1}{3} R_{i k j l}-\frac{\Lambda}{3(n+1)}\left(\delta_{i j} \delta_{k l}+2 \delta_{i k} \delta_{j l}\right) .$$

Then the constant $\lambda$ in Proposition 3.2 is given by
$$\lambda=\frac{1}{\|o\|_{L^{2}}} \frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{n}\left(\frac{2-n}{2} \Lambda-\frac{1}{8}\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle\right) .$$
Proof. It follows from (2.27) that in ALE coordinates, we have
$$o=\frac{1}{r^{2 n}}\left(-g_{e u c}+n(d r \otimes d r)+n r^{2}(\theta \otimes \theta)\right)+O\left(r^{-2 n-1}\right)$$
as $r \rightarrow \infty$. Defining
$$o_{e u c}=\frac{1}{r^{2 n}}\left(-g_{e u c}+n(d r \otimes d r)+n r^{2}(\theta \otimes \theta)\right),$$
we have
$$\lim _{r \rightarrow \infty} \int_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}} \frac{n+1}{r}\langle H, o\rangle d S_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}}=\int_{|x|=1}(n+1)\left\langle H, o_{\text {euc }}\right\rangle d S_{S_{r} / \Gamma_{n}}$$
The inner product is
$$\left\langle H, o_{\text {euc }}\right\rangle=-\left\langle H, g_{\text {euc }}\right\rangle+n\langle H, d r \otimes d r\rangle+n\left\langle H, r^{2} \theta \otimes \theta\right\rangle .$$
We lift up to $\mathbb{R}^{2 n}$, and next we calculate each term in (3.33); the first term is
$$\left\langle H, g_{\text {euc }}\right\rangle=H_{\text {ijll }} x^{i} x^{j},$$
so by (3.21),
$$\int_{|x|=1}\left\langle H, g_{e u c}\right\rangle d S=\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{2 n} H_{i i k k}$$
Also, since $r^{2}=\left(x^{i}\right)^{2}$, we have
$$\begin{aligned}
r d r & =x^{i} d x^{i} \\
r^{2} d r \otimes d r & =x^{i} x^{j} d x^{i} d x^{j}
\end{aligned}$$
Letting $J$ denote the standard complex structure on $\mathbb{R}^{2 n}$, then $\theta=-J\left(\frac{d r}{r}\right)$, so we have
$$(r \theta) \otimes(r \theta)=x^{p} x^{q} J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} d x^{k} d x^{l} .$$
Clearly,
$$\begin{aligned}
r^{2}\langle\theta \otimes \theta, H\rangle & =x^{i} x^{j} x^{p} x^{q} J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{i j k l}, \\
r^{2}\langle d r \otimes d r, H\rangle & =x^{i} x^{j} x^{k} x^{l} H_{i j k l} .
\end{aligned}$$
Using (3.22), this implies that
$$\int_{|x|=1}\langle H, d r \otimes d r\rangle d S=\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{2 n(2 n+2)}\left(H_{i i k k}+H_{i k i k}+H_{i k k i}\right)$$
Also, (3.22) implies
$$\int_{|x|=1}\langle H, \theta \otimes \theta\rangle d S=\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{2 n(2 n+2)}\left(J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{p} H_{i i k l}+J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{p q k l}+J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{q p k l}\right) .$$

Putting together (3.35), (3.41), and (3.42), we obtain
$$\begin{aligned}
\int_{|x|=1}(n+1)\left\langle H, o_{e u c}\right\rangle d S= & -\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{2 n}(n+1) H_{k k l l}+\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{4}\left(H_{i i k k}+H_{i k i k}+H_{i k k i}\right) \\
& +\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{4}\left(J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{p} H_{i i k l}+J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{p q k l}+J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{q p k l}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
Next we compute the terms involving $H$ appearing in (3.43). First,
$$H_{k k l l}=-\frac{1}{3} R_{k l k l}-\frac{\Lambda}{3(n+1)}\left(\delta_{k k} \delta_{l l}+2 \delta_{k l} \delta_{k l}\right)=-2 n \Lambda .$$
The next two terms are
$$H_{i k i k}=-\frac{1}{3} R_{i i k k}-\frac{\Lambda}{3(n+1)}\left(\delta_{i k} \delta_{i k}+2 \delta_{i i} \delta_{k k}\right)=-\frac{\Lambda}{3(n+1)}\left(2 n+8 n^{2}\right),$$
and
$$H_{i k k i}=-\frac{1}{3} R_{i k k i}-\frac{\Lambda}{3(n+1)}\left(\delta_{i k} \delta_{i k}+2 \delta_{i k} \delta_{i k}\right)=\frac{2 n \Lambda}{3}-\frac{2 n \Lambda}{n+1} .$$
The first term involving $J$ is
$$J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{p} H_{i i k l}=-2 n \Lambda .$$
The next term is
$$J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{p q k l}=-\frac{1}{3}\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle-\frac{2 n \Lambda}{3(n+1)} .$$
For the last term, we have
$$J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{q p k l}=-\frac{1}{3} J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} R_{q k p l}+\frac{2 n \Lambda}{3(n+1)}$$
Note that using the Bianchi identity, we have
$$0=J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} R_{q k p l}-J_{p}^{k} J_{l}^{q} R_{k p q l}+J_{k}^{p} J_{q}^{l} R_{p q l k} .$$
The first and third terms in the last line of (3.50) are easily seen to be equal, thus we have
$$J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} R_{q k p l}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle .$$
Plugging (3.51) into (3.49), we obtain
$$J_{k}^{p} J_{l}^{q} H_{q p k l}=-\frac{1}{6}\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle+\frac{2 n \Lambda}{3(n+1)} .$$
We now insert (3.44), (3.45), (3.46), (3.47), (3.48), and (3.52) into (3.43) to obtain
$$\int_{|x|=1}(n+1)\langle H, o\rangle d S=\omega_{2 n-1}\left(\frac{2-n}{2} \Lambda-\frac{1}{8}\langle\mathcal{R}(\omega), \omega\rangle\right) .$$
Finally, since we are integrating over $S^{2 n-1} / \Gamma$, where $\Gamma$ is of order $n$, the area of the unit sphere is $\frac{\omega_{2 n-1}}{n}$, which accounts for the extra factor of $\frac{1}{n}$ appearing in (3.29). \(\square\)

\section*{4. The refined approximate metric}

The approximate metric described in the introduction will be referred to as the "naïve" approximate metric. In this section, we will construct a "refined" approximate metric, which will be necessary to compute the obstruction. The construction closely follows that in [Biq13] with minor modifications. Recall that the Einstein orbifold is denoted by $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$ and $\left(X, g_{\text {cal }}\right)$ denotes the Calabi metric. Also, recall that $\left\{x^{i}\right\}, i=1 \ldots 2 n$, are ALE coordinates on $X$, and that $g_{0}$ satisfies
$$\operatorname{Ric}\left(g_{0}\right)=\Lambda g_{0} .$$
Near $p_{0}$, we use the coordinate system $\left\{z^{i}\right\}, i=1 \ldots 2 n$, from Lemma 3.4, where $g_{0}$ takes the form
$$g_{0}=g_{e u c}+H+o\left(|z|^{2}\right),$$
with
$$H=H_{i j k l} z^{i} z^{j} d z^{k} d z^{l},$$
given by (3.25).
On the Calabi metric, using Proposition 3.2, there exists a solution $(h, \lambda)$ for the following problem
$$\begin{aligned}
P_{g_{c a l}} R i c(h) & =\Lambda g_{c a l}+\lambda o \\
h & =H+O\left(r^{2-2 n+\epsilon}\right), \\
B_{g_{c a l}}(h) & =0,
\end{aligned}$$
for any $\epsilon>0$, where $\lambda$ is given by (3.29), as $r \rightarrow \infty$. Define
$$h_{t}=g_{c a l}+t h .$$
Consider the Calabi metric on $\mathbb{C}^{n}-\{0\} / \Gamma_{n}$ and define the following two regions
$$\begin{aligned}
& D Z^{t} \equiv\left\{\frac{1}{2} t^{-\frac{1}{4}}<r<2 t^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right\} \\
& X^{t} \equiv\left\{r<2 t^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right\}
\end{aligned}$$
(DZ is short for damage zone). Now define the following function on $\mathbb{C}^{n}-\{0\} / \Gamma_{n}$
$$\rho= \begin{cases}r & \text { when } r>2 \\ 1 & \text { when } r \leq 1,\end{cases}$$

Lemma 4.1. For $t$ sufficiently small, $h_{t}$ is a Riemannian metric on $X^{t}$ and the following estimate holds on this region
$$\left|\nabla^{k}\left(\operatorname{Ric}_{h_{t}}-t \Lambda h_{t}-t \lambda o\right)\right|_{g_{c a l}} \leq c_{k} t^{2} \rho^{2-k},$$
for some constants $c_{k}$ and any $k \geq 0$.

Proof. Since $h$ has quadratic leading term and $g_{\text {cal }}$ is ALE, there exist a constant $c$ so that $|h|_{g_{\text {cal }}}(p) \leq c \rho^{2}(p)$ for any $p \in X^{t}$. It follows that
$$|t h|_{g_{c a l}}(p) \leq c t \rho^{2}(p) \leq 2 c t^{\frac{1}{2}} .$$
The right hand side is independent of $p$ so $h_{t}$ is a Riemannian metric on $X^{t}$ for small $t$. Recall that
$$d_{g_{c a l}} \operatorname{Ric}(h)=\Lambda g_{c a l}+\lambda o$$
so we have
$$\operatorname{Ric}\left(g_{c a l}+t h\right)=\operatorname{Ric}\left(g_{c a l}\right)+t d_{g_{c a l}} \operatorname{Ric}(h)+Q(t h),$$
where $Q$ is a nonlinear term. For any Riemannian metric $g$, and symmetric tensor $h$ small, recall the well-known expansion
$$R m_{g+h}=R m_{g}+(g+h)^{-1} * \nabla^{2} h+(g+h)^{-2} * \nabla h * \nabla h,$$
where $*$ denotes various tensor contractions (see, for example [GV16, Section 3]). Contracting again, we obtain
$$R i c_{g+h}=(g+h)^{-1} * R m_{g}+(g+h)^{-2} * \nabla^{2} h+(g+h)^{-3} * \nabla h * \nabla h .$$
It follows that for $t$ sufficiently small
$$|Q(t h)| \leq c_{1} t^{2}\left(\left|R m_{g_{c a l}}\right||h|^{2}+|h|\left|\nabla^{2} h\right|+|\nabla h|^{2}\right) .$$
Using (4.4), it follows that
$$\left|\operatorname{Ric}\left(h_{t}\right)-t \Lambda h_{t}-\lambda o\right|=c_{1} t^{2}\left(\left|R m_{g_{c a l}}\right||h|^{2}+|h|\left|\nabla^{2} h\right|+|\nabla h|^{2}\right)$$
Note that
$$\left|\nabla^{k} h\right| \leq c_{k} \rho^{2-k},$$
so the lemma for $k=0$ follows. For $k>0$ the proof is similar, and is omitted. \(\square\)

Recalling the attaching map $\phi_{t}$ defined in (1.10), consider
$$M^{t}=M_{0}^{t} \cup_{\phi_{t}} X^{t}$$
where
$$M_{0}^{t}=M \backslash B\left(p_{0},(1 / 2) t^{1 / 4}\right) .$$
To define the refined approximate metric on $M^{t}$, let $\chi$ be as in (1.11),
$$g_{t}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
g_{0} & M \backslash B\left(p_{0}, 2 t^{1 / 4}\right) \\
\left(1-\chi_{t}(r)\right) \phi_{t}^{*} g+\chi_{t}(r) t h_{t} & (1 / 2) t^{-1 / 4}<r<2 t^{-1 / 4} \\
t h_{t} & r<(1 / 2) t^{-1 / 4}
\end{array} .\right.$$
We have the following estimation.

Lemma 4.2. For each integer $k \geq 0$, there exist a constant $c_{k}$ so that $g_{t}$ satisfies the following estimate on $X^{t}$
$$\left|\nabla^{k}\left(\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{t}}-\Lambda g_{t}-t \lambda \chi_{t}(\rho) o\right)\right|_{g_{c a l}} \leq c_{k} t^{2} \rho^{2-k}$$
where the covariant derivatives and norm are taken with respect to $g_{\text {cal }}$.
Proof. If $r<\frac{1}{2} t^{-\frac{1}{4}}$ then $g_{t}=t h_{t}$ and by Lemma 4.1 we are done. If
$$(1 / 2) t^{-1 / 4}<r<2 t^{-1 / 4}$$
then $g_{t}=\left(1-\chi_{t}(r)\right) \phi_{t}^{*} g_{0}+\chi_{t}(r) t h_{t}$. Recall that
$$\begin{aligned}
& \phi_{t}^{*} g_{0}=t g_{e u c}+t^{2} H+t O\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}} r^{3}\right) \\
& t h_{t}=t g_{e u c}+t^{2} H+t O\left(r^{-2 n}\right)+t O\left(r^{2-2 n+\epsilon}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
Since the quadratic terms agree, we have the estimate
$$g_{t}=t g_{e u c}+t^{2} H+t O\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}} \rho^{3}\right) .$$
Writing
$$\theta=g_{t}-t g_{e u c},$$
expanding the Ricci tensor near $t \cdot g_{\text {euc }}$ we have
$$\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{t}}=d_{t \cdot e u c} \operatorname{Ric}(\theta)+Q_{e u c}(\theta),$$
where $Q_{\text {euc }}(\theta)$ satisfies the estimate
$$t^{2}\left|Q_{e u c}(\theta)\right| \leq c\left(|\theta|\left|\nabla^{2} \theta\right|+|\nabla \theta|^{2}\right),$$
for some constant $c$, where the norms are with respect to $g_{\text {euc }}$. Note that (4.22) implies that
$$t \lambda \chi_{t}(\rho) o=O\left(t^{2} \rho^{2}\right) .$$
From (4.4) it follows that
$$d_{t \cdot e u c} \operatorname{Ric}(\theta)=t \Lambda g_{e u c}+O\left(t^{2} \rho^{2}\right),$$
where $O$ is with respect to the Euclidean metric. Finally we obtain
$$\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{t}}-\Lambda g_{t}-t \lambda \chi_{t}(\rho) o=O\left(t^{2} \rho^{2}\right),$$
so the lemma for $k=0$ follows. For general $k$ the proof is similar and is omitted. \(\square\)

\section*{5. Solution of the Einstein equation modulo obstructions}
5.1. Function Spaces. In this section we define the function spaces that we will need in order to apply the implicit function theorem. Let $E$ be a tensor bundle over $X^{t}$ and $\delta_{0} \in \mathbb{R}$ be a weight. We define $C_{\delta_{0}}^{k, \alpha}\left(X^{t}, E\right)$ to be
$$\begin{aligned}
& |s|_{\alpha} \equiv \sup _{d(x, y)<i_{g_{c a l}}} \frac{|s(x)-s(y)|}{d(x, y)^{\alpha}} \\
& \|s\|_{C_{\delta_{0}}^{k, \alpha}} \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\sup \rho^{\delta_{0}+i}\left|\nabla^{i} s\right|\right)+\left|\rho^{\delta_{0}+k+\alpha} \nabla^{k} s\right|_{\alpha} .
\end{aligned}$$
Note $s(x)$ and $s(y)$ in (5.1) are compared by means of parallel transport. Let $i_{p_{0}}$ denote the injectivity radius at the point $p_{0}$. Define the following function
$$\tilde{z}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
|z| & \text { for }|z|<\frac{1}{2} i_{p_{0}} \\
1 & \text { on } M_{0}-B\left(p_{0}, z_{p_{0}}\right)
\end{array} .\right.$$
We can define a similar norm $C_{\delta_{0}}^{k, \alpha}\left(M_{0}, E\right)$ for tensor bundles over $M_{0}$
$$\|s\|_{C_{\delta_{0}}^{k, \alpha}} \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\sup \tilde{z}^{\delta_{0}+i}\left|\nabla^{i} s\right|\right)+\left|\tilde{z}^{\delta_{0}+k+\alpha} \nabla^{k} s\right|_{\alpha} .$$
When $\left(M_{0}, \partial M_{0}, g_{0}, x\right)$ is AH, for another weight $\delta_{\infty} \in \mathbb{R}$, we define $C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty}}^{k, \alpha}\left(M_{0}, E\right)$ by the norm
$$\|s\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty}^{k, \alpha}} \equiv\left\|\tilde{x}^{-\delta_{\infty}} s\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}}^{k, \alpha}}$$
where $\tilde{x}$ is defined in (1.20). We also need to define function spaces $C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{k, \alpha}$ on the topological desingularization $M^{t}$. Consider the following norms
$$\begin{aligned}
\|s\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty ; t}^{k, \alpha}} & \equiv t^{\frac{\delta_{0}+l}{2}}\left\|\chi_{t} s\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}}^{k, \alpha}\left(X^{t}\right)}+\left\|\left(1-\chi_{t}\right) s\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty}}^{k, \alpha}\left(M_{0}^{t}\right)} \\
\|s\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty ; t}^{k, \alpha}}^{\prime} & \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\sup w^{\delta_{0}+i}\left|\nabla^{i}\left(\tilde{x}^{-\delta_{\infty}} s\right)\right|+\left|w^{\delta_{0}+k+\alpha} \nabla^{k}\left(\tilde{x}^{-\delta_{\infty}} s\right)\right|_{\alpha},\right.
\end{aligned}$$
where $l=r-s$ for an ( $r, s$ ) tensor $E$ and $w$ is the weight function and the covariant derivatives are with respect to $g_{t}$ in last expression. It is easy to see that these norms are uniformly equivalent when $t$ is sufficiently small, so we will use them interchangeably, and omit the prime notation. The exponent of $t$ in (5.6) follows because
$$t^{\frac{l}{2}}|s|_{\frac{g}{t}}=|s|_{g}$$
and since $\rho$ is comparable to $\frac{\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{t}}$ in the damage zone.
In the following, we fix $\delta_{0}>0$ and $\delta_{\infty}>0$ sufficiently small.
5.2. Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction. Define the space of obstructions
$$\mathcal{O}_{t} \subset C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{\alpha}\left(M^{t}\right)$$
to be the one-dimensional span of $\chi(t) o$. Define the following operator
$$\Phi_{g_{t}}(g)=\operatorname{Ric}(g)-\Lambda g+\delta_{g}^{*} B_{g_{t}} g .$$
The essential ingredient to the gluing argument is the following implicit function theorem [Biq13].

Lemma 5.1. Let $\Phi: E \rightarrow F$ be a differentiable map between Banach spaces and
$$Q=\Phi-\Phi(0)-d_{0} \Phi .$$
Assume there exist positive constants $q, r_{0}, c$ such that
$$\begin{aligned}
& \|Q(x)-Q(y)\| \leq q\|x-y\|(\|x\|+\|y\|) x, y \in B\left(0, r_{0}\right) \\
& d_{0} \Phi \text { is an isomorphism and }\left\|\left(d_{0} \Phi\right)^{-1}\right\| \leq c .
\end{aligned}$$
If $r \leq \min \left(r_{0}, \frac{1}{2 q c}\right)$ and $\|\Phi(0)\| \leq \frac{r}{2 c}$ then $\Phi(x)=0$ has a unique solution in $B(0, r)$.
Let $v \in \mathbb{R}$ and consider the following metric on $M^{t}$
$$g_{t, v}=g_{t}+t v \chi_{t} o .$$
Notice that
$$\left\|t \chi_{t} o\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty ; t}^{2, \alpha}}=O\left(t^{\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\right) .$$

Proposition 5.2. Assume ( $M_{0}, g_{0}$ ) is a non-degenerate Einstein orbifold with one singular point $p_{0}$ with orbifold group $\Gamma_{n}$ ( $M_{0}$ can either be compact or AHE). Then there exist $\eta, \epsilon>0$ such that for any $t<\eta$ and $v \in \mathbb{R}$ with $|v| \leq \epsilon$, there exist a unique metric $\hat{g}_{t, v}$ solving the following equation
$$\Phi\left(\hat{g}_{t, v}\right) \in \mathcal{O}_{t},$$
where $\hat{g}_{t, v}$ satisfies the following
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left\|\hat{g}_{t, v}-g_{t}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty}^{2, \alpha}} \leq \epsilon t^{\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}} \\
& \int_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\left\langle\frac{1}{t}\left(\hat{g}_{t, v}-g_{t, v}\right), o\right\rangle_{c a l} d V_{c a l}=v
\end{aligned}$$
Also, if we write $\Phi\left(\hat{g}_{t, v}\right)=\lambda(t)\left(\chi_{t} o\right)$, then we have the following estimation
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left\|\hat{g}_{t, v}-g_{t, v}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty ; t}}^{2, \alpha}} \leq c t^{1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{4}} \\
& \lambda(t)=t \lambda+O\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}-\delta}\right)
\end{aligned}$$
for any $\delta>0$ as $t \rightarrow 0$.

Proof. The following proof is very similar to [Biq13, Proposition 9.1]. However, since scalings of various terms are different in higher dimensions, we give a fairly detailed outline here, omitting some computations. To begin, define the following operator
$$\begin{aligned}
& \Phi: t^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}} \tilde{C}_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{2, \alpha} \oplus \mathbb{R} \rightarrow t^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}} C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{\alpha} \\
& \Phi(h, x)=\text { Ric }_{g_{t, v, h}}-\Lambda g_{t, v, h}+\delta_{g_{t, v, h}}^{*} B_{g_{t}} g_{t, v, h}-x \chi_{t} o
\end{aligned}$$
where $g_{t, v, h}=g_{t, v}+h$ and $\tilde{C}_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{2, \alpha}$ is space of all $C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{2, \alpha}$ tensors like $h$ which satisfies:
$$\int_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\left\langle\frac{1}{t}\left(h-g_{t}\right), o\right\rangle_{c a l} d V_{c a l}=v .$$
For simplicity of notation, in the remainder of the proof we will omit the variable $v$. We want to apply Lemma 5.1 for $E=t^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}} \tilde{C}_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{2, \alpha} \oplus \mathbb{R}$ and $F=t^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}} C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{\alpha}$. From Lemma 4.1 it follows that
$$\|\Phi(0,0)\|_{C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{0, \alpha}}=O\left(t^{1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{4}}\right) .$$
The linearization of $\Phi$ at $(0,0)$ is given by
$$d_{(0,0)} \Phi(h, x)=P_{t, v} h-x \chi_{t} o+\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{Ric}_{g_{t, v}} \circ h+h \circ \operatorname{Ric}_{g_{t, v}}-2 \Lambda h\right) .$$
Also the quadratic terms are given by
$$Q(h, x)=\Phi(h, x)-\Phi(0,0)-d_{(0,0)} \Phi(h, x) .$$
Using the expansion (4.14), and a similar expansion for the gauge term (see [GV16, Section 3]), it is not hard to see that there exist constants $r_{0}$ and $q$ so that (5.12) is satisfied. We need to show that $d_{(0,0)} \Phi$ is an isomorphism with bounded inverse. Equivalently, we need to show that there exists a constant $c>0$ so that
$$t^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\|h\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty ; t}^{2, \alpha}}+|x| \leq c t^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\left\|d_{(0,0)} \Phi\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t}^{0, \alpha}} .$$
Assume this is not true, then there exist a sequence $t_{i} \rightarrow 0$ and $\left(h_{i}, x_{i}\right)$ such that
$$t_{i}^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\left\|h_{i}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t_{i}}^{2, \alpha}}+\left|x_{i}\right|=1,$$
but
$$t_{i}^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\left\|d_{(0,0)} \Phi\left(h_{i}, x_{i}\right)\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t_{i}}^{0, \alpha}} \rightarrow 0$$
Let us first consider that case that $x_{i} \rightarrow 0$. Then without loss of generality we may assume that
$$t_{i}^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\left\|h_{i}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty} ; t_{i}}^{0}} \rightarrow 1,$$
and
$$t_{i}^{-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\left\|d_{(0,0)} \Phi\left(h_{i}, x_{i}\right)\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty} ; t_{i}}^{0, \alpha}} \rightarrow 0$$

To get a contradiction, we briefly outline the standard blow-up argument. Let $p_{i} \in M_{t}$ be a sequence of points such that norm is attained. By passing to a subsequence we can assume that $p_{i} \rightarrow p$. There are three cases for $p$.
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] If $p \in M_{0}$ then by standard elliptic theory we can obtain a limit $h_{\infty}$ satisfying $\left\|h_{\infty}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta_{\infty}}^{0}}=1$ and $P_{g_{0}} h_{\infty}=0$. But this is impossible because we assumed that $M_{0}$ is non-degenerate.
\item[-] If $p \in X$ then we can obtain a limit $h_{\infty}$ with $\left\|h_{\infty}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}}^{0}}=1$ and $P_{g_{\text {cal }}} h_{\infty}=0$. From Theorem 2.2, $h_{\infty}=c \cdot o$, for some constant $c$. But then (5.18) implies that $c=0$, a contradiction.
\item[-] If $p \in \mathbb{C}^{n} / \Gamma_{n}$ then we can obtain a limit $h_{\infty}$ with $\left\|h_{\infty}\right\|_{C_{\delta_{0}}^{0}}=1$ and $\nabla^{*} \nabla h_{\infty}=$ 0 , but this is impossible since the indicial roots are the integers and $\delta_{0}$ was chosen small but non-zero.
\end{itemize}
Next, we show that $x_{i} \rightarrow 0$. To prove this, we need the following estimates,
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left|\left\langle u, \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}\right| \leq C t^{n-2-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\|u\|_{C_{\delta_{0}+2, \delta_{\infty ; t}}^{0, \alpha}}, \\
& \left|\left\langle P_{t} h, \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}\right| \leq C t^{n-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\delta_{0}}{4}}\|h\|_{C_{\delta_{0}, \delta \infty ; t}^{2, \alpha}}, \\
& \left\langle\chi_{t} o, \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}=t^{n-2}\left(\langle o, o\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}+O\left(t^{\frac{n}{2}}\right)\right),
\end{aligned}$$
for some constant $C>0$. The proofs of these estimates are almost exactly the same as those of [Biq13, (71)-(73)] and are omitted. Using (5.28), (5.33), and (5.34), we can estimate
$$\left\langle d_{(0,0)} \Phi\left(h_{i}, x_{i}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}=x_{i} t_{i}^{n-2}\langle o, o\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}+o\left(t_{i}^{n-2}\right),$$
as $i \rightarrow \infty$. However, using (5.29) and (5.32), we also have
$$\left\langle d_{(0,0)} \Phi\left(h_{i}, x_{i}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}=o\left(t_{i}^{n-2}\right),$$
as $i \rightarrow \infty$. It follows that
$$x_{i} t_{i}^{n-2}\langle o, o\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}=o\left(t_{i}^{n-2}\right),$$
as $i \rightarrow \infty$. Consequently, we must have $x_{i} \rightarrow 0$ as $i \rightarrow \infty$.
It remains to prove the expansion for $\lambda(t)$. From Lemma 5.1, for each $t$ sufficiently small, we obtain a solution $\left(h_{t}, x_{t}\right)$ of $\Phi\left(h_{t}, x_{t}\right)=0$. Defining $\hat{g}_{t}=g_{t}+h_{t}$, we have $\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(\hat{g}_{t}\right)=\lambda(t)\left(\chi_{t} o\right)$. Notice that
$$\left\langle\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(g_{t}+h_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}=\lambda(t)\left\langle\chi_{t} o, \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)} .$$
On the other hand
$$\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(\hat{g}_{t}\right)=\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(g_{t}\right)+d_{g_{t}} \Phi_{g_{t}}\left(h_{t}\right)+Q_{g_{t}}\left(h_{t}\right),$$
which implies that
$$\left\langle\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(\hat{g}_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}=\left\langle\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(g_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}+\left\langle d_{g_{t}} \Phi_{g_{t}}\left(h_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)}+\left\langle Q_{g_{t}}\left(h_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)} .$$

Using (5.32)-(5.34), Lemma 4.2, and the expansion (4.14), respectively, we can estimate each of the above terms by
$$\begin{aligned}
\left\langle\Phi_{g_{t}}\left(g_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)} & =\lambda t^{n-1}\|o\|_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}^{2}+O\left(t^{n}\right), \\
\left\langle d_{g_{t}} \Phi\left(h_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)} & =O\left(t^{n-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta_{0}}{4}}\right) \\
\left\langle Q_{g_{t}}\left(h_{t}\right), \chi_{t} o\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(g_{t}\right)} & =O\left(t^{n-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}\right),
\end{aligned}$$
as $t \rightarrow 0$, and we obtain
$$\lambda(t) t^{n-2}\|o\|_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}^{2}=t^{n-2}\left(\lambda t\|o\|_{L^{2}\left(g_{c a l}\right)}^{2}+O\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\delta_{0}}{4}}\right)\right),$$
which implies
$$\lambda(t)=t \lambda+O\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\delta_{0}}{4}}\right),$$
as $t \rightarrow 0$. \(\square\)

Proof of Theorem 1.1. We first assume that $g_{0}$ is non-degenerate. Observe that the refined approximate metric is close to the naïve approximate metric in the $C_{\delta_{0} ; t}^{2, \alpha}$ weighted norm, for $t$ sufficiently small. From identical arguments as in [Biq13, Section 8], for any metric $g_{E}$ satisfying (1.16), then there exists a diffeomorphism $\varphi: M^{t} \rightarrow M^{t}$ so that $B_{g_{t}}\left(g_{t}-\varphi^{*} g_{E}\right)=0$, and $\varphi^{*} g_{E}$ remains close to $g_{t}$ in the $C_{\delta_{0} ; t}^{2, \alpha}$ weighted norm. The metric $\varphi^{*} g_{E}$ would then give a zero of the mapping $\Phi_{t}$, but then (5.20) would then imply that $\lambda=0$, a contradiction.

If $g_{0}$ is not non-degenerate, then there is a finite dimensional space of infinitesimal Einstein deformations on $\left(M_{0}, g_{0}\right)$, of dimension $k \geq 0$. The proof of Proposition 5.2 can be modified to yield a mapping
$$\psi_{t}: B_{1} \times(-\epsilon, \epsilon) \rightarrow B_{1} \times(-\epsilon, \epsilon),$$
where $B_{1}$ is a small ball in $\mathbb{R}^{k}$, whose zero set is in one-to-one correspondence with the zeroes of $\Phi_{g_{t}}$ (see also the argument in [GV16, Section 11]). An almost identical argument as in the proof of Proposition 5.2 shows that the mapping $\psi$ admits an expansion
$$\psi\left(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{k}, v\right)=\left(\psi_{1}, \ldots, \psi_{k}, t \lambda+O\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}-\delta}\right)\right),$$
as $t \rightarrow 0$, so the existence of an Einstein metric in the stated neighborhood still implies that $\lambda=0$. \(\square\)

\section*{6. Analysis on asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds}

The goal of this section is to study the asymptotic behavior of $L^{2}$-solutions of some elliptic operators on an AHE manifold ( $M^{m}, \partial M, g, x$ ). We consider the following operators:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(1)] $P_{0}=\Delta_{H}-2$ Ric, acting on functions,
\item[(2)] $P_{1}=B \delta^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla^{*} \nabla-\right.$ Ric $)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{H}-2\right.$ Ric $)$, acting on 1-forms,
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
\item[(3)] $P_{2}=\Delta_{H}-2$ Ric, acting on 2-forms,
\item[(4)] $P=\frac{1}{2} \nabla^{*} \nabla-\stackrel{\circ}{R}$, acting on traceless symmetric 2-tensors.
\end{itemize}
We also need the following decompositions, noting that we will only consider $C^{\infty}$ sections of bundles, so notations of regularity will be suppressed.

For $\left.\Omega^{1} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M}$, we have the decomposition
$$\left.\Omega^{1} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M}=\left.\left.\Omega_{t}^{1} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M} \oplus \Omega_{n}^{1} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M},$$
where $\Omega_{t}^{1}(\bar{M})$ is the space of one-forms on $\bar{M}$ which vanish on normal vectors (with respect to $\bar{g}$ ) when restricted to the boundary and $\Omega_{n}^{1}(\bar{M})$ is the space one-forms on $\bar{M}$ which vanish on tangential vectors when restricted to the boundary.

Next, for $\left.\Omega^{2} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M}$ we have the following,
$$\left.\Omega^{2} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M}=\left.\left.\Omega_{t}^{2} \bar{M}\right|_{\partial M} \oplus\left(\left.\Omega_{n}^{1}(\bar{M})\right|_{\partial M} \wedge \Omega_{t}^{1} \partial \bar{M}\right)\right|_{\partial M},$$
where $\Omega_{t}^{2}(\bar{M})$ is space of two-forms on $\bar{M}$ which satisfy $\left.\overline{\partial_{x}}\right\lrcorner \omega=0$ on the boundary, where $\overline{\partial_{x}}$ is dual of $d x$ with respect to $\bar{g}$.

For $\left.S_{0}^{2}(\bar{M})\right|_{\partial M}$ we have the following decomposition
$$\left.S_{0}^{2}(\bar{M})\right|_{\partial M}=V_{0} \oplus V_{1} \oplus V_{2},$$
where
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left.V_{0} \equiv\left\{\left.h \in S_{0}^{2}(\bar{M})\right|_{\partial M} \mid \operatorname{tr}_{\bar{g}} h=0, \overline{\partial_{x}}\right\lrcorner h=0\right\} \\
& V_{1} \equiv\left\{\left.h \in S_{0}^{2}(\bar{M})\right|_{\partial M} \mid h=v \odot d x, v \in T^{*} \partial M\right\} \\
& V_{2} \equiv\left\{\left.h \in S_{0}^{2}(\bar{M})\right|_{\partial M} \mid h \text { is a multiple of }((m-1) d x \odot d x-\bar{g})\right\}
\end{aligned}$$
where ⊙ denotes the symmetric product. Finally, let $\bar{V}_{0}, \bar{V}_{1}, \bar{V}_{2}$ be those traceless symmetric tensors on $\bar{M}$ such that they lie in $V_{0}, V_{1}, V_{2}$ when restricted to the boundary, respectively.

An important property of these operators that they are uniformly degenerate at the boundary, and there are a finite set of numbers, called indicial roots which characterize invertibility of the associated indicial operators [Lee06, Maz91a]. The following proposition gives the indicial roots of these operators, and will be used throughout the remainder of the paper. For simplicity, we state the result for solutions defined on all of $M$, but note that the same conclusions hold only assuming that the solutions are defined in a neighborhood of the boundary of $M$.

Proposition 6.1. Let ( $M^{m}, \partial M, g, x$ ) be an $A H$ manifold.
$$\delta_{0, P_{0}}^{+}, \delta_{0, P_{0}}^{-}=\frac{m-1 \pm \sqrt{(m-1)^{2}+8(m-1)}}{2} .$$
\begin{itemize}
\item[] If $P_{0}(f)=0$ for some function $f \in L^{2}(M)$ then $f=f_{\infty} x^{\delta_{0, P_{0}}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{0, P_{0}}^{+}}\right)$as $x \rightarrow 0$, where $f_{\infty}: \bar{M} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, and if $f_{\infty}=0$ on $\partial M$, then $f \equiv 0$.
\item[-] The indicial roots associated with $P_{1}$ are given by
$$\begin{aligned}
& \delta_{0, P_{1}}^{+}, \delta_{0, P_{1}}^{-}=m,-1 \\
& \delta_{1, P_{1}}^{+}, \delta_{1, P_{1}}^{-}=\frac{m-1 \pm \sqrt{(m-1)^{2}+8(m-1)}}{2}
\end{aligned}$$
where the roots $\delta_{0, P_{1}}^{ \pm}$correspond to sections of $\Omega_{t}^{1} \bar{M}$ and $\delta_{1, P_{1}}^{ \pm}$corresponds to sections $\Omega_{n}^{1} \bar{M}$. Moreover, if $\alpha \in \Omega^{1}(M)$ in is $L^{2}$ and solves $P_{1}(\alpha)=0$, then we have the following expansion as $x \rightarrow 0$
$$\alpha=\alpha_{t} x^{\delta_{0, P_{1}}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{0, P_{1}}^{+}}\right),$$
where $x \alpha_{t} \in \Omega_{t}^{1} \bar{M}$ and if $x \alpha_{t} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then
$$\alpha=\alpha_{n} x^{\delta_{1, P_{1}}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{1, P_{1}}^{+}}\right),$$
where $x \alpha_{n} \in \Omega_{n}^{1} \bar{M}$ and if $x \alpha_{n} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then $\alpha \equiv 0$.
\item[-] The indicial roots associated with $P_{2}$ are given by
$$\begin{aligned}
& \delta_{0, P_{2}}^{+}, \delta_{0, P_{2}}^{-}=\frac{m-1 \pm \sqrt{(m-1)^{2}+16}}{2} \\
& \delta_{1, P_{2}}^{+}, \delta_{1, P_{2}}^{-}=m,-1,
\end{aligned}$$
where $\delta_{0, P_{2}}^{ \pm}$corresponds to sections of $\Omega_{t}^{2} \bar{M}$ and $\delta_{1, P_{2}}^{ \pm}$corresponds to sections of $\left(\Omega_{n}^{1} \bar{M} \wedge \Omega_{t}^{1} \bar{M}\right)$. Moreover, if $\omega \in \Omega^{2}(M)$ is in $L^{2}$ and satisfies $P_{2} \omega=0$, then we have the following expansion as $x \rightarrow 0$
$$\omega=\omega_{t} x^{\delta_{0, P_{2}}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{0, P_{2}}^{+}}\right)$$
where $x^{2} \omega_{t} \in \Omega_{t}^{2} \bar{M}$ and if $x^{2} \omega_{t} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then
$$\omega=\omega_{n} x^{\delta_{1, P_{2}}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{1, P_{2}}^{+}}\right)$$
where $x^{2} \omega_{n} \in\left(\Omega_{n}^{1} \bar{M} \wedge \Omega_{t}^{1} \bar{M}\right)$ and if $x^{2} \omega_{n} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then $\omega \equiv 0$.
\item[-] The indicial roots associated with $P$ are given by
$$\begin{aligned}
& \delta_{0, P}^{+}, \delta_{0, P}^{-}=m-1,0 \\
& \delta_{1, P}^{+}, \delta_{1, P}^{-}=m,-1 \\
& \delta_{2, P}^{+}, \delta_{2, P}^{-}=\frac{m-1 \pm \sqrt{(m-1)^{2}+8(m-1)}}{2},
\end{aligned}$$
\end{itemize}
where $\delta_{0, P}^{ \pm}, \delta_{1, P}^{ \pm}, \delta_{2, P}^{ \pm}$corresponds to sections of $V_{0}, V_{1}, V_{2}$, respectively. Moreover, if $h \in S_{0}^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right)$ is in $L^{2}$ and satisfies $P h=0$, then we have the following expansion as $x \rightarrow 0$
$$h=\sigma_{0} x^{\delta_{0, P}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{0, P}^{+}}\right)$$
where $x^{2} \sigma_{0} \in \bar{V}_{0}$ and if $x^{2} \sigma_{0} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then
$$h=\sigma_{1} x^{\delta_{1, P}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{1, P}^{+}}\right)$$
where $x^{2} \sigma_{1} \in \bar{V}_{1}$ and if $x^{2} \sigma_{1} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then
$$h=\sigma_{2} x^{\delta_{2, P}^{+}}+o\left(x^{\delta_{2, P}^{+}}\right)$$
where $x^{2} \sigma_{2} \in \bar{V}_{2}$ and if $x^{2} \sigma_{2} \equiv 0$ on $\partial M$ then $h \equiv 0$.
Proof. The proof of existence of an expansion around infinity for uniformly degenerate elliptic operators can be found in [Maz88, Maz91a]. The vanishing results follow from a unique continuation principle due to Mazzeo [Maz91b, Maz91a]. The calculation of indicial roots can be found in [GL91, Lee06, Maz88]. \(\square\)

The reader will notice that there are several coincidences between the indicial roots in Proposition 6.1. This is not an accident, due the the following relations between the various operators (which also contains some formulas which will be useful later).

Lemma 6.2. Let $\left(M^{m}, g\right)$ be an Einstein manifold such that Ric $_{g}=\Lambda g$ then we have the following relations for $f: M \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, \omega \in \Omega^{1}(M)$, and $h \in \Gamma\left(S^{2}\left(T^{*} M\right)\right)$.

Proof. The proof is elementary, and is omitted. \(\square\)

\section*{7. From germs to boundary tensors}

To complete the proof of Theorem 1.2, we will need to find global solutions of $P h=0$ on $M$ with prescribed leading order quadratic asymptotics at the point $p_{0}$, and such that $h$ is bounded (in the interior metric) on approach to conformal infinity. The argument is based on the four-dimensional case considered by Biquard in [Biq13], but there are some key differences. We have the following definition.

Definition 7.1. Let $\left(M^{m}, \partial M, g, x\right)$ be an AH orbifold with one singular point $p_{0}$. Let $F_{k}$ denote the space of harmonic polynomials of degree $k$, in normal coordinates at $p_{0}$. We refer to elements of $F_{k} \otimes S_{0}^{2}\left(T_{p_{0}}^{*} M\right)$ as $k$-germs at $p_{0}$.

We next need a few basic lemmas.

Lemma 7.2. Let $\left(M^{m}, \partial M, x, g\right)$ be an $A H$ manifold and let $h$ be a symmetric two tensor defined in neighborhood of the boundary. Then there exist a one-form $\alpha$ defined in neighborhood of the boundary such that $s=h+\delta^{*} \alpha$ is in radial gauge (i.e. $\left.\partial_{x}\right\lrcorner s=$ 0 ). Furthermore, if $|h|_{g}=o\left(x^{m-1}\right)$, then $\alpha$ can be chosen to satisfy $|\alpha|_{g}=o\left(x^{m-1}\right)$ as $x \rightarrow 0$.
