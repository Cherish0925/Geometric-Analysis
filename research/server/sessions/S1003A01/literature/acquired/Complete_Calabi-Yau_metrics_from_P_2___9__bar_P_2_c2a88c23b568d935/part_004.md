[Part 4/4 of: Complete Calabi-Yau metrics from P^2 # 9 \bar P^2]

This section studies uniqueness and deformation questions for hyperkähler ALH metrics on open 4-manifolds $M$ which are diffeomorphic to complements of smooth fibers $D$ in rational elliptic fibrations $f: X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$. This relies on a computation of the topological invariants of $M$ and on Hodge theory for asymptotically cylindrical manifolds, which we work out and summarize as needed in Section 5.1. Section 5.2 contains the proof of our partial uniqueness result, Theorem 1.10. The deformation space of an ALH hyperkähler metric is studied in Section 5.3, clarifying some issues that appear in applying Kovalev [46] to this particular setting.
5.1. Topology and analysis on ALH spaces. $H^{2}(X)=H^{1,1}(X)=\mathbb{R}^{10}$ and $M$ is simply-connected, hence the Mayer-Vietoris sequence
$$0 \rightarrow H^{1}\left(T^{2}\right) \rightarrow H^{1}\left(T^{3}\right) \rightarrow H^{2}(X) \rightarrow H^{2}(M) \oplus H^{2}\left(T^{2}\right) \rightarrow H^{2}\left(T^{3}\right) \rightarrow 0$$
yields $H^{2}(M)=\mathbb{R}^{11}$. More precisely, since the map $H^{2}(X) \rightarrow H^{2}(M) \oplus H^{2}\left(T^{2}\right)$ has a 9-dimensional image and 1-dimensional kernel (spanned by the Poincaré dual of a fiber, i.e. $\left.c_{1}(X)\right)$, and since the restriction $H^{2}(X) \rightarrow H^{2}(M)$ maps into $H^{1,1}(M)$, the subspace of all de Rham classes representable by closed forms which are $(1,1)$ for the complex structure on $X$, we find $\operatorname{dim} H^{1,1}(M) \geq 9$. However, for a meromorphic 2-form $\Omega$ with $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)=-D,[\operatorname{Re}(\Omega)]$ and $[\operatorname{Im}(\Omega)]$ are linearly independent and not contained in $H^{1,1}(M)$, so $H^{2}(X) \rightarrow H^{1,1}(M)$ is onto with kernel spanned by $c_{1}(X)$. In particular, all closed (1, 1)-forms on $M$ pair to zero with all 2-cycles in $M$ which are boundaries in $X$, such as the bad cycles from Definition 1.2.

The cohomology long exact sequence for $(X \backslash U, \partial U)$ for a tubular neighborhood $U$ of $D$ gives $H_{c}^{2}(M)=\mathbb{R}^{11}$ and $\operatorname{im}\left(H_{c}^{2}(M) \rightarrow H^{2}(M)\right)=\mathbb{R}^{8}$. The classes in the image are precisely the ones that can be represented by compactly supported forms. If $g$ is any ALH Riemannian metric on $M$, then the de Rham cohomology class of every $L^{2}$-harmonic 2-form with respect to $g$ is of this type, and conversely all such classes are uniquely representable by $L^{2}$-harmonic forms. (For this last fact, and for all other standard results from Hodge theory on cylindrical manifolds that appear in the following, we refer to the nice overview in Nordström [61, Sections 5.1-5.2].) If $g$ is Kähler with respect to the complex structure on $X$ and if $\operatorname{Ric}(g) \geq 0$, we see that $H^{1,1}(M)$ splits as the direct sum of $\mathbb{R}[\omega]$ and the space of $L^{2}$-harmonic forms, all of which are primitive $(1,1)$ since $\wedge \omega$ preserves harmonicity and ED harmonic (2, 0)-forms are trivial by the Bochner formula.

Back to a general ALH metric, the space of bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic $r$-forms with respect to $g$ has dimension $\operatorname{dim} H^{r}(M)+\operatorname{dim} H_{c}^{r}(M)-\operatorname{dimim}\left(H_{c}^{r}(M) \rightarrow H^{r}(M)\right)$. Outside a compact set, all such forms split into one part which is parallel for $g$ and another one which is exponentially decaying (ED) as in Definition 1.8. Therefore, the space of bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic 2-forms splits (not uniquely) as the direct sum of its 8-dimensional $L^{2}$-harmonic subspace, and a 6-dimensional subspace of forms which are asymptotic to $\alpha \wedge d t+\beta\left(\alpha \in \wedge^{1} \mathbb{R}^{3}, \beta \in \wedge^{2} \mathbb{R}^{3}\right)$ with respect to any ALH coordinate system $\Phi$ as in Definition 1.4(ii). Finally, we will need to know that $\Delta_{d}$
is Fredholm of index $-\left(b_{r}\left(T^{3}\right)+b_{r-1}\left(T^{3}\right)\right)$ when acting on ED $r$-forms with a small enough decay rate, some suitable weighted Hölder topology to be understood.

Corollary 5.1. Let $\beta$ be an ED $r$-form on $M$ with $r \in\{0,1,2\}$.
(i) If $r=0$, then for any fixed ALH coordinate system there exists a unique $\alpha=$ $C t+\mathrm{ED}$ such that $\Delta_{d} \alpha=\beta$, and moreover $C=0$ if and only if $\int \beta d \mathrm{vol}=0$.
(ii) If $r=1$, there exists a unique $\alpha=$ parallel +ED with $\Delta_{d} \alpha=\beta$. The parallel part of $\alpha$ vanishes if and only if $\int\langle\beta, \eta\rangle d \mathrm{vol}=0$ for all $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic 1-forms $\eta$ of linear growth. The space of all such harmonic 1-forms has dimension four.
(iii) If $r=2$, then a smooth and in addition ED 2-form $\alpha$ with $\Delta_{d} \alpha=\beta$ exists if and only if $\int\langle\beta, \eta\rangle d \mathrm{vol}=0$ for all bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic 2-forms $\eta$.

Proof. If $r=0$, the condition $\int \beta=0$ is clearly necessary for an ED solution $\alpha$ to exist, and thus sufficient because the index is -1 and the kernel is trivial. All $\alpha_{0}$ with $\alpha_{0}=t+\mathrm{ED}$ outside a compact set satisfy $\Delta_{d} \alpha_{0}=\mathrm{ED}$, but $\Delta_{d} \alpha_{0}=0$ implies $d \alpha_{0}$ is bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic and hence still $\alpha_{0}=0$, and so $\Delta_{d}: \mathrm{ED} \oplus \mathbb{R} \alpha_{0} \rightarrow \mathrm{ED}$ is onto. For $r=1$, note that the index on ED 1-forms is -4, and we can obviously find four linearly independent 1-forms which are parallel + ED, while, again, there are no bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic 1-forms at all. This in turn proves that we can take any 1-form which is equal to $t \xi+\mathrm{ED}$ outside a compact set for some constant $\xi$, and correct it by a bounded form so as to become $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic, providing us with a 4-dimensional space of linearly growing harmonic 1-forms, which must then define the cokernel of $\Delta_{d}$ on ED 1-forms because the index is -4. If $r=2$, the given condition is necessary, and so dim coker $\geq 14$, but also index $=-6$ and $\operatorname{dim} \operatorname{ker}=8$. \(\square\)

The key fact here is that while on a general asymptotically cylindrical manifold, the space of bounded harmonic $r$-forms mod ED is of dimension $\leq b_{r}(Y)+b_{r-1}(Y)$ if $Y$ denotes the cross-section, in this particular case, we have equality for $r=2$.
5.2. Proof of Theorem 1.10. To prove Part (i), define $\omega:=\Psi^{*} \omega_{2}-\omega_{1}$. From our assumptions, $\omega=\mathrm{ED}$ on $M_{1}$. By Corollary 5.1(ii), there exists a bounded 1-form $\alpha=$ parallel +ED such that $\Delta_{d} \alpha=d^{*} \omega$. Since $\omega$ is exact by assumption, the form $d \alpha-\omega$ is $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic, ED, and exact, and hence $d \alpha=\omega$ by the Hodge theorem. Now $\bar{\partial}^{*} \alpha^{0,1}$ is an ED function. Thus, by Corollary 5.1(i) and the Kähler identities, there exists a function $\gamma$ of at most linear growth such that $\bar{\partial}^{*} \bar{\partial} \gamma=\bar{\partial}^{*} \alpha^{0,1}$. Since $\bar{\partial} \alpha^{0,1}=0$ for type reasons, $\bar{\partial} \gamma-\alpha^{0,1}$ is a bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic 1-form, thus zero, so eventually $\omega=i \partial \bar{\partial} u$ with $u:=2 \operatorname{Im}(\gamma)$. Now $\Psi^{*} \omega_{2}^{2}=h \omega_{1}^{2}$ with $h=1+\mathrm{ED}$, but $h$ must be constant because $\omega_{1}$ and $\Psi^{*} \omega_{2}$ are globally hyperkähler and $\Psi^{*} J_{2}=J_{1}$, and hence $\Psi^{*} \omega_{2}^{2}=\omega_{1}^{2}$. With $\omega=i \partial \bar{\partial} u$, this yields $\Delta u=0$, where the Laplacian is taken with respect to the metric $g_{1}+\Psi^{*} g_{2}$. This metric is still ALH, so $u=$ const from Section 4.1, and thus $\omega=0$ as desired.

Part (ii) is completely elementary. There exist $g_{\text {flat }}$-parallel and $g_{\text {flat }}$-orthogonal almost-complex structures $J_{i, 0}$ on $\mathbb{R}^{+} \times S^{1} \times T^{2}$ such that $\Phi_{i}^{*} J_{i}=J_{i, 0}+\mathrm{ED}$, where $g_{\text {flat }}=d t^{2} \oplus \ell^{2} d \varphi^{2} \oplus g_{\varepsilon, \tau}$ and $\varphi \in S^{1}=\mathbb{R} / 2 \pi \mathbb{Z}$ as in Definition 1.4(ii). To see this, define a family $J_{i, 0}(T), T \in \mathbb{R}^{+}$, of such almost-complex structures by setting
$$\left.J_{i, 0}(T)\right|_{(T, *)}:=P\left[\left.\left(\Phi_{i}^{*} J_{i}\right)\right|_{(T, *)}\right],$$
where $P$ denotes nearest-neighbor projection onto $g_{\text {flat }}$-orthogonal almost-complex structures and * is some base point in $S^{1} \times T^{2}$, and then by $g_{\text {flat }}$-parallel transport, noting that flat cylinders $\mathbb{R} \times T^{3}$ are hyperkähler so there are no holonomy issues.

From ODE estimates, $\left|J_{i, 0}(T)-\Phi_{i}^{*} J_{i}\right|_{g_{\text {flat }}}=O\left(e^{-\delta t}+e^{-\delta T}\right)$ on the whole cylinder, uniformly as $T \rightarrow \infty$. Thus $J_{i, 0}(T)$ limits out to some $J_{i, 0}$ as desired.

Since $\left(M_{i}, J_{i}\right)$ is $\Phi_{i}$-compactifiable, $\mu^{*} J_{i, 0}$ must extend continuously to $\Delta \times T^{2}$. This already forces $J_{i, 0}= \pm J_{\ell} \oplus \pm J_{\tau}$, where $J_{\ell}\left(\partial_{t}\right)=\ell^{-1} \partial_{\varphi}$ and $J_{\tau}$ is one of the two $g_{\varepsilon, \tau}$-parallel, $g_{\varepsilon, \tau}$-orthogonal almost-complex structures on $T^{2}$. Since the map $\Pi:=\mu^{-1} \circ \Phi_{2}^{-1} \circ \Psi \circ \Phi_{1} \circ \mu$ extends as a diffeomorphism from $\Delta \times T^{2}$ to itself, preserving $\{0\} \times T^{2}, \Pi$ must pull back $\mu^{*} J_{2,0}$ to $\mu^{*} J_{1,0}$ along $\{0\} \times T^{2}$. Therefore, $\Pi^{*}\left( \pm J_{\mathbb{C}} \oplus \pm J_{\tau}\right)= \pm J_{\mathbb{C}} \oplus \pm J_{\tau}$ along $\{0\} \times T^{2}$ for some combination of the signs. We can make all signs positive by composing with isometries of $g_{\text {flat }}$. Thus, in complex coordinates $(z, w)$ on $\Delta \times T^{2}$, with $w$ uniformizing $\left(T^{2}, J_{\tau}\right), \Pi_{*} \in \operatorname{Gl}(2, \mathbb{C})$ along $\{0\} \times T^{2}$, more precisely $\Pi_{*}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}a & 0 \\ * & b\end{array}\right)$ with $|b|=1$. Hence, by Taylor expansion,
$$\Pi(z, w)=(a z, b w+c)+\left(O\left(|z|^{2}\right), O(|z|)\right), c \in \mathbb{C},$$
thus $\Pi^{*}\left(\mu^{*} g_{\text {flat }}\right)=\left(\mu^{*} g_{\text {flat }}\right)(1+O(|z|))$ and so $\Psi^{*} g_{2}-g_{1}=\mathrm{ED}$. \(\square\)
5.3. Proof of Theorem 1.11. Koiso [44] showed that the kernel of the linearized Einstein operator on a compact Calabi-Yau manifold, modulo Lie derivatives of the metric, is invariant under the action of the almost-complex structure $J$. Thus, the kernel contains its hermitian and skew-hermitian components, which can be viewed as spaces of first-order deformations of the Kähler class, and of $J$, respectively. If $J$ has unobstructed deformations (which is always true, by later work of Tian [70] and Todorov [75]), Koiso then gave a method to integrate all Ricci-flat deformations of the metric by decomposing them into their hermitian and skew-hermitian parts and using the implicit function theorem to solve a family of Monge-Ampère equations on the resulting family of compact Kähler manifolds. Kovalev [46] has sketched an extension of Koiso's theory to the setting of open Calabi-Yau manifolds asymptotic to a complex cylinder $\mathbb{C}^{*} \times D$ with metric $|d \log z|^{2} \oplus g_{D}$, where $\left(D, g_{D}\right)$ is compact Calabi-Yau with $\pi_{1}(D)=0$. The condition on $\pi_{1}$ implies that the complex splitting at infinity is preserved under bounded Ricci-flat deformations. We will develop this theory carefully on ALH spaces, in which case $D$ is a torus and so $\pi_{1}(D) \neq 0$. This ultimately leads to various complications when counting moduli.

In Step 1, we study deformations of the flat metric on $\mathbb{R} \times T^{3}$ from Koiso's point of view, viewing $\mathbb{R} \times T^{3}=\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ for some rank-3 lattice $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{C}^{2}$.

In Step 2, we show that on ALH hyperkähler manifolds diffeomorphic to $X \backslash D$, the kernel $\mathcal{E} \subset \mathcal{H}$ of the linearized Einstein operator contains a harmonic space $\mathcal{D}$ of dimension 34 (the kernel of the Lichnerowicz Laplacian) such that $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}:=\left\{L_{X} g: X=\right.$ parallel + ED $\}$. Except for the precise dimension count, this follows from work in [46].

In Step 3, we describe the space of Lie derivatives that are still contained in $\mathcal{D}$. This turns out to be the space $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}:=\left\{L_{X} g: X\right.$ linearly growing harmonic\}, which reduces the number of moduli from 34 to 30, still counting the multiples of $g$.

In Step 4, we obtain integrability of all first-order Ricci-flat deformations if the hyperkähler ALH space to be deformed is one of those constructed in Tian-Yau [73]. Let ${ }^{ \pm}$denote the projections onto $J$-(skew-)hermitian forms, where $J$ is the unique parallel orthogonal almost-complex structure on $M$ that can be compactified. Then $\mathcal{D}^{ \pm} \subset \mathcal{D}, \operatorname{dim} \mathcal{D}^{+}=12, \operatorname{dim} \mathcal{D}^{-}=22, \operatorname{dim} \mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{lin}}=4, \mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{lin}} \cap \mathcal{D}^{+}=0, \mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{lin}} \cap \mathcal{D}^{-}=\mathbb{R}$, so $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}^{-}$is of codimension 18 in $\mathcal{D}^{-}$. Luckily, $(M, J)$ admits obvious complex moduli from deforming the pencil or moving $D$, and we will see that these yield a subspace $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{D}^{-}$with $\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}} \mathcal{S}=9$ and $\mathcal{S} \cap \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}^{-}=0$. Every element of $\mathcal{E}$ thus differs from a
unique element of $\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S}$ by a Lie derivative. All elements of $\mathcal{S}$ are integrable as deformations of $J$, and it is fairly easy to see that the $\mathcal{S}$-deformations of $(M, J)$ still admit ALH Kähler metrics. This then suffices for Koiso-Kovalev to go through.

Step 1: Deformations of flat cylinders. Equip $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ with its standard Kähler structure $(g, J)$ and let $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{C}^{2}$ be a rank-3 lattice. Then $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda=\mathbb{R} \times T^{3}$ isometrically for the flat metric on $T^{3}=\mathbb{R}^{3} / \mathbb{Z}^{3}$ induced by any marking $\mathbb{Z}^{3} \rightarrow \Lambda$. Thus, the deformation space of the flat metric on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$, including a scale, is naturally identified with the 6-dimensional space $\mathrm{Gl}(3, \mathbb{R}) / \mathrm{O}(3)$ of marked flat metrics on $T^{3}$. We are now going to compare this with Koiso's general approach [44].

The natural space of infinitesimal Ricci-flat deformations here is $\mathcal{D}=\operatorname{Sym}^{2} \mathbb{R}^{4}$. We decompose $\mathcal{D}$ into its $J$-hermitian and $J$-skew-hermitian parts, $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{D}^{-}$, and, writing $h(u, v)=g(H u, v), H=H^{\operatorname{tr}}$, for $h \in \mathcal{D}$, we introduce a linear map
$$\mathfrak{I}: \mathcal{D} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}, \mathfrak{I}(h):=H^{+} J+H^{-} .$$
This defines an isomorphism of $\mathrm{U}(2)$-modules between $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathfrak{s p}(4, \mathbb{R})$ :
$$\begin{aligned}
& \mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{+}\right)=\left\{I \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}: I^{\operatorname{tr}}+I=I J-J I=0\right\}=\mathfrak{u}(2), \\
& \mathfrak{J}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)=\left\{I \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}: I^{\operatorname{tr}}-I=I J+J I=0\right\} \cong \mathfrak{s p}(4, \mathbb{R}) / \mathfrak{u}(2) .
\end{aligned}$$
We can understand $\mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)$as a space of infinitesimal deformations of $J$. In general, if $I \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$ and $I J+J I=0$, there is an associated complex structure $\mathfrak{C}(I)$ on $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ defined by having its (0, 1)-space in $\mathbb{R}^{4} \otimes \mathbb{C}$ equal to $\left\{u^{\prime \prime}+(I u)^{\prime}: u \in \mathbb{R}^{4}\right\}$, where $u^{\prime}=u-i J u$ and $u^{\prime \prime}=u+i J u$. This is the standard way of exponentiating first-order complex deformations in Kodaira-Spencer theory; when applied pointwise on a complex manifold, the integrability of $\mathfrak{C}(I)$ as an almost-complex structure then translates into the Maurer-Cartan equation $\bar{\partial} I+[I, I]=0$. Explicitly,
$$\mathfrak{C}(I)=J(1-I)(1+I)^{-1} .$$
If in addition $I^{\operatorname{tr}}=I$, or equivalently $I \in \mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)$, then $\omega(u, v)=g(J u, v)$ is still of type $(1,1)$ with respect to $\mathfrak{C}(I)$, and $\mathfrak{C}$ identifies its domain of definition in $\mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)$, which is a bounded domain, with the Siegel upper half plane $\mathfrak{H}_{2}=\operatorname{Sp}(4, \mathbb{R}) / \mathrm{U}(2)=$ $\left\{K \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}: K^{2}=-1, K^{*} \omega=\omega\right\}$. In fact, $\mathfrak{C}$ is the generalized Cayley transform. On the other hand, $\mathfrak{C}$ is globally defined on the space of $I \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$ with $I J+J I=0$ and $I^{\operatorname{tr}}+I=0$, which is a complex line (the infinitesimal twistor line of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ ), and maps this to the complement of $-J$ in the actual twistor $\mathbb{P}^{1}$.
(i) $\mathcal{D}$ has dimension 10 and hence overcounts the number of moduli of the flat metric $g$ on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ by four. This is because $\mathcal{D}$ contains a four-dimensional space $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$ of Lie derivatives of $g$ by linear vector fields: On $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, for any $h \in \mathcal{D}, h=L_{X} g$ with $\left.X\right|_{u}=(1 / 2) H u, H$ as above, and $X$ descends to $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ precisely if $H(\Lambda)=0$.
(ii) Suppose the cross-section $T^{3}=S^{1} \times T^{2}$ as an isometric product. Then
$$\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}\left(2 \pi \ell e_{2}\right)+\Lambda^{\prime}, \Lambda^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\operatorname{Im}(\tau)}(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z} \tau) \subset \mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R} e_{3} \oplus \mathbb{R} e_{4},$$
with $\varepsilon, \ell>0$ and $\tau \in \mathfrak{H}$, after rotating the lattice if necessary. In this case, $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }} \subset \mathcal{D}$ has a canonical basis $h_{i}=L_{x^{1} e_{i}} g$ for $1 \leq i \leq 4$, and $h_{2} \in \mathcal{D}^{-}$while $\operatorname{span}\left\{h_{1}, h_{3}, h_{4}\right\}$ projects isomorphically into both $\mathcal{D}^{+}$and $\mathcal{D}^{-}$. Hence, if we quotient by $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$, there remain two skew-hermitian deformations dual to changing the complex modulus $\tau$, and four hermitian ones. One of these is an overall scale, one is dual to changing $\ell^{2} / \varepsilon$, and the remaining two break the isometric splitting of the cross-section. □

Remark 5.2. Step 1(ii) shows that if $\Lambda$ isometrically splits off a $\mathbb{Z}$-factor, then even though the metric on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ can be deformed so that its cross-section no longer splits, the complex structure on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ nevertheless remains compactifiable as $\mathbb{P}^{1} \times\left(\mathbb{C} / \Lambda^{\prime}\right)$ after hyperkähler rotation. Said differently, we can transform any $\Lambda$ into split form by hyperkähler rotation and a $\mathbb{C}$-linear map, but this may twist the metric. \(\square\)

Step 2: The kernel of the linearized Einstein operator. Let $g$ be ALH hyperkähler on a manifold $M$ diffeomorphic to the complement of a smooth fiber in a rational elliptic surface, and recall $\mathcal{H}=\left\{h \in C^{\infty}\left(\operatorname{Sym}^{2} T^{*} M\right): h=\right.$ parallel + ED outside a compact set\}. The linearization $E$ of the Einstein operator at $g$ is given by
$$\left\{\begin{array}{l}
E(h)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla^{*} \nabla h-2 R(h)+L_{D(h)} g\right), \\
R(h)(X, Y):=\sum h\left(R\left(E_{i}, X\right) Y, E_{i}\right), \\
\langle D(h), X\rangle:=\sum \nabla_{E_{i}}\left(h-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{tr} h) g\right)\left(E_{i}, X\right) .
\end{array}\right.$$
Our goal is to describe the space $\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{H} \cap \operatorname{ker} E$ as explicitly as possible. For this, we first of all observe that $D\left(L_{X} g\right)=-\nabla^{*} \nabla X+\operatorname{Ric} X$ for all Riemannian metrics $g$ and vector fields $X$. Thus, from Corollary 5.1 any given $h \in \mathcal{H}$ can be corrected by a Lie derivative $L_{X} g$ with $X=$ parallel + ED outside a compact set such that $D\left(h+L_{X} g\right)=0$. Denote the space of all such Lie derivatives by $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}$. We conclude $\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{D} \cap \operatorname{ker} D) \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}$, with $\mathcal{D}:=\left\{h \in \mathcal{H}: \nabla^{*} \nabla h-2 R(h)=0\right\}$, and the two spaces are transversal since otherwise $(M, g)$ would admit a nontrivial parallel vector field. The space $\mathcal{D}$ is the counterpart over $(M, g)$ to $\operatorname{Sym}^{2} \mathbb{R}^{4}$ over $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ as in Step 1.

Fix a parallel orthogonal almost-complex structure $J$. Then $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{D}^{-}$, and there are Bochner formulas showing that $\mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{+}\right)$and $\mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)$(recall (5.1)) are exactly the spaces of bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic real (1, 1)-forms and $\Delta_{\bar{\partial}}$-harmonic symmetric $T^{1,0} M$-valued (0,1)-forms, respectively. As $\Delta_{\bar{\partial}} \alpha=0 \Leftrightarrow \bar{\partial} \alpha=\bar{\partial}^{*} \alpha=0, \alpha \in \mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{H}^{-}\right)$, we find that $\mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)$consists of first-order deformations of $J$ as an integrable almost-complex structure, i.e. if $I \in \mathfrak{I}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-}\right)$, then all curves $I_{t}$ with $I_{0}=0,\left.I_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}=I$, satisfy $\bar{\partial} I_{t}+\left[I_{t}, I_{t}\right]=0$ to first order in $t$, cf. Step 1. Note that $\Delta_{\bar{\partial}}$-harmonic $T^{1,0} M$-valued (0, 1)-forms are in bijection with $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic $\mathbb{C}$-valued (1, 1)-forms, from dualizing with a holomorphic symplectic form. Thus $\mathcal{D} \cap \operatorname{ker} D=\mathcal{D}$ because wedging a scalar form with $\omega$ preserves harmonicity, so $\operatorname{tr} h$ is harmonic and hence constant for any $h \in \mathcal{D}$, and $\operatorname{div} h=0$ since bounded harmonic forms are $d^{*}$-closed.

In conclusion, $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {bd }}$. Also, $\operatorname{dim} \mathcal{D}=(1+2) \cdot 8+10=34$. This is because by Section 5.1, the space of ED $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic real (1, 1)-forms is 8-dimensional, and the space of bounded $\Delta_{d}$-harmonic 2-forms modulo ED is isomorphic to the space of constant 2-forms on the flat cylinder $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ to which $(M, g)$ asymptotes, through the map $P$ which sends any tensor to its constant part outside a compact set; now $P$ preserves exterior form types with respect to $J$ and $P(J)$, and maps the twistor line $\mathbb{C} \Omega$ of $(M, g, J)$ to that of $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$, so $(P \circ I)(\mathcal{D})=\operatorname{Sym}^{2} \mathbb{R}^{4}=\mathbb{R}^{10}$. \(\square\)

Step 3: Lie derivatives in the harmonic space. Let $h=L_{X} g \in \mathcal{D}$, so $\nabla^{*} \nabla X=0$.
Claim: If $h=\mathrm{ED}$, then $|\nabla X|$ is uniformly bounded on $M$.
Assuming this claim, $X$ is harmonic of at most linear growth. Then the proof of Corollary 5.1(ii) shows $X=t X_{0}+$ parallel + ED outside a compact set, where $X_{0}$ is a constant vector field on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$. But then $h=\mathrm{ED}$ implies $t X_{0}$ is Killing for the flat metric, and so $X_{0}=0$, which implies $X \in \mathcal{D} \cap \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}=\{0\}$ and hence $h=0$.

Thus, if $h \neq 0$, then $h=h_{0}+\mathrm{ED}$ outside a compact set with $h_{0} \neq 0$ a constant bilinear form on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$. Moreover, $h_{0} \in \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$ as defined in Step 1(i) because otherwise $h$ would change the cross-sectional geometry of $(M, g)$ even though $h=L_{X} g$.

Conversely, given any $h_{0} \in \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$, there exists a unique $h=h_{0}+\mathrm{ED}=L_{X} g \in \mathcal{D}$. Here, uniqueness holds by the above, and existence holds because if $h_{0}=L_{X_{0}} g_{\text {flat }}$ with $X_{0}$ linear on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$, then from Corollary 5.1(ii) there exists a harmonic vector field $X$ on $M$ with $X-X_{0}=$ parallel + ED outside a compact set, and then $h:=$ $L_{X} g=h_{0}+$ ED satisfies $E(h)=D(h)=0$, so that $h \in \mathcal{D}$.

Thus, the subspace of $\mathcal{D}$ consisting of Lie derivatives of $g$ is given by $\left\{L_{X} g: X\right.$ linearly growing harmonic\}. We denote this space by $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$, abusing notation.
Proof of the claim. Consider $[0, \infty) \times \mathbb{R}^{3}$. Write $x^{\mu}, \mu \in\{0,1,2,3\}$, for the obvious coordinates, and use Latin superscripts $i \in\{1,2,3\}$ to address the $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ coordinates. Let $u^{\mu}, f_{\lambda}^{\mu \nu}, g^{\mu \nu}$ be smooth functions on $[0, \infty) \times \mathbb{R}^{3}$ which are $\Gamma$-periodic in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ for some lattice $\Gamma$, and suppose that the $f_{\lambda}^{\mu \nu}$ and $g^{\mu \nu}$ decay exponentially, i.e.
$$\left\|f_{\lambda}^{\mu \nu}\right\|_{C^{k}(t)}+\left\|g^{\mu \nu}\right\|_{C^{k}(t)} \leq C(k) e^{-\varepsilon t}$$
for some $\varepsilon \in(0,1]$, all $k \in \mathbb{N}_{0}$, all $t \in[0, \infty)$, where notation such as $C^{k}(t)$ refers to the relevant function spaces on the slice $\{t\} \times \mathbb{R}^{3}$.
Reduced claim: There exists $C=C\left(f_{\lambda}^{\mu \nu}, g^{\mu \nu}\right) \geq 1$ such that if
$$\frac{\partial u^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\nu}}+\frac{\partial u^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\mu}}+\sum f_{\lambda}^{\mu \nu} u^{\lambda}=g^{\mu \nu},$$
and if $\left\|u^{\mu}\right\|_{C^{0}\left(T_{0}\right)}+\left\|\partial_{\nu} u^{\mu}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}\left(T_{0}\right)} \leq C_{0}$ and $\left\|\partial_{\nu} u^{\mu}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq 3 C_{0}$ for all $t \in\left[T_{0}, T\right]$, where $C_{0} \geq 1$ and $T \geq T_{0} \geq C$, then $\left\|\partial_{\nu} u^{\mu}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq 2 C_{0}$ for all $t \in\left[T_{0}, T\right]$.

Thus, assuming (5.3), every smooth and spatially $\Gamma$-periodic solution $u^{\mu}$ to (5.4) on $[0, \infty) \times \mathbb{R}^{3}$ must automatically have uniformly bounded derivatives. From this, the original claim then follows immediately.

To prove the reduced claim, fix $T \geq T_{0} \geq 0, t \in\left[T_{0}, T\right]$. By (5.4), $\mu=\nu=0$,
$$\left\|\partial_{t} u^{0}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq C_{0} C\left(t-T_{0}+1\right) e^{-\varepsilon t} \leq C_{0} C e^{-\varepsilon T_{0}} .$$
Next, we differentiate (5.4), $\mu=\nu=0$, with respect to $x^{j}$. Using the general fact that $\frac{d}{d t}\|v\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq\left\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)}$ and integrating in $t=x^{0}$, we obtain
$$\left\|\partial_{j} u^{0}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq C_{0}\left(1+C e^{-\varepsilon T_{0}}\right) .$$
Plugging this into (5.4) with $\mu=i, \nu=0$ yields
$$\left\|\partial_{t} u^{i}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq C_{0}\left(1+C e^{-\varepsilon T_{0}}\right) .$$
Finally, differentiating (5.4) for $\mu=i, \nu=0$ by $x^{j}$ and integrating in $t$,
$$\left\|\partial_{j} u^{i}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq C_{0}\left(1+C e^{-\varepsilon T_{0}}\right)+\int_{T_{0}}^{t}\left\|\partial_{i} \partial_{j} u^{0}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(s)} d s$$
By Schauder theory, $\left\|\partial_{i} \partial_{j} u^{0}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(s)} \leq C\left\|\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}} u^{0}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(s)}$, where $C=C(\Gamma, \alpha)$, and
$$\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}} u^{0}=\sum\left(\partial_{i} g^{0 i}-\frac{1}{2} \partial_{t} g^{i i}\right)-\sum\left(\partial_{i}\left[f_{\lambda}^{0 i} u^{\lambda}\right]-\frac{1}{2} \partial_{t}\left[f_{\lambda}^{i i} u^{\lambda}\right]\right)$$
from differentiating and tracing (5.4), which shows that
$$\left\|\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}} u^{0}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(s)} \leq C_{0} C\left(s-T_{0}+1\right) e^{-\varepsilon s} .$$
Altogether then, $\left\|\partial_{\nu} u^{\mu}\right\|_{C^{0, \alpha}(t)} \leq C_{0}\left(1+C e^{-\varepsilon T_{0}}\right)$, where $C=C\left(f_{\lambda}^{\mu \nu}, g^{\mu \nu}\right)$. \(\square\)

Step 4: Integrability. Assume now that $(M, g)$ is one of the hyperkähler ALH spaces obtained by Tian-Yau [73] and that the underlying pencil of cubics is generic. Let $J$ be the unique $g$-parallel orthogonal almost-complex structure on $M$ which extends as a complex structure on $X$. By Steps 2-3, $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}, \mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{D}^{-}, \operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{R}} \mathcal{D}^{+}$ $=8+4=12, \operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}} \mathcal{D}^{-}=8+3=11$ (in both cases, the subspaces of ED tensors are of dimension 8, and the remaining dimensions are as on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$ ), and $\mathcal{D}$ contains an exactly four-dimensional space $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$ of Lie derivatives of $g$, which asymptotes to the corresponding space on $\mathbb{C}^{2} / \Lambda$. Since $g$ is $\operatorname{ALH}(\ell, \varepsilon, \tau)$, we can assume that $\Lambda$ is as in Step 1(ii), and hence we have a canonical basis $h_{i}$ for $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}$ such that $h_{2} \in \mathcal{D}^{-}$ while $\operatorname{span}\left\{h_{1}, h_{3}, h_{4}\right\}$ projects isomorphically into both $\mathcal{D}^{+}$and $\mathcal{D}^{-}$.

To prove integrability of the infinitesimal Einstein deformations in $\mathcal{D}$, we proceed in three stages: (i) Construct a space $\mathcal{R}$ of infinitesimal deformations of $J$ which are integrable and compactifiable, such that the corresponding deformed complex structures all admit holomorphic volume forms and ALH Kähler metrics, and such that $\mathcal{R}=\mathfrak{I}(\mathcal{S})$ for a $\mathbb{C}$-linear subspace $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{D}^{-}$with $\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}} \mathcal{S}=9$ and $\mathcal{D}^{-}=\mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}^{-}$. Thus, in particular, $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {bd }}$, and the sum of the first two factors does not contain any Lie derivatives. (ii) Given any $h \in \mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S}$, set up and solve a family of Monge-Ampère equations associated to the complex deformation $h^{-}$and the Kähler deformation $h^{+}$. This produces a curve $g_{t}$ of ALH hyperkähler metrics with $g_{0}=g$, hence a linear map $\mathfrak{L}: \mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{E}, \mathfrak{L}(h):=\left.g_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}$. (iii) Let $\mathfrak{P}$ denote the projection of $\mathcal{E}$ onto $\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S}$ along $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {bd }}$. Then $\mathfrak{P} \circ \mathfrak{L}$ is an isomorphism of $\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S}$, though perhaps not equal to the identity.
(i) Write $M=X \backslash D$ such that $J$ extends to $X, f:(X, J) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$ is the elliptic fibration gotten by blowing up the nine distinct base points of a generic pencil of cubics, and $D=f^{-1}(p)$ is a smooth elliptic curve of modulus $\tau \in \mathfrak{H}$. Tian-Yau [73] construct the $J$-Kähler form of the $\operatorname{ALH}(\ell, \varepsilon, \tau)$ hyperkähler metric $g$ as
$$\omega=\omega_{X}+\ell^{2} f^{*} i \partial \bar{\partial}\left(\chi(\log |z|)^{2}\right)+f^{*} \omega_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u$$
with Kähler metrics $\omega_{X}, \omega_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}$, on $X, \mathbb{P}^{1}$, so that $\omega_{X}$ restricts to a flat Kähler metric of area $\varepsilon$ on $D$, where $z$ is a local coordinate around $p$ on $\mathbb{P}^{1}, \chi$ is a suitable cut-off function, and $u$ is a smooth bounded potential of finite Dirichlet energy. (Observe that Tian-Yau do not establish $\operatorname{ALH}(\ell, \varepsilon, \tau)$ asymptotics for $g$, but this follows from our work in Section 3.4.) Let $f_{t}:\left(X, J_{t}\right) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$ be a small deformation of $f$ obtained by varying the pencil $f$ in its moduli space, which is smooth of complex dimension 8 near $f$, and let $D_{t}=f_{t}^{-1}\left(p_{t}\right)$ for a curve $p_{t}$ in $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ with $p_{0}=p$. By classical work of Kodaira-Spencer [43], $\omega_{X}$ fits into a smooth family of $J_{t}$-Kähler forms $\omega_{X, t}$ on $X$. These may not be exactly flat when restricted to $D_{t}$, however $\left.\omega_{X, t}\right|_{D_{t}}+i \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} v_{t}$ will again be flat of area $\varepsilon$ for a unique smooth function $v_{t}$ on $D_{t}$ such that $\int v_{t} \omega_{X, t}=0$, and $v_{t}$ extends as a smooth family of functions on $X$ with $v_{0} \equiv 0$. Then
$$\omega_{t}:=\omega_{X, t}+i \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} v_{t}+\ell^{2} f_{t}^{*} i \partial \bar{\partial}\left(\chi(\log |z|)^{2}\right)+f_{t}^{*} \omega_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}+i \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} u$$
yields a family of $J_{t}$-Kähler metrics $g_{t}$ on $M_{t}=X \backslash D_{t}$. Let $\Phi: \mathbb{R}^{+} \times T^{3} \rightarrow M \backslash K$ be an ALH coordinate system for $g$. One can construct diffeomorphisms $\Psi_{t}: M \rightarrow M_{t}$ such that $\Psi_{0}=\mathrm{id}, \Psi_{t}^{*} g_{t}$ is smooth in $t$, and $\Phi^{*} \Psi_{t}^{*} g_{t}=\left(d r^{2} \oplus \ell^{2} d \varphi^{2} \oplus h_{t}\right)+O(t \mathrm{ED})$ for some fixed topological splitting $T^{3}=S^{1} \times T^{2}$, where $h_{t}$ is a smooth family of flat Riemannian metrics on $T^{2}$ of area $\varepsilon$ and modulus dictated by $D_{t}$. In addition, one can arrange for $\nabla_{h_{s}} h_{t}=0$ for all $s, t$. In particular, $\Phi^{*} \Psi_{t}^{*} J_{t}=\left(J_{\ell} \oplus K_{t}\right)+O(t$ ED) after reflecting $\Phi$ if necessary, where $J_{\ell}\left(\partial_{r}\right)=\ell^{-1} \partial_{\varphi}$ and $K_{t}$ is a smooth family of $h_{t}$-orthogonal almost-complex structures on $T^{2}$ with $\nabla_{h_{s}} K_{t}=0$ for all $s, t$. Writing
$\Psi_{t}^{*} J_{t}=\mathfrak{C}\left(I_{t}\right)$ with $\mathfrak{C}$ from (5.2), and then $I_{t}=t I+O\left(t^{2}\right)$, we obtain a $(0,1)$-form $I$ with values in $T^{1,0} M$ such that $\bar{\partial} I=0$ and $\Phi^{*} I=\left(\begin{array}{ll}0 & 0 \\ 0 & *\end{array}\right)+\mathrm{ED}$, where the blocks correspond to $\mathbb{R}^{+} \times S^{1}$ and $T^{2}$, and * means a smooth endomorphism field on $T^{2}$ which is $h_{s}$-parallel for any $s$. By Corollary 5.1(ii), there exists a unique vector field $X=$ parallel +ED such that $I^{\circ}:=I+L_{X} J$ satisfies $\bar{\partial} I^{\circ}=\bar{\partial}^{*} I^{\circ}=0$.

This process can be carried out for all deformations $f_{t}:\left(X, J_{t}\right) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$ and $D_{t}=$ $f_{t}^{-1}\left(p_{t}\right)$. We obtain a complex vector space $\mathcal{R}$ of $T^{1,0} M$-valued (0, 1)-forms on $M$, $\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}} \mathcal{R}=9$, such that $\bar{\partial} I=\bar{\partial}^{*} I=0$ for all $I \in \mathcal{R}$. In addition, $\Phi^{*} I=\left(\begin{array}{cc}0 & 0 \\ 0 & *\end{array}\right)+\mathrm{ED}$, and $I$ is tangent to a curve $I_{t}$ with $I_{0}=0$ such that $J_{t}:=\mathfrak{C}\left(I_{t}\right)$ defines a curve of integrable almost-complex structures. By construction, $\left(M, J_{t}\right)$ admits holomorphic volume forms $\Omega_{t}$ and complete Kähler metrics $\omega_{t}$, all of which are ALH with respect to $\Phi$, such that $\nabla_{g_{s}} g_{t}, \nabla_{g_{s}} \Omega_{t}, \nabla_{g_{s}} J_{t}$ are ED for all $s, t$. Also, by direct calculation, if $h \in \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}^{-}$, then $\Phi^{*} h=\left(\begin{array}{c}* \\ *\end{array} 0\right)+\mathrm{ED}$, and so $\mathcal{D}^{-}=\mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}^{-}$with $\mathcal{S}:=\mathfrak{I}^{-1}(\mathcal{R})$.
(ii) Let $h \in \mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S}$ and split $h=h^{+}+h^{-}$accordingly. Put $I:=-\frac{1}{2} \mathfrak{I}\left(h^{-}\right) \in \mathcal{R}$ and let $J_{t}, \Omega_{t}, \omega_{t}$ be as above. The form $\eta(X, Y):=g\left(\mathfrak{I}\left(h^{+}\right) X, Y\right)$ is (1,1)-harmonic with respect to $g$ and $J$ from Step 2. Let $\eta_{t}$ denote the (1, 1)-component of $\eta$ with respect to $J_{t}$, and consider the following family of Monge-Ampère equations:
$$\left(\omega_{t}+t \eta_{t}+i \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} u_{t}\right)^{2}=\alpha_{t} \Omega_{t} \wedge \bar{\Omega}_{t} .$$
Since $\nabla_{g_{s}} g_{t}, \nabla_{g_{s}} \Omega_{t}, \nabla_{g_{s}} J_{t}$ are ED for all $s, t$, we can choose $\alpha_{t}$ in such a way that $\left(\omega_{t}+t \eta_{t}\right)^{2}-\alpha_{t} \Omega_{t} \wedge \bar{\Omega}_{t}$ is ED for all $t$. Then, as indicated by Kovalev [46], because $u_{0} \equiv 0$ is a solution for $t=0$, by Corollary 5.1(i) and the implicit function theorem there exists a smooth family of solutions $u_{t}$ of linear growth, so $\omega_{t}+t \eta_{t}+i \partial_{t} \bar{\partial}_{t} u_{t}$ defines an ALH hyperkähler metric $g_{t}$. We obtain a linear map $\mathfrak{L}: \mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{E}$ by setting $\mathfrak{L}(h):=\left.g_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}$, and it remains to show that this is essentially the identity.
(iii) Recall $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {bd }}$, and let $\mathfrak{P}$ denote the projection from $\mathcal{E}$ onto the first two factors along the last two. We will prove that $\mathfrak{P} \circ \mathfrak{L}$ is an isomorphism, which is enough for Theorem 1.11. Accept for the time being that $\mathfrak{L}(h)^{-}=h^{-}$and $\mathfrak{L}\left(h^{+}\right)=h^{+}$for all $h \in \mathcal{D}$. Then $(\mathfrak{P} \circ \mathfrak{L})(h)=0$ for some $h \in \mathcal{D}^{+} \oplus \mathcal{S}$ would imply $\mathfrak{L}(h)=L_{X} g \in \mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }} \oplus \mathcal{L}_{\text {bd }}$, so $h^{-}=\left(L_{X} g\right)^{-}$and $h^{+}+\mathfrak{L}\left(h^{-}\right)^{+}=\left(L_{X} g\right)^{+}$. From the first equation, since all elements of $\mathcal{L}_{\text {bd }}$ are ED and the parallel parts of non-trivial elements of $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text {lin }}^{-}$are all distinct, we conclude $L_{X} g \in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bd}}$, hence $h^{-}=0$ from integration by parts, and so $h^{+}=0$ from the second equation and integration by parts. This shows that $\mathfrak{P} \circ \mathfrak{L}$ is injective and therefore an isomorphism.

As for the proof of the two properties of $\mathfrak{L}$ that we used, notice that $\mathfrak{L}(h)^{-}=h^{-}$ simply follows from $J_{t}^{*} g_{t}=g_{t}$ and $\left.J_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}=J \mathfrak{I}\left(h^{-}\right)$. The second property is the only point where we need to exploit that $\eta$ is harmonic. Let $h=h^{+} \in \mathcal{D}^{+}$. Then (5.5) becomes $\left(\omega+t \eta+i \partial \bar{\partial} u_{t}\right)^{2}=\alpha_{t} \Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}$ with $\alpha_{t}$ such that $(\omega+t \eta)^{2}-\alpha_{t} \Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}$ is ED. The claim is trivial for $h$ a constant multiple of $g$, so we can assume that $\eta \wedge \omega=0$ because $\eta$ is harmonic and so $\operatorname{tr} h$ is constant. This implies $\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{0}+O\left(t^{2}\right)$, hence $\alpha_{t} \Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}=\left(1+O\left(t^{2}\right)\right)(\omega+t \eta)^{2}$, so $\Delta\left(\left.u_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}\right)=0$ from differentiating the PDE by $t$, and therefore $\left.u_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}=0,\left.\omega_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}=\eta,\left.g_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}=h$, since $\left.u_{t}^{\prime}\right|_{0}$ is of linear growth. \(\square\)

\section*{6. Further questions}

One major open question in 4-manifold geometry is the structure of Riemannian metrics with bounded Ricci tensor, which even in the Einstein case is not very well understood if collapsing is not prohibited [72]. Any loss of compactness should be caused by gravitational instantons with little "internal structure."

Problem 6.1. Let $f: X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$ be an elliptic fibration on $K 3$. Let $\varpi \in H^{2}(X, \mathbb{Z})$ be the Poincaré dual of the fibers and let $\varpi_{t}, t \in[0,1]$, be a smooth path in $H^{2}(X, \mathbb{R})$ with $\varpi_{t}$ inside the Kähler cone for $t<1$ and $\varpi_{1}=\varpi$. The collapsed limit metric on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ of the Ricci-flat metrics in $\varpi_{t}$ as $t \rightarrow 1$ was identified in [31, 69, 76], see also (4.10), fitting in with the general picture that limits of compact Einstein 4-manifolds with bounded Euler numbers are Riemannian orbifolds away from a finite number of points [59]. Prove that exactly the following bubbles form:
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] For every singular fiber of finite monodromy, an isotrivial ALG space with the corresponding dual fiber at infinity, cf. Example 4.1.
\item[-] For $\mathrm{I}_{b}$, one Taub-NUT at every fixed point of the canonical $S^{1}$-action [31, 49].
\item[-] For $\mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}$ with $b>0, b$ copies of Taub-NUT as before, plus one copy of the crepant resolution of $\left(\mathbb{R}^{3} \times S^{1}\right) / \mathbb{Z}_{2}$, cf. [5], for each tail of the dual Dynkin diagram.
\end{itemize}

One motivation for this is the Kummer surface heuristics from the Introduction, which would correspond to four isotrivial ALG spaces with an $\mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}$ fiber both at the core and at infinity. Also, along the Gross-Wilson path with $24 \mathrm{I}_{1}$ singular fibers, we expect one Taub-NUT to bubble off from each Ooguri-Vafa space that was glued in. Finally, recall from Table 4.1 that in the general setting described in this problem, the limiting metric on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ will have a cone point underneath each singular fiber of finite monodromy, whose angle is the same as the asymptotic cone angle of an ALG space with the dual fiber removed, which suggests a gluing construction.
