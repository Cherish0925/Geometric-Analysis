If $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ is parabolic, this means that we must impose an integrability condition, $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$, to kill off unbounded contributions from the leading term in the asymptotic expansion of the Green's function, even though geometrically reasonable solutions $u$ may well exist for arbitrary $f$ of sufficient decay. In our main application, integrability will come from replacing $\omega_{0}$ by $\omega_{0}+\beta$ if necessary, where $\beta \in C_{0}^{\infty}$ and $\beta=i \partial \bar{\partial} \varphi$ with $\varphi$ asymptotic to a Green's function, cf. Remark 4.7(i).

To state the existence result, let $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ denote a complete noncompact Kähler manifold with a $C^{3, \alpha}$ quasi-atlas (cf. Definition 3.2 and also (3.1) for the associated Hölder spaces) which satisfies $\operatorname{SOB}(\beta)$ from Definition 2.1 for some $\beta>0$.

Proposition 3.1. Let $f \in C^{2, \alpha}(M)$ satisfy $|f| \leq C r^{-\mu}$ for some $\mu>2$, and in addition $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$ if $\beta \leq 2$. Then there exists $u \in C^{4, \bar{\alpha}}(M), \bar{\alpha} \in(0, \alpha]$, with $\int|\nabla u|^{2}<\infty$ if $\beta \leq 2$, such that $\left(\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u\right)^{m}=e^{f} \omega_{0}^{m}$. If moreover $f \in C_{\text {loc }}^{k, \bar{\alpha}}(M)$ for some $k \geq 3$, then every such $u$ is in $C_{\text {loc }}^{k+2, \bar{\alpha}}(M)$.

We will prove this in Sections 3.1-3.3 by applying Tian-Yau's method from [73], but using our sharp Sobolev inequalities from Section 2. In fact, the existence result from [73] would already be sufficient for all applications in this paper because in our setting, $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$ and $\beta \leq 2$. However, Proposition 3.1 appears to be essentially sharp under the given conditions and may thus be of independent interest.

Theorem 1.5 requires some amount of decay for $u$ rather than just boundedness. The analysis for this will be carried out in Section 3.4 and is self-contained in that we do not need pseudodifferential methods (we stay "above the first indicial root"). To get pointwise decay, we employ an energy argument if $\beta \leq 2$ and Moser iteration with weights if $\beta>2$. Derivative decay then follows by bootstrapping. We refer to Section 3.4 for the precise statements, which are a little unwieldy.

\subsection*{3.1. Set-up, structure of the proof.}

Definition 3.2. Let $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ be a complete Kähler manifold. A $C^{k, \alpha}$ quasi-atlas for $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ is a collection $\left\{\Phi_{x}: x \in A\right\}, A \subset M$, of holomorphic local diffeomorphisms $\Phi_{x}: B \rightarrow M, \Phi_{x}(0)=x$, from $B=B(0,1) \subset \mathbb{C}^{m}$ into $M$ which extend smoothly to the closure $\bar{B}$, and such that there exists a constant $C \geq 1$ such that $\operatorname{inj}\left(\Phi_{x}^{*} g_{0}\right) \geq \frac{1}{C}$, $\frac{1}{C} g_{\mathbb{C}^{m}} \leq \Phi_{x}^{*} g_{0} \leq C g_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}$, and $\left\|\Phi_{x}^{*} g_{0}\right\|_{C^{k, \alpha}(B)} \leq C$ for all $x \in A$, and such that for all $y \in M$ there exists $x \in A$ with $y \in \Phi_{x}(B)$ and $\operatorname{dist}_{g_{0}}\left(y, \partial \Phi_{x}(B)\right) \geq \frac{1}{C}$.

Given a $C^{k, \alpha}$ quasi-atlas, we can define global Hölder spaces of functions $C^{l, \gamma}(M)$ for all $l \in \mathbb{N}_{0}$ and $0 \leq \gamma<1$ by setting
$$\|u\|_{C^{l, \gamma}(M)}:=\sup \left\{\left\|u \circ \Phi_{x}\right\|_{C^{l, \gamma}(B)}: x \in A\right\} .$$
There is an analogous definition of $C^{l, \gamma}(U)$ for any open set $U \subset M$. We also work with local Hölder spaces, whose definition only requires an ordinary atlas and is in fact independent of such a choice. For a second order elliptic differential operator $L$ with coefficients in $C^{l, \gamma}(M)$, we then have $\|u\|_{C^{l+2, \gamma}(M)} \leq C\left(\|L u\|_{C^{l, \gamma}(M)}+\|u\|_{\infty}\right)$ for all $u \in C^{\infty}(M)$, provided that $l \leq k-1$ and $0<\gamma \leq \alpha$. This follows from elliptic estimates, together with the fact that the euclidean $C^{l, \gamma}$ norm on $B$ is comparable with the intrinsic $C^{l, \gamma}$ norm associated to $\Phi_{x}^{*} g_{0}$ if $l \leq k+1$ and $\gamma \leq \alpha$.

Tian-Yau [73, Proposition 1.2] provide a simple criterion for $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ to admit a $C^{k, \alpha}$ quasi-atlas. We restate it here and write out a slightly more detailed proof.

Lemma 3.3. If $|\mathrm{Rm}| \leq C$, then there exists a quasi-atlas which is $C^{1, \alpha}$ for all $\alpha$. If moreover $|\nabla \mathrm{Scal}|+\ldots+\left|\nabla^{k} \mathrm{Scal}\right| \leq C$, then this quasi-atlas is even $C^{k+1, \alpha}$.

Sketch of proof. Fix $x_{0} \in M$. By pulling back the Kähler structure under $\exp _{x_{0}}$ and rescaling, we may assume that $\operatorname{inj}\left(x_{0}\right) \geq \frac{\pi}{2}$. Fix an orthonormal frame $\left\{X_{j}, Y_{j}\right\}$ at $x_{0}, Y_{j}=J X_{j}$, and extend it to $B\left(x_{0}, 1\right)$ by parallel transport along radial geodesics. From Jost-Karcher [38, Satz 2.1], there exist coordinates $\left\{x_{j}, y_{j}\right\}$ on $B\left(x_{0}, 1\right)$ with $\left|\nabla x_{j}-X_{j}\right| \leq C \rho$ and $\left|\nabla y_{j}-Y_{j}\right| \leq C \rho$, where $\rho:=\operatorname{dist}\left(x_{0},-\right)$. By 38, Satz 5.1], there then exists $r_{0}>0$ small but definite such that for all $0<r \leq r_{0}$ there exist harmonic coordinates $\left\{\tilde{x}_{j}, \tilde{y}_{j}\right\}$ on $B\left(x_{0}, r\right)$ with $\left|\nabla \tilde{x}_{j}-\nabla x_{j}\right| \leq C r^{2}$. These satisfy the $C^{1, \alpha}$ requirement, but may not be holomorphic.

However, if $\tilde{z}_{j}:=\tilde{x}_{j}+i \tilde{y}_{j}$, then $\left|\bar{\partial} \tilde{z}_{j}\right| \leq C r$. Solving the $\bar{\partial}$-Neumann problem on $B\left(x_{0}, r\right)$ with $r$ small but still definite, we can construct a function $w_{j}$ on $B\left(x_{0}, r\right)$ such that $\bar{\partial} w_{j}=\bar{\partial} \tilde{z}_{j}$ and $\left\|w_{j}\right\|_{2} \leq C r\left\|\bar{\partial} w_{j}\right\|_{2} \leq C r^{m+2}$. To see this, we apply the version of Hörmander's $L^{2}$ estimate in Demailly [20, Corollary 8.10], using that $\rho^{2}$ is smooth and uniformly convex on $B\left(x_{0}, r\right)$ when $r$ is small enough.

Now $\Delta w_{j}=\Delta \tilde{z}_{j}=0$. With respect to $\left\{\tilde{x}_{j}, \tilde{y}_{j}\right\}$ (even with respect to $\left\{x_{j}, y_{j}\right\}$ ), $\Delta$ is uniformly elliptic with bounded coefficients, so [25, Theorem 8.15] applies and yields $\left\|w_{j}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(B\left(x_{0}, r / 2\right)\right)} \leq C r^{-m}\left\|w_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(B\left(x_{0}, r\right)\right)} \leq C r^{2}$. Finally, since $\Delta$ even has $C^{0, \alpha}$ coefficients in $\left\{\tilde{x}_{j}, \tilde{y}_{j}\right\}$, we obtain $\left\|\nabla w_{j}\right\|_{\infty} \leq C r,\left\|w_{j}\right\|_{C^{2, \alpha}} \leq C r^{-\alpha}$. Thus, making $r$ smaller if needed, the functions $\tilde{z}_{j}-w_{j}$ define holomorphic coordinates on $B\left(x_{0}, r\right)$ because their gradients are still linearly independent, and moreover still satisfy the $C^{1, \alpha}$ condition. For $C^{k+1, \alpha}$ one uses the local formulas for the Ricci and scalar curvatures of a Kähler metric, as in the proof of [73, Proposition 1.2]. \(\square\)

We now outline the proof of Proposition 3.1. Some preliminaries: If
$$\left(\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u\right)^{m}=e^{f} \omega_{0}^{m}$$
with $u \in C^{4}(M)$ and $f \in C^{2}(M)$, then $\omega:=\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u$ is again positive, thus even uniformly equivalent to $\omega_{0}$ because $\operatorname{det}_{\omega_{0}} \omega=e^{f}$ and $\operatorname{tr}_{\omega_{0}} \omega=m+\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{\omega_{0}} u$. To see this, it suffices to prove $\omega>0$ at one point because $\operatorname{det}_{\omega_{0}} \omega$ never vanishes. Now $u$ need not attain its infimum but we can use the following version of Yau's maximum principle, which is a simple modification of Yau [80, Theorem 1]:

Lemma 3.4 (Yau). Let $(M, g)$ be complete with sectional curvature bounded below, and let $x_{0} \in M$. If $u \in C_{\text {loc }}^{2}(M)$ with $\sup |u|+\sup |\nabla u|+\sup \left|\nabla^{2} u\right|<\infty$ does not attain its supremum on $M$, then there exists $\left\{x_{k}\right\} \subset M$ such that $\operatorname{dist}\left(x_{0}, x_{k}\right) \rightarrow \infty$ and $u\left(x_{k}\right) \rightarrow \sup u,\left|(\nabla u)\left(x_{k}\right)\right| \rightarrow 0$, and $\lim \sup _{k} \max \left\{\operatorname{spec}\left(\left(\nabla^{2} u\right)\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\right\} \leq 0$. \(\square\)

As usual, the following identity is all-important when studying (3.2):
$$\omega^{m}-\omega_{0}^{m}=\frac{1}{2} d d^{c} u \wedge \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\left(\omega^{k} \wedge \omega_{0}^{m-1-k}\right)$$
This first of all shows that the linearization of (3.2) at $\omega_{0}$ is a Poisson equation,
$$\dot{f} \omega_{0}^{m}=\frac{m}{2} d d^{c} \dot{u} \wedge \omega_{0}^{m-1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{\omega_{0}} \dot{u}\right) \omega_{0}^{m},$$
but more substantially, it expresses the divergence structure of $\mathbb{C M A}$, allowing one to treat (3.2) much in analogy with (3.4) even when $u$ is not infinitesimal.

In particular, $\omega^{m}-\omega_{0}^{m}$ is exact, so $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$ if $M$ is compact. By Yau's famous theorem [81], this is then the only constraint that a given $f$ must satisfy in order for (3.2) to admit a solution $u$, which then is unique up to a constant.

If $M$ is noncompact, then (3.2) does not necessarily imply $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$, not even if $f \in C_{0}^{2}(M)$, because $|\nabla u|$ may not decay fast enough to permit integration
by parts. Indeed, if $\beta>2$, we will prove existence of bounded solutions $u$ for any $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$. On the other hand, if $\beta \leq 2$, then presumably $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$ is not just technically necessary to produce a bounded $u$, but we may expect solutions for general $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$ to grow like a Green's function, cf. Remark 4.7(i).

As for details, if one attempts to generalize Yau's proof in the compact case, one must eventually prove a-priori estimates for both (3.2) and its linearization. Given Yau's work, the remaining difficulties for the linear and the non-linear equation are fairly similar. The derivative estimates are either local anyway, or can be salvaged using Lemma 3.4. The $L^{\infty}$ estimate relies on iteration, thus on Sobolev inequalities. For $\beta>2$, the iteration formally works just like in the compact case, and for $\beta \leq 2$, one exploits $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$ to compensate for the "subtraction" in (2.1).

The only issue with this strategy is that we need to be able to integrate by parts in order to perform Moser iteration, but $|u|$ and $|\nabla u|$ might not decay fast enough. For the linear problem, one could resort to solving Dirichlet problems on larger and larger domains, but this is not an option for $\mathbb{C} M A$.

The key idea in Tian-Yau [73] is to introduce a small parameter $\varepsilon>0$ :
$$\left(\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u_{\varepsilon}\right)^{m}=e^{f+\varepsilon u_{\varepsilon}} \omega_{0}^{m} .$$
This is then fairly simple to solve by the continuity method because both (3.5) and its linearization enjoy a trivial $L^{\infty}$ bound from the maximum principle. It remains to establish a uniform $L^{\infty}$ estimate for (3.5) as $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$. For intuition, observe that the Green's function of $\Delta-\varepsilon$ in $\mathbb{R}^{\beta}$ decays exponentially even if $\beta=2$. Indeed, it turns out that solutions to (3.5) are highly integrable, so Moser iteration does go through now, although the extra $\varepsilon$ term is not easy to deal with when $\beta \leq 2$.

In Section 3.2, we recall how to solve (3.5). This is due to Cheng-Yau [16]. We mainly wish to clarify what assumptions on $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ and $f$ are really used.

In Section 3.3, we then establish high integrability of the solutions of (3.5), and show how to prove an $L^{\infty}$ estimate for (3.5) that is uniform in $\varepsilon$. Both parts of the argument are based on iteration, thus on the divergence structure in (3.3).
3.2. Solution of the $\varepsilon$-perturbed equation. Here we only require that $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ is a complete Kähler manifold which admits a $C^{3, \alpha}$ quasi-atlas for some $\alpha \in(0,1)$, and $f \in C^{2, \alpha}(M)$ in the sense of (3.1). For a fixed $\varepsilon>0$, we will use the continuity method to prove that (3.5) has a solution $u_{\varepsilon} \in C^{4, \bar{\alpha}}(M)$ for some $\bar{\alpha} \in(0, \alpha]$ which may possibly depend on $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right), \alpha, f$, but not on $\varepsilon$ if $\varepsilon \leq 1$.

Replacing $f$ by $t f, t \in[0,1]$, in (3.5), we obtain an equation (3.5 $t$ ). For $\gamma \in(0, \alpha]$ define $\mathcal{T}_{\gamma}:=\{t \in[0,1]: 3.5 t)$ has a solution $\left.u_{\varepsilon, t} \in C^{4, \gamma}(M)\right\}$. Trivially, $0 \in \mathcal{T}_{\gamma}$ for all $\gamma$, and the gist of the proof is to establish existence of at least one $\bar{\alpha} \in(0, \alpha]$ such that $\mathcal{T}_{\bar{\alpha}}$ is both open and closed in [0, 1].

Claim 1. For all $\gamma \in(0, \alpha]$ and $t \in \mathcal{T}_{\gamma}$ there exists a bounded linear operator $G$ : $C^{2, \gamma}(M) \rightarrow C^{4, \gamma}(M)$ with $\left(\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{\omega}-\varepsilon\right) \circ G=\mathrm{id}$, where $\omega:=\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u_{\varepsilon, t}$.

Claim 1 implies openness of $\mathcal{T}_{\gamma}$ in [0, 1]. Notice that $\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u>0$ is indeed an open condition on $u \in C^{4, \gamma}(M)$ because of the definition, (3.1).

Proof of Claim 1. By Schauder theory and because $\varepsilon>0$, for every bounded and sufficiently smooth domain $\Omega \subset M$ there exists a unique solution $u=u_{\Omega} \in C^{4, \gamma}(\Omega)$ for the Dirichlet problem $\left(\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{\omega}-\varepsilon\right) u=\left.f\right|_{\Omega}$ in $\Omega, u=0$ on $\partial \Omega$, and there are local estimates (with respect to the charts of the chosen quasi-atlas) for $\|u\|_{C^{4, \gamma}}$ in terms
of $\|f\|_{C^{2, \gamma}}$ and $\|u\|_{\infty}$. By the maximum principle, $\|u\|_{\infty} \leq \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{\infty}$. Thus we can apply Arzelà-Ascoli to the sequence $\left\{u_{\Omega_{k}}\right\}$ for an exhaustion $M=\bigcup \Omega_{k}$. \(\square\)

Claim 2. There exists $\bar{\alpha} \in(0, \alpha]$ such that if $u=u_{\varepsilon, t} \in C^{4}(M)$ solves (3.5 t) for $t \in[0,1], \varepsilon \in(0,1]$, then $\|u\|_{C^{2, \bar{\alpha}}(M)} \leq C\left(M, \omega_{0},\|f\|_{C^{2, \alpha}(M)}\right)$.

Then, writing out (3.5, $t$ ) in a local quasi-chart and taking partials with respect to coordinates, we find that $v:=D_{i} u$ satisfies an elliptic equation $\operatorname{tr}\left(A \cdot D^{2} v\right)=$ $B * D^{2} u+t D_{i} f+\varepsilon v$, where $A$ is controlled in $C^{2, \bar{\alpha}}$ and $B$ is controlled in $C^{1, \bar{\alpha}}$, independent of the chart, by Claim 2. Thus, $u$ is controlled in the global Hölder space $C^{4, \bar{\alpha}}(M)$. Altogether this yields closedness of $\mathcal{T}_{\bar{\alpha}}$ in [0, 1].

Proof of Claim 2. First, by Lemma 3.4, $\|u\|_{\infty} \leq \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{\infty}$. Next,
$$0 \leq \operatorname{tr}_{\omega_{0}} \omega=m+\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{\omega_{0}} u \leq C \exp (C(u-\inf u)),$$
with $C=C\left(M, \omega_{0},\|f\|_{C^{2}(M)}\right)$ if $\varepsilon \leq 1$. This is a simple variant, using Lemma 3.4, of Yau's classical $C^{2}$ estimate. We recommend Błocki [6] for a readable exposition. (3.6) has two important implications: First, $\left\|d d^{c} u\right\|_{\infty} \leq C$ since $\operatorname{det}_{\omega_{0}} \omega=e^{f+\varepsilon u}$, so in particular (3.5) is uniformly elliptic at $u$. Second, $\|u\|_{C^{1, \gamma}(M)} \leq C(\gamma)$ for all $\gamma \in(0,1)$ by linear theory. Finally, exploiting $\left\|d d^{c} u\right\|_{\infty} \leq C$ and uniform ellipticity, well-known local estimates yield the desired $C^{2, \bar{\alpha}}$ bound, see again [6]. \(\square\)
3.3. Passage to the limit as $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$. From our previous discussion, only the $L^{\infty}$ estimate needs to be made uniform in $\varepsilon$, and this involves two steps: (1) Solutions of (3.5) are highly integrable, although the values of their $L^{p}$ norms may blow up as $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$, and (2) Moser iteration, which crucially relies on being able to integrate by parts, which in turn is justified by (1). Both steps involve multiplying (3.3) by a host of test functions and integrating by parts. We impose conditions on $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$, $u$, and $f$ as they become necessary.

Here is the basic calculation. For $u=u_{\varepsilon} \in C_{\text {loc }}^{2}(M)$ solving (3.5), $\zeta \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$, $p>1$, multiply both sides of (3.3) by $\zeta u|u|^{p-2}$ to obtain
$$\zeta u|u|^{p-2}\left(e^{f+\varepsilon u}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=\frac{1}{2} \zeta u|u|^{p-2} d d^{c} u \wedge T$$
with $T:=\omega_{0}^{m-1}+\omega_{0}^{m-2} \wedge \omega+\ldots+\omega^{m-1}$. Then integrate by parts, which yields
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left.\left.\int \zeta|\nabla| u\right|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right|^{2} \omega_{0}^{m}+\frac{m p^{2}}{2(p-1)} \int \zeta u|u|^{p-2}\left(e^{\varepsilon u}-1\right) e^{f} \omega_{0}^{m} \leq \\
& -\frac{m p^{2}}{2(p-1)}\left[\int \zeta u|u|^{p-2}\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}+\frac{1}{2} \int u|u|^{p-2} d \zeta \wedge d^{c} u \wedge T\right] .
\end{aligned}$$
Notice the structure of the good term with $\varepsilon$ on the left: For all $C<\infty$ there exists $\delta=\delta(C)>0$ such that if $|x| \leq C$, then $x\left(e^{x}-1\right) \geq \delta x^{2}$, so $u\left(e^{\varepsilon u}-1\right) \geq \delta \varepsilon u^{2}$ for a constant $\delta>0$ that only depends on an upper bound for $\|f\|_{\infty}$.
(1) High integrability. By [68, Theorem I.4.2], if $\operatorname{Ric}\left(\omega_{0}\right)$ is bounded below, then there exists a smooth function $\rho: M \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ such that $\rho \sim 1+\operatorname{dist}\left(x_{0},-\right)$ for every fixed $x_{0} \in M$. Fix a smooth cut-off function $\chi: \mathbb{R}^{+} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{+}$with $\chi(t)=1$ for $t<1$, $\chi(t)=0$ for $t>2$, apply (3.8) with $\zeta=\left(\chi \circ \frac{\rho}{R}\right) \rho^{k}, k \in \mathbb{R}, R>0$, and let $R \rightarrow \infty$.

Assuming $\|u\|_{C^{2}(M)}<\infty$ (so in particular $\omega$ and $\omega_{0}$ are uniformly equivalent) and $\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty$, this readily implies
$$\int \rho^{k}|u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon \int \rho^{k}|u|^{p} \leq C\left(\int \rho^{k}|u|^{p-1}\left|e^{f}-1\right|+\int \rho^{k-2}|u|^{p}\right),$$
where all integrals are against $\omega_{0}^{m}$, and $C$ may depend in an unspecified way on $M$, $\omega_{0}, f, u, \rho, p$, and $k$. Now assume $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ has polynomial volume growth and

Temporary Assumption: $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$.
Then fix $k_{0} \in \mathbb{Z}$ sufficiently negative and iterate (3.9) from $k=k_{0}$ to obtain
$$\int \rho^{k}|u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int \rho^{k}|u|^{p}<\infty \quad\left(k \in \mathbb{N}_{0}, p>1\right) .$$
As mentioned above, this qualitative high integrability (at least for $k=0$ ) of $u=u_{\varepsilon}$, $\varepsilon>0$, is what allows us to perform Moser iteration in Step (2) and thus derive an $L^{\infty}$ bound for $u$ which is uniform in $\varepsilon$. We also need (3.10), but for all $k \in \mathbb{N}_{0}$, in Section 3.4 to prove pointwise decay for $u$ if $M$ satisfies $\operatorname{SOB}(\beta)$ with $\beta>2$.

It may help to stress at this point that the Temporary Assumption is stronger than needed for (3.10), and the full strength of (3.10) is not needed for everything that follows. However, we will eventually find that the size of the support of $f$ does not influence the final quantitative bounds for $u$, which will then allow us to relax $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$ to the condition stated in Proposition 3.1
(2) Uniform $L^{\infty}$ estimate. Applying (3.8) with $\zeta=\chi \circ \frac{\rho}{R}, R \rightarrow \infty$, any $p>1$, and using (3.10) with $k=0$ to get rid of the boundary term, we obtain
$$\left.\left.\int|\nabla| u\right|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right|^{2}+\frac{m p^{2}}{2(p-1)} \int u|u|^{p-2}\left(e^{\varepsilon u}-1\right) \leq-\frac{m p^{2}}{2(p-1)} \int u|u|^{p-2}\left(e^{f}-1\right) .$$
We now assume all the hypotheses of Proposition 3.1. If $\beta>2$, then, by (2.2),
$$\left(\int|U|^{2 \alpha} \rho^{\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq C \int|\nabla U|^{2}\left(U \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M), \alpha \in\left[1, \alpha_{n 2}\right]\right) .$$
For $\mu>2$ as in the hypothesis, let $C_{0}:=\sup \rho^{\mu}|f|<\infty$. Then from (3.11), (3.12), and Hölder's inequality, we can deduce the following two facts: First,
$$p>\frac{\beta-2}{\mu-2} \Longrightarrow\left(\int|u|^{\alpha p} \rho^{\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq \frac{C_{0} C p^{2}}{p-1}\left(\int|u|^{\alpha p} \rho^{\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{\alpha p}},$$
so the corresponding weighted $L^{\alpha p}$ norm of $u$ is finite for all such $p$ and we may start the Moser iteration process at $\alpha p$. Second, if $q:=\mu+\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta>0$, which can be arranged for by a suitable choice of $\alpha>1$, then
$$p>\frac{\alpha}{q}(\beta-2) \Longrightarrow\left(\int|u|^{\alpha p} \rho^{\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq \frac{C_{0} C p^{2}}{p-1}\left(\int|u|^{p} \rho^{\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}},$$
so we can keep iterating from any such $p$. This proves Proposition 3.1 if $\beta>2$; in particular, we can now trivially get rid of the Temporary Assumption above.

The parabolic case, $\beta \leq 2$, is considerably more delicate. By (2.1),
$$\left(\int_{M}\left|U-U_{\psi}\right|^{2 \alpha} \varphi\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq C \int|\nabla U|^{2} \quad\left(U \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M), \alpha \in\left[1, \alpha_{n 2}\right]\right),$$
where we can take $\varphi=\rho^{\alpha(\beta-2-\delta)-\beta}, \psi \sim \rho^{-2-\delta}$ for any $\delta>0$, and $U_{\psi}$ denotes the weighted average of $U$ with respect to $\psi$. If $v:=u-u_{\psi}$ was still a solution of (3.5), then from (3.11) applied to $v$, and (3.14), we would get
$$\begin{array}{r}
\left(\int|v|^{2 \alpha} \varphi\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq C \int|v|\left|e^{f}-1\right|, \\
\left(\int\left||v|^{\frac{p}{2}}-\left(|v|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)_{\psi}\right|^{2 \alpha} \varphi\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq \frac{C p^{2}}{p-1} \int|v|^{p-1}\left|e^{f}-1\right| .
\end{array}$$
Thus, if $|f| \leq C \rho^{-\mu}$ with $\mu>1+\frac{\beta}{2}$, then (3.15) would imply $\|v\|_{\varphi, 2 \alpha}<\infty$, and using the general inequality
$$\left\|\left(|U|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)_{\psi}\right\|_{\varphi, 2 \alpha} \leq\left(\int \psi\right)^{-1}\left(\int \frac{\psi^{2}}{\varphi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int \varphi\right)^{\frac{1}{2 \alpha}}\|U\|_{\varphi, p}^{\frac{p}{2}}$$
(where all three factors on the right-hand side are finite provided $\alpha<2$ ) we could then iterate (3.16), assuming in addition that $\mu>2$.

The integrability condition $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$ will be used precisely to circumvent the difficulty that $v:=u-u_{\psi}$ does not in general solve (3.5) anymore. For this we now entirely follow Tian-Yau. The first thing to observe is that from (3.11) applied to $u$, (3.14), and $\int\left(e^{f}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$, the inequality (3.15) still holds for $v$ even though $v$ itself may not solve (3.5). However, (3.11) is not enough to iterate. For this one has to go back to (3.3) and multiply by other test functions than $\zeta u|u|^{p-2}$, e.g. by $\zeta \max \left\{0, u-u_{\psi}\right\}^{p-1}$ if $u_{\psi} \geq 0$, cf. [73, Lemma 3.5]. As a result, $-C \leq u \leq u_{\psi}+C$ if $u_{\psi} \geq 0$, and $u_{\psi}-C \leq u \leq C$ if $u_{\psi} \leq 0$, assuming $|f| \leq C \rho^{-\mu}, \mu>2$.

Integrating by parts in (3.5) gives $\int\left(e^{f+\varepsilon u}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$, so $\int e^{f}\left(e^{\varepsilon u}-1\right) \omega_{0}^{m}=0$ from integrability, thus $\inf u<0<\sup u$. If $\sup u$ was attained at $x_{\text {max }} \notin \operatorname{supp}(f)$ or $u\left(x_{k}\right) \rightarrow \sup u, x_{k} \rightarrow \infty$, then $\left(\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u\right)^{m}=e^{\varepsilon u} \omega_{0}^{m}$ at $x_{\text {max }}$ or at $x_{k}, k \gg 0$, a contradiction. We conclude that $\inf u, \sup u$ are attained at $x_{\text {min }}, x_{\text {max }} \in \operatorname{supp}(f)$. If $u_{\psi} \geq 0$, then $-C \leq u \leq u_{\psi}+C$ from before, and $u\left(x_{\text {min }}\right)<0$. Let $B$ denote the connected component of $x_{\text {min }}$ in $\{u<1+\min u\}$. Then $\Delta u \leq C$ on $B$ from (3.6), which yields $\int_{B} \psi \geq \delta \int_{M} \psi$ for some definite $\delta>0$, which may however depend on $\rho\left(x_{\text {min }}\right)$, cf. [73, Lemma 3.7]. Thus, $\max u \leq u_{\psi}+C \leq(1-\delta)(\max u)+C$, and so $\|u\|_{\infty}$ is bounded in terms of $\sup \rho^{\mu}|f|$, for some $\mu>2$, and the size of $\operatorname{supp}(f)$. A similar argument works if $u_{\psi} \leq 0$, with $\Delta u \geq-n$ applied near $x_{\text {max }}$.

Taking the limit as $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$, we obtain $u \in C^{4, \bar{\alpha}}(M)$ with $\left(\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u\right)^{m}=e^{f} \omega_{0}^{m}$. However, unlike before, our $C^{4, \bar{\alpha}}$ bound for $u$ now depends on the size of $\operatorname{supp}(f)$. To overcome this issue, we iterate again, but with $v:=u-u_{\psi}$, which does satisfy the same equation as $u$. The only issue now is the boundary term in (3.11), but we have $\int|\nabla u|^{2}<\infty$ as a by-product of the Moser iterations used in constructing $u$, which fortuitously suffices because the volume growth $\beta \leq 2$, cf. [73, p.596]. \(\square\)
3.4. Decay of the solutions. Our final goal here is to exhibit improved decay for bounded solutions $u$ of the $\mathbb{C}$ MA (3.2) under various hypotheses on the decay of $f$ and on the geometry of the complete Kähler manifold $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$.

Proposition 3.8 collects all the results that we need for proving the asymptotics in Theorem 1.5. The main underlying idea only works for quadratic volume growth or less, and under the additional assumption that $\int|\nabla u|^{2}<\infty$, but is fairly simple and universal. We write $M$ as a union of annuli $A_{i}=A\left(x_{0}, r_{i}, r_{i+1}\right)$ and try to use integration by parts to conclude $\int_{A_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2} \leq C e^{-\delta i}$ for some small $\delta=\delta(u, f)>0$.

This works whenever the $A_{i}$ have their first Neumann eigenvalues bounded below by $\frac{1}{C}\left(r_{i+1}-r_{i}\right)^{-2}$, and there are basically two different settings where we can make sure this is the case: (1) $A_{i} \subset B\left(x_{i}, \rho_{i}\right)$ with $x_{i} \in A_{i}$ and $\rho_{i} \sim r_{i+1}-r_{i}=o\left(r_{i}\right)$, and (2) $r_{i+1}-r_{i} \sim r_{i}$, assuming appropriate lower Ricci bounds in each case. The conditions needed for (1) are detailed in Definition 3.5, and they lead to exponentially decaying bounds, in terms of $r(x)$, for the integral of $|\nabla u|^{2}$ over $B(x, 1)$. For (2) it is enough to assume $\operatorname{SOB}(\beta)$ (Definition 2.1), and we obtain polynomial energy decay.

In each case, if the injectivity radius does not decay too fast, we can apply Moser iteration to get pointwise decay. Higher derivative bounds then follow from (scaled) Schauder estimates, in the setting described in Definition 3.6 and Lemma 3.7.

We make two remarks on this approach before going into details. First, the decay is qualitatively optimal in each case (cf. Remark 4.7(ii)), but the actual decay rates obtained will be unreasonably small. Second, we need to assume parabolic volume growth when integrating by parts, but non-parabolic manifolds ought to be better behaved. We will briefly address both issues after proving Proposition 3.8

Proof of the claim. To show $S(0)$, pull the equation $L_{u}\left(u_{x}\right)=e^{f}-1$ back under $\Phi_{x}$ and apply Schauder theory on $\Phi_{x}^{-1}\left(B\left(x, \frac{1}{C}\right)\right)$, using the $\Phi_{x}^{*} \omega_{0}$-Hölder norms. As we mentioned earlier, there are two obvious issues with Proposition 3.8: (1) The decay rates are not quantitatively optimal. (2) We need to assume low volume growth, but non-parabolic manifolds ought to be rather better behaved in terms of potential theory. We conclude here by briefly discussing these questions.
(1) Optimal decay rates in Proposition 3.8 (see also Remark 4.7(ii)).

On an asymptotically cylindrical manifold, if $-\lambda^{2}(\lambda>0)$ is the first eigenvalue of the cross-section, then separation of variables suggests $|u|+|\nabla u|+\ldots+\left|\nabla^{k} u\right| \leq$ $C e^{-\delta r}$ if $|f|+|\nabla f|+\ldots+\left|\nabla^{k} f\right| \leq C e^{-\varepsilon r}$, with $\delta=\varepsilon$ if $\varepsilon \in(0, \lambda)$, but no better than that (even if $\varepsilon \geq \lambda$ ) unless we impose extra integrability conditions on $f$. Similarly, on a space asymptotic to $C \times T^{n-2}$ (or even a flat $T^{n-2}$-submersion over $C$ ) for a flat 2-dimensional cone $C$ of cone angle $\theta \in(0,1]$, we expect $|u|+r|\nabla u|+\ldots+r^{k}\left|\nabla^{k} u\right| \leq$ $C r^{-\delta}$ if $|f|+r|\nabla f|+\ldots+r^{k}\left|\nabla^{k} f\right| \leq C r^{-2-\varepsilon}$, with $\delta=\varepsilon$ if $\varepsilon \in\left(0, \frac{1}{\theta}\right)$. In either case, Proposition 3.8 yields the right qualitative behavior, but not the best values for $\delta$. However, there is a relatively simple way of boosting the decay. The idea is that if $u$ did not decay fast enough compared to $f$, then after translating in the cylindrical, or rescaling and unwrapping the fibers in the homogeneous setting, thanks to the bounds established in Proposition 3.8, $u$ would converge to a harmonic function on $\mathbb{R}^{+} \times$cross-section or $C^{2} \times \mathbb{R}^{n-2}$ (constant in the $\mathbb{R}^{n-2}$ directions) with some and hence optimal decay, which contradicts the assumption. This outline can be made rigorous using "3-circles" type arguments, as in Cheeger-Tian [13]. \(\square\)
(2) Non-parabolic manifolds $(\beta>2)$.

Suppose $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ satisfies $\operatorname{SOB}(\beta)$ with $\beta>2$. Proposition 3.1 yields a bounded solution $u$ if $|f| \leq C r^{-\mu}$ for some $\mu>2$, however one would not expect $u$ to have finite energy outside of a certain range for $\mu$. On the other hand, from the behavior of the Laplacian on $\mathbb{R}^{\beta}$ and since the Green's function on $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ decays like $r^{2-\beta}$, one might hope for $|u| \leq C r^{2-\mu}$ if $\mu \in(2, \beta)$. We now describe two settings where this can be proved, though with a small loss in the exponent.
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] Barrier method (Santoro [67]). This is based on the observation that on $\mathbb{R}^{\beta}$,
$$\Delta\left(r^{2-\mu}(\log r)^{\varepsilon}\right)=r^{-\mu}(\log r)^{\varepsilon}\left[(2-\mu)(\beta-\mu)+\frac{\varepsilon(2+\beta-2 \mu)}{\log r}+\frac{\varepsilon(\varepsilon-1)}{(\log r)^{2}}\right],$$
\end{itemize}
which becomes negative for $r \gg 1$ if either $\mu \in(2, \beta), \varepsilon$ arbitrary, or $\mu=\beta, \varepsilon>0$. Thus, if $\Delta u=f$ with $|u|+|\nabla u|+\left|\nabla^{2} u\right| \leq C$ and $|f| \leq C r^{-\mu}(\log r)^{\varepsilon}, \mu \in(2, \beta)$, or $|f| \leq C r^{-\mu}(\log r)^{\varepsilon-1}, \mu=\beta, \varepsilon>0$, then $|u| \leq C r^{2-\mu}(\log r)^{\varepsilon}$ by Lemma 3.4. This idea obviously generalizes to various sorts of asymptotically flat submersions over $\mathbb{R}^{\beta}$ with bounded fibers, and also works for $\mathbb{C M A}$ on such spaces.
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] Moser iteration with weights. Let $\left(M, \omega_{0}\right)$ and $f$ be as in Proposition 3.1 and assume there exists a smooth $\rho \sim 1+r$ with $|d \rho|+\rho\left|d d^{c} \rho\right| \leq C$. By Section 3.2, for all $\varepsilon \in(0,1)$ there exists $u=u_{\varepsilon},\|u\|_{C^{4, \bar{\alpha}}(M)} \leq C$, with $\left(\omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u\right)^{m}=e^{f+\varepsilon u} \omega_{0}^{m}$. Moreover, assuming for the moment that $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$, by (3.10),
\end{itemize}
$$\int \rho^{k}|u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int \rho^{k}|u|^{p}<\infty \quad\left(k \in \mathbb{N}_{0}, p>1\right) .$$
For a cut-off function $\chi$ with $\chi(t)=1$ for $t<1, \chi(t)=0$ for $t>2$, multiply (3.3) by $\left(\chi \circ \frac{\rho}{R}\right) \zeta u|\zeta u|^{p-2} \zeta$, where $R>0, \zeta:=\rho^{l}$ for any $l \in \mathbb{R}$, and $p>1$. Commute the spare factor of $\zeta$ past the $d d^{c}$, integrate by parts, and let $R \rightarrow \infty$. The boundary term disappears by (3.22). Eventually, using $|d \rho|+\rho\left|d d^{c} \rho\right| \leq C, \omega_{0}+i \partial \bar{\partial} u \sim \omega_{0}$, and the sharp weighted Sobolev inequality (2.2),
$$\left(\int \rho^{\alpha(\beta-2)-\beta}|\zeta u|^{\alpha p}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}} \leq \frac{C p^{2}}{p-1}\left(\int|\zeta u|^{p-1} \zeta|f|+\frac{|l|(|l|+1) p}{p-1} \int \rho^{-2}|\zeta u|^{p}\right),$$
for any $\alpha \in\left[1, \alpha_{n 2}\right]$, with $C$ depending only on $\|f\|_{C^{2}(M)}$. This yields
$$\left(\int \zeta_{k+1}|u|^{p_{k+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{k+1}}} \leq\left(\frac{C_{0} C p_{k}^{3}}{\left(p_{k}-1\right)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{k}}} \max \left\{1,\left(\int \zeta_{k}|u|^{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{k}}}\right\}$$
for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$, where $p_{k}:=\alpha^{k} p_{0}$ with a fixed $p_{0}>1$, and $\zeta_{k}:=\rho^{\alpha^{k}(\beta-2)-\beta}$, assuming that $|f| \leq C_{0} \rho^{-\mu}, \mu>2, p_{0}(\mu-2)>\beta-2$, and $\alpha$ is close enough to 1 depending on $p_{0}, \beta, \mu$. The basic step, $k=1$, can be estimated from (3.13), so altogether
$$p_{0}>\frac{\beta-2}{\mu-2} \Longrightarrow|u|=\left|u_{\varepsilon}\right| \leq C\left(p_{0}, \beta, \mu,\|f\|_{C^{2}(M)}, \sup \rho^{\mu}|f|\right) \rho^{\frac{2-\beta}{p_{0}}} .$$
Therefore, $\left|u_{\varepsilon}\right| \leq C(\delta) \rho^{2-\mu+\delta}$ for all $\delta>0$ if $\mu \in(2, \beta)$, uniformly as $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$, and the assumption $f \in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)$ can be relaxed to $\|f\|_{C^{2, \alpha}(M)}+\sup \rho^{\mu}|f|<\infty$. \(\square\)

\section*{4. The new Calabi-Yau metrics}

This section leads up to the proof of Theorems 1.3 and 1.5 in Section 4.4.
Section 4.1 reviews the basic properties of rational elliptic surfaces and works out some isotrivial examples, which we expect to occur as bubbles in the collapsing of Ricci-flat metrics on K3, cf. Problem 6.1 We also summarize Kodaira's results from [42] on the structure of singular fibers in elliptic fibrations in general.

Section 4.2 describes a general construction of CY metrics on the total spaces of certain polarized families of complex $m$-tori. Given $\varepsilon>0$, a section of the family, and a holomorphic volume form $\Omega$ on the total space, we obtain a semi-flat (i.e. flat when restricted to fibers) CY metric $\omega_{\text {sf }, \varepsilon}$, with top power proportional to $\Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}$ and such that the tori have volume $\varepsilon$ with respect to $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$, which is explicit in terms of $\varepsilon, \Omega$, and the periods of the tori. Changing the reference section translates $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$.

Gross-Wilson [31] have used such metrics on the complement of the singular fibers of generic Jacobian elliptic K3 surfaces, with $\Omega=\Omega_{\mathrm{K} 3}$.

Section 4.3 looks at elliptic fibrations $f: U \rightarrow \Delta$ over a disk, with a section and such that the central fiber $D=f^{-1}(0)$ does not contain (-1)-curves. The fibration $f$ is then isomorphic to an explicit canonical form that can be extracted from [42]. Given a meromorphic 2-form $\Omega$ on $U$ such that $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)$ is an integer multiple of $D$, this then allows us to write down an explicit formula for the associated CY metrics $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ on $U \backslash D$ and read off their geometric properties. The most relevant cases are $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)=0$ as in [31], and $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)=-D$ as on a rational elliptic surface.

For a rational elliptic surface $f: X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$, a fiber $D=f^{-1}(p)$, a meromorphic 2-form $\Omega$ with $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)=-D$, and any choice of local section near $p$ we thus obtain CY metrics $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ on $U \backslash D, U=f^{-1}(\Delta), p \in \Delta \subset \mathbb{P}^{1}$, with $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}^{2}=\Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}$ and such that the fibers of $f$ have area $\varepsilon$ with respect to $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ and $D$ is at infinite distance. What remains to be done in Section 4.4 is glue $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ with as many different Kähler metrics on $X \backslash U$ as possible to produce complete Kähler metrics $\omega_{0}$ on $M=X \backslash D$ such that the existence theory from Section 3 applies with $e^{f}=(\Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}) / \omega_{0}^{2}$. The freedom of choosing a local section for $f$ is exactly what makes the Kähler gluing possible, thanks to a remarkable $\partial \bar{\partial}$-type lemma for $U \backslash D$ from [31].
4.1. Rational elliptic surfaces and singular fibers. Besides Kodaira's original work [42], useful references for what follows are [4], [26], [32], and [57].

Consider a rational elliptic surface $X$ as in Definition 1.1, i.e. the blow-up of $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ in the nine base points of a pencil $s F+t G=0$, where $(s: t) \in \mathbb{P}^{1}$ and $F$ and $G$ are smooth cubics. Then $X=\mathbb{P}^{2} \# 9 \overline{\mathbb{P}}^{2}$ as smooth manifolds, and the Hodge numbers of $X$ are $h^{1,0}=h^{2,0}=0$ and $h^{1,1}=10$. The natural elliptic fibration $f: X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$ has an intrinsic description as the complete linear system $\left|-K_{X}\right|$, meaning that for each fiber $D=f^{-1}(p)$ there exists a meromorphic 2-form $\Omega$ on $X$, unique up to a scale, with $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)=-D$, and all meromorphic 2-forms on $X$ are obtained in this way. Moreover, $f$ is the only elliptic fibration on $X$. When blowing up a base point until all tangencies are resolved, the last exceptional divisor, which is a (-1)-curve, becomes a global section of $f$. All fibers of $f$ are reduced, i.e. the multiplicities of $f$ along the irreducible components of any fiber are coprime. A fiber $f^{-1}(p)$ in a general elliptic fibration is reduced if and only if $f$ admits a local section near $p$.

Rational elliptic surfaces are characterized as those smooth projective surfaces which admit a meromorphic 2-form $\Omega$ and an elliptic fibration whose fibers do not contain $(-1)$-curves such that $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)$ is an integer multiple of a fiber. Even though our construction of complete CY metrics only requires such a fibration near infinity, this essentially shows that we do not have any other examples to work with.

As a last general point, notice that the space of cubics in $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ is a $\mathbb{P}^{9}$, so a general set of 9 points in $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ determines a unique cubic passing through it, and the space of 9-point subsets of $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ modulo PGl(3) has dimension $9 \cdot 2-8=10$. On the other hand, if cubics $F$ and $G$ intersect in finitely many points, and $H$ is another cubic passing through these, then, by the $A F+B G$ theorem, $H$ must already be a linear combination of $F$ and $G$. Thus, modulo PGl(3) again, 9-point sets with more than one cubic passing through them depend on $2 \cdot(9-1)-8=8$ free parameters only. If we blow up $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ in a 9-point set which does not determine a pencil of cubics, the resulting $X$ still has meromorphic 2-forms $\Omega$, and sometimes an elliptic fibration, but then one fiber $D$ must be of multiplicity $m>1$ and $\operatorname{div}(\Omega)$ is either $-\frac{1}{m} D$ or disconnected, so our construction of complete CY metrics breaks down.

Example 4.1. Let $E=\mathbb{C} / \Lambda$ be a complex torus and let $\Gamma$ be a finite subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(E)$, so $\Gamma$ is generated by a root of unity and either $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ (for any $\Lambda$ ), $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{4}$ (for $\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}[i]$ ), or $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{3}, \mathbb{Z}_{6}$ (for $\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}\left[\zeta_{3}\right]$ ), $\zeta_{m}:=\exp (2 \pi i / m)$. Then $\Gamma$ acts on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ in a natural way, and the minimal resolution $X$ of the singular surface $\left(\mathbb{P}^{1} \times E\right) / \Gamma$ comes with an obvious elliptic fibration $f: X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}$ with two singular fibers and all smooth fibers isomorphic to $E$. Blowing down all (-1)-curves in the singular fibers makes $X$ rational elliptic. Below, we work this out explicitly for $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2}, \mathbb{Z}_{3}$.

Elliptic surfaces of this sort are called isotrivial, and are of special interest in our story for several reasons. First, they explain the mechanism of creation of singular fibers of finite monodromy in arbitrary elliptic fibrations, and the occurrence of such fiber types in dual pairs T, T* with the same monodromy $\left(\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{T}^{*}\right.$ if $\left.\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2} \subset \mathbb{R}\right)$. Second, only the T*-types are associated with crepant resolutions, so only in these cases (i.e. with T* at the core and T at infinity) does the complete semi-flat model metric $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ come from the flat orbifold metric on $(\mathbb{C} \times E) / \Gamma$. Third, we expect the associated ALG spaces to occur as bubbles for collapsing CY metrics on elliptic K3 surfaces near finite monodromy singular fibers of the appropriate type.
(i) $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2}$. Fix a smooth elliptic curve $E: y^{2}=g(x)$ where $g$ is a cubic, and fix an affine coordinate $t$ on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ such that the natural $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-action on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ becomes $t \mapsto-t$. Setting $(T, X, Y)=\left(t^{2}, x, t y\right)$ yields a rational map $\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{P}_{T}^{1} \times \mathbb{P}_{X Y}^{2}$ which factors through the globally defined $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-action $(t, x, y) \mapsto(-t, x,-y)$ and induces a birational map from $\left(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E\right) / \mathbb{Z}_{2}$ to the surface $S: Y^{2}=T g(X)$ which is in fact an isomorphism of elliptic fibrations away from $t=0, \infty$. Project $S$ onto $\mathbb{P}_{X Y}^{2}$ and blow up the base points of the pencil $Y^{2}=T g(X)$. Going back then already yields a resolution for $\left(\mathbb{P}^{1} \times E\right) / \mathbb{Z}_{2}$, without having to blow up in a fiber first.

To see what this looks like, notice the base points are $P_{i}=\left(x_{i}, 0\right)=\left(x_{i}: 0: 1\right)$, $i=1,2,3$, where $g\left(x_{i}\right)=0$, and $P_{0}=(0: 1: 0)$. At $P_{i}$, the curves in the pencil have a common tangent $L_{i}: X=x_{i}$ of multiplicity 2, while at $P_{0}$, they share the line at infinity $L_{0}$ as a flex tangent. We blow up $P_{i}$ twice with exceptional curves $E_{i}^{1}, E_{i}^{2}$, and $P_{0}$ three times with exceptional curves $E_{0}^{1}, E_{0}^{2}, E_{0}^{3}$. The resulting elliptic surface then has four sections, given by the (-1)-curves $E_{i}^{2}$ and $E_{0}^{3}$, and two singular fibers. The one at $T=0$ consists of the "spine" $Y=0$, plus the four "ribs" $E_{i}^{1}, L_{0}$, which intersect the spine transversely in four different points. The singular fiber at $T=\infty$ looks the same, with $E_{0}^{1}$ as spine and $L_{i}, E_{0}^{2}$ as ribs. Both are of Kodaira type $\mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}$. In the resolution picture, the spines are the strict transforms of the rational curves $E / \mathbb{Z}_{2} \subset\left(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E\right) / \mathbb{Z}_{2}$ over $t=0, \infty$, while the ribs resolve the singularities.

It is clear what background metric one would have to take in order to construct a complete Calabi-Yau metric when removing either one of the singular fibers. There are flat metrics on $\mathbb{C}_{t} \times E, \mathbb{C}_{s} \times E\left(s=\frac{1}{t}\right)$ with corresponding 2-forms $\Omega_{0}=d t \wedge d w$, $\Omega_{\infty}=d s \wedge d w$, where $d w$ denotes an invariant differential on $E$. The key point is that $\Omega_{0}, \Omega_{\infty}$ are both invariant under the $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-action, hence they lift to the rational elliptic surface with polar divisor the singular fiber at $T=\infty, 0$, respectively. Thus one can use the flat orbifold $(\mathbb{C} \times E) / \mathbb{Z}_{2}$ as a background. Settings of this sort have been treated in [5, 67] and heuristically in [27, 36], cf. Remark 1.6(ii).
(ii) $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{3}$. Take $E: y^{2}+y=x^{3}$ and $(x, y) \mapsto\left(\zeta_{3} x, y\right)$ as a generator for $\Gamma$. Fix an affine coordinate $t$ on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ such that the natural $\mathbb{Z}_{3}$-action on $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ becomes $t \mapsto \zeta_{3}^{2} t$. Then the rational map $\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{P}_{T}^{1} \times \mathbb{P}_{X Y}^{2}$ given by $(T, X, Y)=\left(t^{3}, t x, y\right)$ factors through the diagonal $\Gamma$-action and thus induces a birational map from $\left(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E\right) / \Gamma$ to the surface $T\left(Y^{2}+Y\right)=X^{3}$ in $\mathbb{P}_{T}^{1} \times \mathbb{P}_{X Y}^{2}$. The pencil of cubics obtained from
this by projection has three base points of multiplicity 3: $P_{1}=(0,0)=(0: 0: 1)$, $P_{2}=(0,-1)=(0:-1: 1)$, and $P_{3}=(0: 1: 0)$, with corresponding flex tangents $L_{1}: Y=0, L_{2}: Y=-1$, and $L_{3}$, the line at infinity. Blowing up therefore produces three exceptional divisors $E_{i}^{1}, E_{i}^{2}, E_{i}^{3}$ at every $P_{i}$, where the $E_{i}^{3}$ become sections. The resulting singular fiber over $T=0$ has a spine-rib structure again, with $X=0$ as spine and three two-component ribs $E_{i}^{1} \cup E_{i}^{2}$ (Kodaira type IV*). The fiber over $T=\infty$ consists of $L_{1}, L_{2}, L_{3}$, intersecting in $(1: 0: 0) \in L_{3}$ (Kodaira type IV). To regard this as a resolution of singularities for $\left(\mathbb{P}^{1} \times E\right) / \Gamma$, we need to blow up the intersection of $L_{1}, L_{2}, L_{3}$ over $T=\infty$, the new exceptional divisor then becoming the strict transform of the rational curve $E / \Gamma \subset\left(\mathbb{P}^{1} \times E\right) / \Gamma$ over $t=\infty$.

As for the semi-flat background metrics on $X$ with one singular fiber removed, there is a new phenomenon compared to the $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ case. Of the meromorphic 2-forms $\Omega_{0}=d t \wedge d w, \Omega_{\infty}=d s \wedge d w\left(s=\frac{1}{t}\right)$ on $\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E$ associated to the standard flat metrics on $\mathbb{C}_{t} \times E, \mathbb{C}_{s} \times E$, only $\Omega_{0}$ is invariant under the $\mathbb{Z}_{3}$-action we are looking at, which is generated by $(t, w) \mapsto\left(\zeta_{3}^{2} t, \zeta_{3} w\right)$ or $(s, w) \mapsto\left(\zeta_{3} s, \zeta_{3} w\right)$ (cf. the three two-component ribs $E_{i}^{1} \cup E_{i}^{2}$ in the singular fiber over $T=0$, which correspond to crepant resolutions of the three $A_{2}$-singularities of $\left(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{1} \times E\right) / \Gamma$ over $t=0$ ). Thus, when removing the type IV fiber over $T=\infty$, then obviously the right metric $\omega_{0}$ to use as a background is the flat orbifold metric on $\left(\mathbb{C}_{t} \times E\right) / \Gamma$, which is a flat $T^{2}$ -submersion over a flat 2-cone of cone angle 1/3. On the other hand, when removing the type IV* fiber over $T=0$, we would need to find $\omega_{\infty}$ with $\omega_{\infty}^{2}=\Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}$, where $\Omega=\frac{1}{T} \Omega_{0}$ is the unique meromorphic 2-form on the rational elliptic surface with a simple pole along that fiber. Thus it seems reasonable to take $\omega_{0}$ and conformally change the flat cone metric $|T|^{-\frac{4}{3}}|d T|^{2}$ on the base (complete at $T=\infty$ ) by $|T|^{-2}$, which results in a flat cone metric (complete at $T=0$ ) of cone angle 2/3. It follows from Sections 4.2-4.3 that this is indeed what the appropriate $\omega_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ looks like. \(\square\)

We now turn to the possible structures of singular fibers in general. The principal result in Kodaira [42] asserts the following: Let $f: U \rightarrow \Delta$ be an elliptic fibration with a section $\sigma$ over the unit disk such that all fibers except possibly $D=f^{-1}(0)$ are smooth and $D$ does not contain any (-1)-curves (recall that existence of a local section is equivalent to $D$ being reduced). Then the pair $(f, \sigma)$ is isomorphic to a canonical form $(\bar{f}, \bar{\sigma})$ whose total space $\bar{U}$ is birational, via explicit fiber-preserving maps, to the quotient of the total space of an explicit elliptic fibration over $\Delta$ by a finite group related to the monodromy of $f$. To explain the notion of monodromy, note $\left.U\right|_{\Delta^{*}} \cong L / \Lambda$ due to the existence of $\sigma$, where $L$ is a holomorphic line bundle on $\Delta^{*}$ and $\Lambda \subset L$ is a lattice bundle, but $H^{1}\left(\Delta^{*}, \mathcal{O}^{*}\right)=0$, so $L$ is trivial and $\left.U\right|_{\Delta^{*}} \cong$ $\left(\Delta^{*} \times \mathbb{C}\right) /\left(\mathbb{Z} \tau_{1}+\mathbb{Z} \tau_{2}\right)$ for multi-valued holomorphic functions $\tau_{1}, \tau_{2}$ on $\Delta^{*}$. The pair $T=\left(\tau_{1}, \tau_{2}\right) \in \mathbb{C}^{2 \times 1}$, assumed to be positively oriented, transforms as $T \mapsto T A$ with $A=\left(\begin{array}{cc}a & b \\ c & d\end{array}\right) \in \operatorname{Sl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$ when going around the puncture once in the counterclockwise sense. The conjugacy class of $A$ is then referred to as the monodromy of $f$.

The central fibers that can occur are classified by topological type as $\mathrm{I}_{b}(b \geq 0)$, II, III, IV, and corresponding "*-types," $\mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}$, etc. $\mathrm{I}_{0}$ is smooth, $\mathrm{I}_{1}$ a node, II a cusp, III is two rational curves touching at a point, IV three rational curves intersecting transversely in a point, and $\mathrm{I}_{b}(b \geq 2), \mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}(b \geq 0), \mathrm{II}^{*}, \mathrm{III}^{*}, \mathrm{IV}^{*}$ are graphs of rational curves which if weighted by intersection numbers become affine Dynkin diagrams: $\tilde{A}_{b-1}, \tilde{D}_{b+4}, \tilde{E}_{8}, \tilde{E}_{7}, \tilde{E}_{6}$. The topological type determines the monodromy, whose order in $\operatorname{Sl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$ is finite except for $\mathrm{I}_{b}, \mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}(b \geq 1)$. In Example 4.1 we have seen how the pairs $\left(\mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}, \mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}\right)$, (IV, $\left.\mathrm{IV}^{*}\right)$ occur in rational elliptic surfaces birational to $\left(\mathbb{P}^{1} \times E\right) / \Gamma$
with $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2}, \mathbb{Z}_{3}$. The pairs (II, II*), (III, III*) can be created in a similar way with $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{6}, \mathbb{Z}_{4}$. In each case the monodromy of both fibers is $\Gamma$. The $T^{2}$-bundles over $S^{1}$ obtained by restricting $U$ to a loop around 0 are orientable. They are Bieberbach manifolds (five out of six possible ones occur) if the monodromy is trivial or finite, nil-manifolds for $\mathrm{I}_{b}, b \geq 1$ (all possibilities occur), and infranil for $\mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}, b \geq 1$.

If $X$ is rational elliptic, then the Euler numbers of the singular fibers must add up to $\chi(X)=12$. Persson [58] has determined all possible configurations of singular fibers; there are 279 in total, the generic one being $12 \times \mathrm{I}_{1}$. All finite monodromy types can occur, as well as $\mathrm{I}_{b}, 0 \leq b \leq 9(\chi=b)$ and $\mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}, 0 \leq b \leq 4(\chi=6+b)$.

We need to understand how Kodaira's canonical form $(\bar{f}, \bar{\sigma})$ for a given fibration $(f, \sigma)$ with a section is constructed. Write $\left.U\right|_{\Delta^{*}}=\left(\Delta^{*} \times \mathbb{C}\right) /\left(\mathbb{Z} \tau_{1}+\mathbb{Z} \tau_{2}\right)$ as before. Then $\tau:=\tau_{2} / \tau_{1}: \Delta^{*} \rightarrow \mathfrak{H}$ is multi-valued holomorphic, with values in the upper half-plane, and transforms as $\tau \mapsto(d \tau+b) /(c \tau+a)$ under the image $[A] \in \operatorname{PSl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$ of the monodromy when going around the puncture in the counterclockwise sense. Denote by $j: \mathfrak{H} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ the classical elliptic modular function, normalized such that $j(i)=1$ and $j\left(\zeta_{3}\right)=0$. Recall that $j$ is $\operatorname{PSl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$-invariant and unramified except for branch points of order 2,3 along the orbits of $i, \zeta_{3}$. It turns out that $\mathcal{J}:=j \circ \tau$ (Kodaira's functional invariant) is a single-valued meromorphic function on $\Delta^{*}$.
- If $\mathcal{J}(0) \in \mathbb{C} \backslash\{0,1\}$, then $\tau$ is single-valued and extends to a regular function on $\Delta$ with $\tau(0)$ not in the $\operatorname{PSl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$-orbits of $i$ or $\zeta_{3}$. Thus in particular the stabilizer of $\tau(0)$ in $\operatorname{PSl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$ is trivial, so $A= \pm 1$. If $A=1$, then $\tau_{1}, \tau_{2}$ extend as single-valued functions on $\Delta$ and $U \cong(\Delta \times \mathbb{C}) /\left(\mathbb{Z} \tau_{1}+\mathbb{Z} \tau_{2}\right)$ with $D$ smooth $\left(\mathrm{I}_{0}\right)$. If $A=-1$, we make a base change $z=u^{2}$ over $\Delta_{z}^{*}$. The lifted fibration $U^{\prime} \rightarrow \Delta_{u}^{*}$ extends across 0 with a smooth central fiber $D^{\prime}$. The free $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-action on $U^{\prime}$ over $\Delta_{u}^{*}$ also extends but with four fixed points on $D^{\prime}$. The quotient is then an elliptic fibration over $\Delta_{z}$, isomorphic to $U$ over $\Delta_{z}^{*}$, but with four surface singularities of type $A_{1}$ over $z=0$. Resolving these singularities gives the desired normal form $\bar{U} \rightarrow \Delta_{z}$ with a type $\mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}$ central fiber, in analogy with Example 4.1(i).
- If $\mathcal{J}(0)=0$, then $\tau$ is possibly multi-valued but still regular at 0 with $\tau(0)$ in the orbit of $\zeta_{3}$. $[A]$ fixes $\tau(0)$, which yields six possibilities total for $A \in \operatorname{Sl}(2, \mathbb{Z})$ up to conjugation, including $A= \pm 1$. Each case can be treated as before: Make a base change $z=u^{\text {ord } A}$ to obtain an elliptic fibration $U^{\prime} \rightarrow \Delta_{u}^{*}$ which extends to $\Delta_{u}$ with a smooth central fiber $D^{\prime}$. The group $\langle A\rangle$ acts on $U^{\prime}$ with fixed points on $D^{\prime}$, and the desired normal form $\bar{U}$ is obtained by minimally resolving the singularities of the quotient and blowing down (-1)-curves in the central fiber if necessary. The case $\mathcal{J}(0)=1$ is the same. Other than $\mathrm{I}_{0}, \mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}$ for $A= \pm 1$, the fiber types that can occur are II, III, IV, and their corresponding *-types, as in Example 4.1(ii).
- If $\mathcal{J}$ has a pole of order $b \geq 1$ at 0 , then $A$ is conjugate to $\pm A_{b}= \pm\left(\begin{array}{ll}1 & b \\ 0 & 1\end{array}\right)$. Also, it is possible to make $\tau_{1} \equiv 1$ and $\tau_{2}=\tau=b(\log z) /(2 \pi i)$. If $b=1$ and $A=A_{1}$, one can compactify $\left.U\right|_{\Delta^{*}} \cong\left(\Delta^{*} \times \mathbb{C}\right) /(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z} \tau)$ explicitly by embedding it into $\Delta \times \mathbb{P}^{2}$ using suitable $\wp$-functions and taking the closure. This yields the normal form $\bar{U}$ with central fiber a node $\left(\mathrm{I}_{1}\right)$. The case $A=A_{b}, b>1$, with central fiber a cycle of $b$ rational curves $\left(\mathrm{I}_{b}\right)$, reduces to $b=1$ by a fiberwise $b$-fold cover. $A=-A_{b}$ implies $A^{2}=A_{2 b}$, and $\bar{U}$ is obtained by setting $z=u^{2}$, which yields an $\mathrm{I}_{2 b}$ fibration over $\Delta_{u}$, then dividing by $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ and resolving the four resulting $A_{1}$-singularities $\left(\mathrm{I}_{b}^{*}\right)$.
4.2 . Ricci-flat metrics on complex torus bundles. Let $f: X \rightarrow S$ be a holomorphic submersion such that all fibers $X_{s}=f^{-1}(s)$ are complex $m$-tori. Assume $f$ admits a holomorphic section $\sigma$ and a constant polarization $\omega$, meaning that $\omega$ is a
real 2-form on $X$ which restricts to a Kähler form on every $X_{s}$ and there exist $c_{i} \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\xi_{i}(s) \in H^{2}\left(X_{s}, \mathbb{Z}\right)$ such that $\left[\left.\omega\right|_{X_{s}}\right]=\sum c_{i} \xi_{i}(s)$ for all $s \in S$. For $\varepsilon>0, s \in S$ we denote by $g_{s, \varepsilon}$ the unique flat Kähler metric on $X_{s}$ with $\operatorname{vol}\left(X_{s}, g_{s, \varepsilon}\right)=\varepsilon$ whose Kähler class is a scalar multiple of $\left[\left.\omega\right|_{X_{s}}\right]$. Restricting $g_{s, \varepsilon}$ to $T_{\sigma(s)} X_{s}$ then induces a hermitian fiber metric $h_{\varepsilon}$ on the holomorphic vector bundle $E:=\sigma^{*} T_{X / S}$ over $S$, according to our convention that $g$ induces $h=\frac{1}{2}(g+i \omega)$.
- Hyperkähler setting. Let $\operatorname{dim} S=m$ and assume that $X$ admits a holomorphic symplectic form $\Upsilon$ such that all $X_{s}$ are complex Lagrangians. Then there exists a unique Riemannian metric $g_{S, \varepsilon}$ on $S$ compatible with the complex structure on $S$ such that the faithful pairing $E \otimes T^{1,0} S \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ defined by $\Upsilon$ is isometric with respect to $h_{\varepsilon}$ and the hermitian metric induced by $g_{S, \varepsilon}$.
- $C Y$ setting. Let $\operatorname{dim} S=1$ and assume that $X$ admits a holomorphic volume form $\Omega$. Then $\Omega$ defines a faithful pairing $\wedge^{m} E \otimes T^{1,0} S \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$. Using the hermitian metric on $\wedge^{m} E$ induced by $h_{\varepsilon}$, we obtain a Riemannian metric $g_{S, \varepsilon}$ as before.

Using $g_{S, \varepsilon}$ and the family of flat metrics $\left\{g_{s, \varepsilon}\right\}$ on the fibers of $f$, we now wish to build a submersion metric on $X$, thus we need to specify a horizontal distribution. Choose a fiber-preserving biholomorphism $X \cong E / \Lambda$ for some holomorphic lattice bundle $\Lambda \subset E$. Then $\Lambda$ induces a flat $\mathbb{R}$-linear connection on $E$, hence an integrable horizontal distribution $\mathcal{H}$ on $X$, and this is independent of how we made $X \cong E / \Lambda$ since any two such isomorphisms only differ by a holomorphic section $S \rightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(E)$. Then $g_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}(u, v):=g_{S, \varepsilon}\left(f_{*} u, f_{*} v\right)+g_{s, \varepsilon}(P u, P v)$ for $u, v \in T_{x} X, s=f(x)$, and $P=$ projection along $\mathcal{H}_{x}$, defines a "semi-flat" hermitian metric $g_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ on $X$. We will see that $g_{S, \varepsilon}, g_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ are in fact Kähler and the volume form of $g_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ is a constant multiple of either $\Upsilon^{m} \wedge \bar{\Upsilon}^{m}$ or $\Omega \wedge \bar{\Omega}$, so that $g_{\mathrm{sf}, \varepsilon}$ is either hyperkähler or at least CY.
