# Notes on the classification of complex Lie algebras

27 April, 2013 in [expository](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/expository/), [math.RA](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathra/), [math.RT](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/category/mathematics/mathrt/) \| Tags: [Cartan decomposition](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/cartan-decomposition/), [Chevalley basis](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/chevalley-basis/), [Dynkin diagram](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/dynkin-diagram/), [Killing form](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/killing-form/), [Lie algebras](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/lie-algebras/), [roots](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/roots/), [semisimplicity](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/semisimplicity/), [structure](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/tag/structure/) \| by [Terence Tao](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/author/teorth/)

An [abstract finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra), or _Lie algebra_ for short, is a finite-dimensional complex vector space ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) together with an anti-symmetric bilinear form ![{[,] = [,]_{\mathfrak g}: {\mathfrak g} \times {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%2C%5D+%3D+%5B%2C%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Ctimes+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) that obeys the [Jacobi identity](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_identity)

![\displaystyle [[x,y],z] + [[y,z],x] + [[z,x],y] = 0 \ \ \ \ \ (1)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%5B%5Bx%2Cy%5D%2Cz%5D+%2B+%5B%5By%2Cz%5D%2Cx%5D+%2B+%5B%5Bz%2Cx%5D%2Cy%5D+%3D+0+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%281%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

for all ![{x,y,z \in {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%2Cy%2Cz+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); by anti-symmetry one can also rewrite the Jacobi identity as

![\displaystyle [x,[y,z]] = [[x,y],z] + [y,[x,z]]. \ \ \ \ \ (2)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%5Bx%2C%5By%2Cz%5D%5D+%3D+%5B%5Bx%2Cy%5D%2Cz%5D+%2B+%5By%2C%5Bx%2Cz%5D%5D.+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%282%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

We will usually omit the subscript from the Lie bracket ![{[,]_{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%2C%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) when this will not cause ambiguity. A _homomorphism_![{\phi: {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Cphi%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) between two Lie algebras ![{{\mathfrak g},{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is a linear map that respects the Lie bracket, thus ![{\phi([x,y]_{\mathfrak g}) =[\phi(x),\phi(y)]_{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Cphi%28%5Bx%2Cy%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29+%3D%5B%5Cphi%28x%29%2C%5Cphi%28y%29%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for all ![{x,y \in {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%2Cy+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). As with many other classes of mathematical objects, the class of Lie algebras together with their homomorphisms then form a [category](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_(mathematics)). One can of course also consider Lie algebras in infinite dimension or over other fields, but we will restrict attention throughout these notes to the finite-dimensional complex case. The trivial, zero-dimensional Lie algebra is denoted ![{0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); Lie algebras of positive dimension will be called _non-trivial_.

Lie algebras come up in many contexts in mathematics, in particular arising as the tangent space of complex [Lie groups](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group). It is thus very profitable to think of Lie algebras as being the infinitesimal component of a Lie group, and in particular almost all of the notation and concepts that are applicable to Lie groups (e.g. nilpotence, solvability, extensions, etc.) have infinitesimal counterparts in the category of Lie algebras (often with exactly the same terminology). See [this previous blog post](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2011/09/01/254a-notes-1-lie-groups-lie-algebras-and-the-baker-campbell-hausdorff-formula/) for more discussion about the connection between Lie algebras and Lie groups (that post was focused over the reals instead of the complexes, but much of the discussion carries over to the complex case).

A particular example of a Lie algebra is the general linear Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28V%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of linear transformations ![{x: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on a finite-dimensional complex vector space (or _vector space_ for short) ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), with the commutator Lie bracket ![{[x,y] := xy-yx}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Bx%2Cy%5D+%3A%3D+xy-yx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); one easily verifies that this is indeed an abstract Lie algebra. We will define a _concrete_ Lie algebra to be a Lie algebra that is a subalgebra of ![{{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28V%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for some vector space ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and similarly define a _representation_ of a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to be a homomorphism ![{\rho: {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) into a concrete Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). It is a deep [theorem of Ado](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ado%27s_theorem) (discussed in [this previous post](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2011/05/10/ados-theorem/)) that every abstract Lie algebra is in fact isomorphic to a concrete one (or equivalently, that every abstract Lie algebra has a faithful representation), but we will not need or prove this fact here.

Even without Ado’s theorem, though, the structure of abstract Lie algebras is very well understood. As with objects in many other algebraic categories, a basic way to understand a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is to factor it into two simpler algebras ![{{\mathfrak h}, {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) via a [short exact sequence](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence#Short_exact_sequence)

![\displaystyle 0 \rightarrow {\mathfrak h} \rightarrow {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\mathfrak k} \rightarrow 0, \ \ \ \ \ (3)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+0+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%5Crightarrow+0%2C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%283%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

thus one has an injective homomorphism from ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and a surjective homomorphism from ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) such that the image of the former homomorphism is the kernel of the latter. (To be pedantic, a short exact sequence in a general category requires these homomorphisms to be [monomorphisms](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomorphism) and [epimorphisms](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epimorphism) respectively, but in the category of Lie algebras these turn out to reduce to the more familiar concepts of injectivity and surjectivity respectively.) Given such a sequence, one can (non-uniquely) identify ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) with the vector space ![{{\mathfrak h} \times {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Ctimes+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) equipped with a Lie bracket of the form

![\displaystyle [(t,x), (s,y)]_{\mathfrak g} = ([t,s]_{\mathfrak h} + A(t,y) - A(s,x) + B(x,y), [x,y]_{\mathfrak k}) \ \ \ \ \ (4)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%5B%28t%2Cx%29%2C+%28s%2Cy%29%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%28%5Bt%2Cs%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%2B+A%28t%2Cy%29+-+A%28s%2Cx%29+%2B+B%28x%2Cy%29%2C+%5Bx%2Cy%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%29+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%284%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

for some bilinear maps ![{A: {\mathfrak h} \times {\mathfrak k} \rightarrow {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BA%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Ctimes+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{B: {\mathfrak k} \times {\mathfrak k} \rightarrow {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BB%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%5Ctimes+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) that obey some Jacobi-type identities which we will not record here. Understanding exactly what maps ![{A,B}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BA%2CB%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) are possible here (up to coordinate change) can be a difficult task (and is one of the key objectives of [Lie algebra cohomology](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra_cohomology)), but in principle at least, the problem of understanding ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) can be reduced to that of understanding that of its factors ![{{\mathfrak k}, {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). To emphasise this, I will (perhaps idiosyncratically) express the existence of a short exact sequence [(3)](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2013/04/27/notes-on-the-classification-of-complex-lie-algebras/#exact) by the [ATLAS](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATLAS_of_Finite_Groups)-type notation

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = {\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k} \ \ \ \ \ (5)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%285%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

although one should caution that for given ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), there can be multiple non-isomorphic ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) that can form a short exact sequence with ![{{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), so that ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is not a uniquely defined combination of ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); one could emphasise this by writing ![{{\mathfrak h} ._{A,B} {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+._%7BA%2CB%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) instead of ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), though we will not do so here. We will refer to ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) as an _extension_ of ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) by ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and read the notation [(5)](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2013/04/27/notes-on-the-classification-of-complex-lie-algebras/#ghk) as “ ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)-by-![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)“; confusingly, these two notations reverse the subject and object of “by”, but unfortunately both notations are well entrenched in the literature. We caution that the operation ![{.}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B.%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is not commutative, and it is only partly associative: every Lie algebra of the form ![{{\mathfrak k} . ({\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak l})}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+.+%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+l%7D%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is also of the form ![{({\mathfrak k} . {\mathfrak h}) . {\mathfrak l}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%29+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+l%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), but the converse is not true (see [this previous blog post](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2010/01/23/some-notes-on-group-extensions/) for some related discussion). As we are working in the infinitesimal world of Lie algebras (which have an additive group operation) rather than Lie groups (in which the group operation is usually written multiplicatively), it may help to think of ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) as a (twisted) “sum” of ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) rather than a “product”; for instance, we have ![{{\mathfrak g} = 0 . {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+0+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{{\mathfrak g} = {\mathfrak g} . 0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+.+0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and also ![{\dim {\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k} = \dim {\mathfrak h} + \dim {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Cdim+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%3D+%5Cdim+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%2B+%5Cdim+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).

![{{\mathfrak h} \oplus {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Coplus+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (also denoted ![{{\mathfrak h} \times {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Ctimes+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)), which is given by the construction is given by the construction [(4)](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2013/04/27/notes-on-the-classification-of-complex-lie-algebras/#xoi) with for some representation ![{\rho: {\mathfrak k} \rightarrow \hbox{Der} {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D+%5Crightarrow+%5Chbox%7BDer%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) in the concrete Lie algebra of [derivations](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_(abstract_algebra))![{\hbox{Der} {\mathfrak h} \subset {\mathfrak{gl}}({\mathfrak h})}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7BDer%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Csubset+%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), that is to say the algebra of linear maps ![{D: {\mathfrak h} \rightarrow {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BD%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) that obey the Leibniz rule

![\displaystyle D[s,t]_{\mathfrak h} = [Ds,t]_{\mathfrak h} + [s,Dt]_{\mathfrak h}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+D%5Bs%2Ct%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%3D+%5BDs%2Ct%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%2B+%5Bs%2CDt%5D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

for all ![{s,t \in {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bs%2Ct+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). (The derivation algebra ![{\hbox{Der} {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7BDer%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is analogous to the [automorphism group](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphism_group)![{\hbox{Aut}(G)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7BAut%7D%28G%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of a Lie group ![{G}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BG%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), with the two concepts being intertwined by the tangent space functor ![{G \mapsto {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BG+%5Cmapsto+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) from Lie groups to Lie algebras (i.e. the derivation algebra is the infinitesimal version of the automorphism group). Of course, this functor also intertwines the Lie algebra and Lie group versions of most of the other concepts discussed here, such as extensions, semidirect products, etc.)

There are two general ways to factor a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) as an extension ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of a smaller Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) by another smaller Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). One is to locate a [Lie algebra ideal](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra#Homomorphisms.2C_subalgebras.2C_and_ideals) (or _ideal_ for short) ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) in ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), thus ![{[{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak g}] \subset {\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D+%5Csubset+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), where ![{[{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak g}]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) denotes the Lie algebra generated by ![{\{ [x,y]: x \in {\mathfrak h}, y \in {\mathfrak g} \}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5C%7B+%5Bx%2Cy%5D%3A+x+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C+y+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and then take ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to be the quotient space ![{{\mathfrak g}/{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2F%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) in the usual manner; one can check that ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) are also Lie algebras and that we do indeed have a short exact sequence

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = {\mathfrak h} . ({\mathfrak g}/{\mathfrak h}).](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2F%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%29.&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

Conversely, whenever one has a factorisation ![{{\mathfrak g} = {\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), one can identify ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) with an ideal in ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) with the quotient of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) by ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).

The other general way to obtain such a factorisation is is to start with a homomorphism ![{\rho: {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\mathfrak m}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+m%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) into another Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak m}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+m%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), take ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to be the image ![{\rho({\mathfrak g})}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to be the kernel ![{\hbox{ker} \rho := \{ x \in {\mathfrak g}: \rho(x) = 0 \}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Bker%7D+%5Crho+%3A%3D+%5C%7B+x+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%3A+%5Crho%28x%29+%3D+0+%5C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). Again, it is easy to see that this does indeed create a short exact sequence:

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = \hbox{ker} \rho . \rho({\mathfrak g}).](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%5Chbox%7Bker%7D+%5Crho+.+%5Crho%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29.&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

Conversely, whenever one has a factorisation ![{{\mathfrak g} = {\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), one can identify ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) with the image of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) under some homomorphism, and ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) with the kernel of that homomorphism. Note that if a representation ![{\rho: {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\mathfrak m}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak+m%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is [faithful](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_representation) (i.e. injective), then the kernel is trivial and ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is isomorphic to ![{\rho({\mathfrak g})}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).

Now we consider some examples of factoring some class of Lie algebras into simpler Lie algebras. The easiest examples of Lie algebras to understand are the _abelian_ Lie algebras ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), in which the Lie bracket identically vanishes. Every one-dimensional Lie algebra is automatically abelian, and thus isomorphic to the scalar algebra ![{{\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). Conversely, by using an arbitrary linear basis of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), we see that an abelian Lie algebra is isomorphic to the direct sum of one-dimensional algebras. Thus, a Lie algebra is abelian if and only if it is isomorphic to the direct sum of finitely many copies of ![{{\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).

Now consider a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) that is not necessarily abelian. We then form the [derived algebra](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_subgroup)![{[{\mathfrak g},{\mathfrak g}]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); this algebra is trivial if and only if ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is abelian. It is easy to see that ![{[{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak k}]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is an ideal whenever ![{{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) are ideals, so in particular the derived algebra ![{[{\mathfrak g},{\mathfrak g}]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is an ideal and we thus have the short exact sequence

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = [{\mathfrak g},{\mathfrak g}] . ({\mathfrak g}/[{\mathfrak g},{\mathfrak g}]).](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D+.+%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2F%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D%29.&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

The algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}/[{\mathfrak g},{\mathfrak g}]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2F%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is the maximal abelian quotient of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and is known as the [abelianisation](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_subgroup#Abelianization) of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). If it is trivial, we call the Lie algebra [perfect](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_group). If instead it is non-trivial, then the derived algebra has strictly smaller dimension than ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). From this, it is natural to associate two series to any Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), the _lower central series_

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g}_1 = {\mathfrak g}; {\mathfrak g}_2 := [{\mathfrak g}, {\mathfrak g}_1]; {\mathfrak g}_3 := [{\mathfrak g}, {\mathfrak g}_2]; \ldots](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D_1+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%3B+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D_2+%3A%3D+%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D_1%5D%3B+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D_3+%3A%3D+%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D_2%5D%3B+%5Cldots&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

and the _derived series_

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g}^{(1)} := {\mathfrak g}; {\mathfrak g}^{(2)} := [{\mathfrak g}^{(1)}, {\mathfrak g}^{(1)}]; {\mathfrak g}^{(3)} := [{\mathfrak g}^{(2)}, {\mathfrak g}^{(2)}]; \ldots.](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%281%29%7D+%3A%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%3B+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%282%29%7D+%3A%3D+%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%281%29%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%281%29%7D%5D%3B+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%283%29%7D+%3A%3D+%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%282%29%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%282%29%7D%5D%3B+%5Cldots.&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

By induction we see that these are both decreasing series of ideals of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), with the derived series being slightly smaller (![{{\mathfrak g}^{(k)} \subseteq {\mathfrak g}_k}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5E%7B%28k%29%7D+%5Csubseteq+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D_k%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for all ![{k}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bk%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)). We say that a Lie algebra is [nilpotent](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_Lie_algebra) if its lower central series is eventually trivial, and [solvable](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvable_Lie_algebra) if its derived series eventually becomes trivial. Thus, abelian Lie algebras are nilpotent, and nilpotent Lie algebras are solvable, but the converses are not necessarily true. For instance, in the general linear group ![{{\mathfrak{gl}}_n = {\mathfrak{gl}}({\bf C}^n)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D_n+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%5En%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), which can be identified with the Lie algebra of ![{n \times n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Ctimes+n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) complex matrices, the subalgebra ![{{\mathfrak n}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+n%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of strictly upper triangular matrices is nilpotent (but not abelian for ![{n \geq 3}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Cgeq+3%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)), while the subalgebra ![{{\mathfrak n}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+n%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of upper triangular matrices is solvable (but not nilpotent for ![{n \geq 2}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Cgeq+2%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)). It is also clear that any subalgebra of a nilpotent algebra is nilpotent, and similarly for solvable or abelian algebras.

From the above discussion we see that a Lie algebra is solvable if and only if it can be represented by a tower of abelian extensions, thus

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = ( \dots ({\mathfrak a}_k . {\mathfrak a}_{k-1}) \dots . {\mathfrak a}_2 ) . {\mathfrak a}_1](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%28+%5Cdots+%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+a%7D_k+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+a%7D_%7Bk-1%7D%29+%5Cdots+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+a%7D_2+%29+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+a%7D_1&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

for some abelian ![{{\mathfrak a}_1,\ldots,{\mathfrak a}_k}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+a%7D_1%2C%5Cldots%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+a%7D_k%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). Similarly, a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is nilpotent if it is expressible as a tower of _central_ extensions (so that in all the extensions ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) in the above factorisation, ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is central in ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), where we say that ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is central in ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) if ![{[{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak g}]=0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D%3D0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)). We also see that an extension ![{{\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is solvable if and only of both factors ![{{\mathfrak h}, {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) are solvable. Splitting abelian algebras into cyclic (i.e. one-dimensional) ones, we thus see that a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is solvable if and only if it is [polycylic](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycyclic_group), i.e. it can be represented by a tower of cyclic extensions.

For our next fundamental example of using short exact sequences to split a general Lie algebra into simpler objects, we observe that every abstract Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) has an [adjoint representation](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation)![{\hbox{ad}: {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow \hbox{ad} {\mathfrak g} \subset {\mathfrak{gl}}({\mathfrak g})}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Bad%7D%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Csubset+%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), where for each ![{x \in {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), ![{\hbox{ad} x \in {\mathfrak{gl}}({\mathfrak g})}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+x+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is the linear map ![{(\hbox{ad} x)(y) := [x,y]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%28%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+x%29%28y%29+%3A%3D+%5Bx%2Cy%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); one easily verifies that this is indeed a representation (indeed, [(2)](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2013/04/27/notes-on-the-classification-of-complex-lie-algebras/#jacobi-deriv) is equivalent to the assertion that ![{\hbox{ad} [x,y] = [\hbox{ad} x, \hbox{ad} y]}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+%5Bx%2Cy%5D+%3D+%5B%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+x%2C+%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+y%5D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for all ![{x,y \in {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%2Cy+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)). The kernel of this representation is the [center](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_(group_theory))![{Z({\mathfrak g}) := \{ x \in {\mathfrak g}: [x,{\mathfrak g}] = 0\}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BZ%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29+%3A%3D+%5C%7B+x+%5Cin+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%3A+%5Bx%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%5D+%3D+0%5C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), which the maximal central subalgebra of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). We thus have the short exact sequence

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = Z({\mathfrak g}) . \hbox{ad} g \ \ \ \ \ (6)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+Z%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29+.+%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+g+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%286%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

which, among other things, shows that every abstract Lie algebra is a central extension of a concrete Lie algebra (which can serve as a cheap substitute for Ado’s theorem mentioned earlier).

For our next fundamental decomposition of Lie algebras, we need some more definitions. A Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is [simple](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_Lie_algebra) if it is non-abelian and has no ideals other than ![{0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); thus simple Lie algebras cannot be factored ![{{\mathfrak g} = {\mathfrak h} . {\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+.+%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) into strictly smaller algebras ![{{\mathfrak h},{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2C%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). In particular, simple Lie algebras are automatically perfect and centerless. We have the following fundamental theorem:

> **Theorem 1 (Equivalent definitions of semisimplicity)** Let ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) be a Lie algebra. Then the following are equivalent:
>
> - (i) ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) does not contain any non-trivial solvable ideal.
> - (ii) ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) does not contain any non-trivial abelian ideal.
> - (iii) The [Killing form](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_form)![{K: {\mathfrak g} \times {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow {\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BK%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Ctimes+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), defined as the bilinear form ![{K(x,y) := \hbox{tr}_{\mathfrak g}( (\hbox{ad} x) (\hbox{ad} y) )}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BK%28x%2Cy%29+%3A%3D+%5Chbox%7Btr%7D_%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%28+%28%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+x%29+%28%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+y%29+%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), is non-degenerate on ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - (iv) ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is isomorphic to the direct sum of finitely many non-abelian simple Lie algebras.

We review the proof of this theorem later in these notes. A Lie algebra obeying any (and hence all) of the properties (i)-(iv) is known as a [semisimple](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_algebra) Lie algebra. The statement (iv) is usually taken as the _definition_ of semisimplicity; the equivalence of (iv) and (i) is a special case of _Weyl’s complete reducibility theorem_ (see Theorem [44](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2013/04/27/notes-on-the-classification-of-complex-lie-algebras/#weyl-thm)), and the equivalence of (iv) and (iii) is known as the _Cartan semisimplicity criterion_. (The equivalence of (i) and (ii) is easy.)

If ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) are solvable ideals of a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), then it is not difficult to see that the vector sum ![{{\mathfrak h}+{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is also a solvable ideal (because on quotienting by ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) we see that the derived series of ![{{\mathfrak h}+{\mathfrak k}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%2B%7B%5Cmathfrak+k%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) must eventually fall inside ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and thence must eventually become trivial by the solvability of ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)). As our Lie algebras are finite dimensional, we conclude that ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) has a unique maximal solvable ideal, known as the [radical](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_of_a_Lie_algebra)![{\hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). The quotient ![{{\mathfrak g}/\hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2F%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is then a Lie algebra with trivial radical, and is thus semisimple by the above theorem, giving the [Levi decomposition](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_decomposition)

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = \hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g} . ({\mathfrak g} / \hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g})](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+.+%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%2F+%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

expressing an arbitrary Lie algebra as an extension of a semisimple Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}/\hbox{rad}{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%2F%5Chbox%7Brad%7D%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) by a solvable algebra ![{\hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (and it is not hard to see that this is the only possible such extension up to isomorphism). Indeed, a deep theorem of Levi allows one to upgrade this decomposition to a split extension

![\displaystyle {\mathfrak g} = \hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g} : ({\mathfrak g} / \hbox{rad} {\mathfrak g})](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3D+%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%3A+%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%2F+%5Chbox%7Brad%7D+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

although we will not need or prove this result here.

In view of the above decompositions, we see that we can factor any Lie algebra (using a suitable combination of direct sums and extensions) into a finite number of simple Lie algebras and the scalar algebra ![{{\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). In principle, this means that one can understand an arbitrary Lie algebra once one understands all the simple Lie algebras (which, being defined over ![{{\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), are somewhat confusingly referred to as _simple complex Lie algebras_ in the literature). Amazingly, this latter class of algebras are completely classified:

> **Theorem 2 (Classification of simple Lie algebras)** Up to isomorphism, every simple Lie algebra is of one of the following forms:
>
> - ![{A_n = \mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BA_n+%3D+%5Cmathfrak%7Bsl%7D_%7Bn%2B1%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for some ![{n \geq 1}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Cgeq+1%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - ![{B_n = \mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BB_n+%3D+%5Cmathfrak%7Bso%7D_%7B2n%2B1%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for some ![{n \geq 2}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Cgeq+2%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - ![{C_n = \mathfrak{sp}_{2n}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BC_n+%3D+%5Cmathfrak%7Bsp%7D_%7B2n%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for some ![{n \geq 3}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Cgeq+3%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - ![{D_n = \mathfrak{so}_{2n}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BD_n+%3D+%5Cmathfrak%7Bso%7D_%7B2n%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for some ![{n \geq 4}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn+%5Cgeq+4%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - ![{E_6, E_7}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BE_6%2C+E_7%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), or ![{E_8}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BE_8%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - ![{F_4}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BF_4%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
> - ![{G_2}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BG_2%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002).
>
> (The precise definition of the [classical Lie algebras](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_group)![{A_n,B_n,C_n,D_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BA_n%2CB_n%2CC_n%2CD_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and the exceptional Lie algebras ![{E_6,E_7,E_8,F_4,G_2}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BE_6%2CE_7%2CE_8%2CF_4%2CG_2%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) will be recalled later.)

(One can extend the families ![{A_n,B_n,C_n,D_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BA_n%2CB_n%2CC_n%2CD_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of classical Lie algebras a little bit to smaller values of ![{n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), but the resulting algebras are either isomorphic to other algebras on this list, or cease to be simple; see [this previous post](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2011/03/11/exceptional-isogenies-between-the-classical-lie-groups/) for further discussion.)

This classification is a basic starting point for the classification of many other related objects, including Lie algebras and Lie groups over more general fields (e.g. the reals ![{{\bf R}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+R%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)), as well as finite simple groups. Being so fundamental to the subject, this classification is covered in almost every basic textbook in Lie algebras, and I myself learned it many years ago in an honours undergraduate course back in Australia. The proof is rather lengthy, though, and I have always had difficulty keeping it straight in my head. So I have decided to write some notes on the classification in this blog post, aiming to be self-contained (though moving rapidly). There is no new material in this post, though; it is all drawn from standard reference texts (I relied particularly on [Fulton and Harris’s text](http://www.ams.org/mathscinet-getitem?mr=1153249), which I highly recommend). In fact it seems remarkably hard to deviate from the standard routes given in the literature to the classification; I would be interested in knowing about other ways to reach the classification (or substeps in that classification) that are genuinely different from the orthodox route.

**— 1\. Abelian representations —**

One of the key strategies in the classification of a Lie algebra ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is to work with representations of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), particularly the adjoint representation ![{\hbox{ad}: {\mathfrak g} \rightarrow \hbox{ad} g}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Chbox%7Bad%7D%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D+%5Crightarrow+%5Chbox%7Bad%7D+g%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and then restrict such representations to various simpler subalgebras ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{{\mathfrak g}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+g%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), for which the representation theory is well understood. In particular, one aims to exploit the representation theory of _abelian_ algebras (and to a lesser extent, nilpotent and solvable algebras), as well as the fundamental example of the two-dimensional [special linear Lie algebra](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_linear_Lie_algebra)![{\mathfrak{sl}_2}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bsl%7D_2%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), which is the smallest and easiest to understand of the simple Lie algebras, and plays an absolutely crucial role in exploring and then classifying all the other simple Lie algebras.

We begin this program by recording the representation theory of abelian Lie algebras. We begin with representations ![{\rho: {\bf C} \rightarrow {\mathfrak{gl}}(V)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%3A+%7B%5Cbf+C%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28V%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of the one-dimensional algebra ![{{\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). Setting ![{x := \rho(1)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx+%3A%3D+%5Crho%281%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), this is essentially the representation theory of a single linear transformation ![{x: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). Here, the theory is given by the [Jordan decomposition](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan%E2%80%93Chevalley_decomposition). Firstly, for each complex number ![{\lambda\in {\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda%5Cin+%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), we can define the [generalised eigenspace](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenspace)

![\displaystyle V_\lambda^x := \{ v \in V: (x-\lambda)^n v = 0 \hbox{ for some } n \}.](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+V_%5Clambda%5Ex+%3A%3D+%5C%7B+v+%5Cin+V%3A+%28x-%5Clambda%29%5En+v+%3D+0+%5Chbox%7B+for+some+%7D+n+%5C%7D.&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

One easily verifies that the ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) are all linearly independent ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)-invariant subspaces of ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and in particular that there are only finitely many ![{\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (the _spectrum_![{\sigma(x)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Csigma%28x%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)) for which ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is non-trivial. If one quotients out all the generalised eigenspaces, one can check that the quotiented transformation ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) no longer has any spectrum, which contradicts the [fundamental theorem of algebra](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_algebra) applied to the [characteristic polynomial](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_polynomial) of this quotiented transformation (or, if is more analytically inclined, one could apply [Liouville’s theorem](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville%27s_theorem_(complex_analysis)) to the [resolvent operators](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolvent_formalism) to obtain the required contradiction). Thus the generalised eigenspaces span ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002):

![\displaystyle V = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \sigma(x)} V_\lambda^x.](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+V+%3D+%5Cbigoplus_%7B%5Clambda+%5Cin+%5Csigma%28x%29%7D+V_%5Clambda%5Ex.&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

On each space ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), the operator ![{x-\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx-%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) only has spectrum at zero, and thus (again from the fundamental theorem of algebra) has non-trivial kernel; similarly for any ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)-invariant subspace of ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), such as the range ![{(x-\lambda) V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%28x-%5Clambda%29+V_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{x-\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx-%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). Iterating this observation we conclude that ![{x-\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx-%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is a [nilpotent operator](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_operator) on ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), thus ![{(x-\lambda)^n=0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%28x-%5Clambda%29%5En%3D0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for some ![{n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bn%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). If we then write ![{x_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to be the direct sum of the scalar multiplication operators ![{\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on each generalised eigenspace ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and ![{x_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) to be the direct sum of the operators ![{x-\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx-%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on these spaces, we have obtained the [Jordan decomposition](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan%E2%80%93Chevalley_decomposition) (or _Jordan-Chevalley decomposition_)

![\displaystyle x = x_{ss} + x_n](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+x+%3D+x_%7Bss%7D+%2B+x_n&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

where the operator ![{x_{ss}: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is [semisimple](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-simple_operator) in the sense that it is a diagonalisable linear transformation on ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (or equivalently, all generalised eigenspaces are actually eigenspaces), and ![{x_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is nilpotent. Furthermore, as we may use polynomial interpolation to find a polynomial ![{P: {\bf C} \rightarrow {\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BP%3A+%7B%5Cbf+C%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) such that ![{P(z)-\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BP%28z%29-%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) vanishes to arbitrarily high order at ![{z=\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bz%3D%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) for each ![{\lambda \in \sigma(V)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda+%5Cin+%5Csigma%28V%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (and also ![{P(0)=0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BP%280%29%3D0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)), we see that ![{x_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (and hence ![{x_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)) can be expressed as polynomials in ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) with zero constant coefficient; this fact will be important later. In particular, ![{x_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{x_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) commute.

Conversely, given an arbitrary linear transformation ![{x: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition is the unique decomposition into commuting semisimple and nilpotent elements. Indeed, if we have an alternate decomposition ![{x = x'_{ss} + x'_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx+%3D+x%27_%7Bss%7D+%2B+x%27_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) into a semisimple element ![{x'_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%27_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) commuting with a nilpotent element ![{x'_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%27_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), then the generalised eigenspaces of ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) must be preserved by both ![{x'_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%27_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{x'_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%27_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), and so without loss of generality we may assume that there is just a single generalised eigenspace ![{V = V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV+%3D+V_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); subtracting ![{\lambda}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) we may then assume that ![{\lambda=0}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda%3D0%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), but then ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is nilpotent, and so ![{x'_{ss} = x - x'_n}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%27_%7Bss%7D+%3D+x+-+x%27_n%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is also nilpotent; but the only transformation which is both semisimple and nilpotent is the zero transformation, and the claim follows.

From the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition it is not difficult to then place ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) in [Jordan normal form](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_normal_form) by selecting a suitable basis for ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002); see e.g. [this previous blog post](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2007/10/12/the-jordan-normal-form-and-the-euclidean-algorithm/). But in contrast to the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition, the basis is not unique in general, and we will not explicitly use the Jordan normal form in the rest of this post.

Given an abstract complex vector space ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), there is in general no canonical notion of complex conjugation on ![{V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), or of linear transformations ![{x: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). However, we can define the conjugate ![{\overline{x}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Coverline%7Bx%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of any _semisimple_ transformation ![{x: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), defined as the direct sum of ![{\overline{\lambda}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Coverline%7B%5Clambda%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on each eigenspace ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). In particular, we can define the conjugate ![{\overline{x_{ss}}: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Coverline%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of the semisimple component ![{x_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of an arbitrary linear transformation ![{x_{ss}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), which will be the direct sum of ![{\overline{\lambda}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Coverline%7B%5Clambda%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on each _generalised_ eigenspace ![{V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7BV_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002). The significance of this transformation lies in the observation that the product ![{\overline{x_{ss}} x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Coverline%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D+x%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) has trace ![{|\lambda|^2 \hbox{dim} V_\lambda^x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7C%5Clambda%7C%5E2+%5Chbox%7Bdim%7D+V_%5Clambda%5Ex%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on each generalised eigenspace (since nilpotent operators have zero trace), and in particular we see that

![\displaystyle \hbox{tr}(\overline{x_{ss}} x) = 0 \ \ \ \ \ (7)](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+%5Chbox%7Btr%7D%28%5Coverline%7Bx_%7Bss%7D%7D+x%29+%3D+0+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%5C+%287%29&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

if and only if the spectrum consists only of zero, or equivalently that ![{x}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) is nilpotent. Thus [(7)](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2013/04/27/notes-on-the-classification-of-complex-lie-algebras/#test) provides a test for nilpotency, which will be turn out to be quite useful later in this post. (Note that this trick relies very much on the special structure of ![{{\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002), in particular the fact that it has characteristic zero.)

In the above arguments we have used the basic fact that if two operators ![{x: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7Bx%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) and ![{y: V \rightarrow V}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7By%3A+V+%5Crightarrow+V%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) commute, then the generalised eigenspaces of one operator are preserved by the other. Iterating this fact, we can now start understanding the representations ![{\rho: {\mathfrak h} \rightarrow {\mathfrak{gl}}(V)}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Crho%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cmathfrak%7Bgl%7D%7D%28V%29%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of an abelian Lie algebra. Namely, there is a finite set ![{\sigma(\rho) \subset {\mathfrak h}^*}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Csigma%28%5Crho%29+%5Csubset+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%5E%2A%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) of linear functionals (or homomorphisms) ![{\lambda: {\mathfrak h} \rightarrow {\bf C}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%5Clambda%3A+%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%5Crightarrow+%7B%5Cbf+C%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) on ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002) (i.e. elements of the dual space ![{{\mathfrak h}^*}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%5E%2A%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)) for which the generalised eigenspaces

![\displaystyle V_\lambda^{\mathfrak h} := \{ v \in V: (\rho({\mathfrak h}) - \lambda)^n v = 0 \hbox{ for some } n \}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%5Cdisplaystyle+V_%5Clambda%5E%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D+%3A%3D+%5C%7B+v+%5Cin+V%3A+%28%5Crho%28%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%29+-+%5Clambda%29%5En+v+%3D+0+%5Chbox%7B+for+some+%7D+n+%5C%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)

are non-trivial and ![{{\mathfrak h}}](https://s0.wp.com/latex.php?latex=%7B%7B%5Cmathfrak+h%7D%7D&bg=ffffff&fg=000000&s=0&c=20201002)-invariant, and we have the decomposition
