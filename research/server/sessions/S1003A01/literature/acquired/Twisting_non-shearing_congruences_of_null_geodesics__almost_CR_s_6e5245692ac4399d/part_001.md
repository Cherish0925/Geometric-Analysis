\title{
Twisting non-shearing congruences of null geodesics, almost CR structures, and Einstein metrics in even dimensions
}

\author{
Arman Taghavi-Chabert*
}

\begin{abstract}
We investigate the geometry of a twisting non-shearing congruence of null geodesics on a conformal manifold of even dimension greater than four and Lorentzian signature. We give a necessary and sufficient condition on the Weyl tensor for the twist to induce an almost Robinson structure, that is, the screen bundle of the congruence is equipped with a bundle complex structure. In this case, the (local) leaf space of the congruence acquires a partially integrable contact almost CR structure of positive definite signature. We give further curvature conditions for the integrability of the almost Robinson structure and the almost CR structure, and for the flatness of the latter.

We show that under a mild natural assumption on the Weyl tensor, any metric in the conformal class that is a solution to the Einstein field equations determines an almost CR-Einstein structure on the leaf space of the congruence. These metrics depend on three parameters, and include the Fefferman-Einstein metric and Taub-NUT-(A)dS metric in the integrable case. In the non-integrable case, we obtain new solutions to the Einstein field equations, which, we show, can be constructed from strictly almost Kähler-Einstein manifolds.
\end{abstract}

Keywords: Lorentzian geometry, Conformal geometry, Congruences of null geodesics, Almost Robinson structures, Almost CR geometry, Einstein metrics, Almost Kähler-Einstein metrics
Mathematics Subject Classification (2010): 53C50, 53B30, 53C18, 53C10, 32V05, 83C20, 32Q20, 32Q60

\section*{1 Introduction}

A non-shearing congruence of null geodesics on a Lorentzian manifold $(\mathcal{M}, g)$ is a (local) foliation $\mathcal{K}$ by geodesics generated by a null vector field $k$, i.e. $g(k, k)=0$, such that the metric $g$ is preserved along the flow of $k$ when restricted to vectors orthogonal to $k$, i.e.
$$£_{k} g(v, w) \propto g(v, w), \quad \text { for any vector fields } v, w \text { such that } g(k, v)=g(k, w)=0 .$$
In dimension four, non-shearing congruences of null geodesics are central objects of mathematical relativity, and their existence is intimately connected with solutions to the vacuum Maxwell equation and solutions to the Einstein field equations according to the Robinson theorem [56] and the Goldberg-Sachs theorem [19, 20] respectively. The latter asserts that any Einstein spacetime admits a non-shearing congruence of null geodesics if and only if its Weyl tensor is algebraically special.

This includes many well-known solutions such as the Kerr black hole [28], Robinson-Trautman spacetimes [57] and Kundt spacetimes [30].

Another feature of these congruences in dimension four is that they are equivalent to the existence of an involutive totally null complex 2-plane distribution N. What is more, N induces a Cauchy-Riemann (CR) structure on the three-dimensional (local) leaf space $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ of $\mathcal{K}$, that is, $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ is endowed with a rank-2 distribution together with a bundle complex structure [71, 72, 59, 54, 60, 61, 73]. This is particularly relevant to the study of solutions to the Einstein field equations, which, as beautifully demonstrated in [37], can then be reduced to CR data on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ when the congruence is twisting i.e. $\kappa \wedge \mathrm{d} \kappa \neq 0$ where $\kappa=g(k, \cdot)$.

In dimensions greater than four, such congruences have not been as prominent in Einstein spacetimes as they have in dimension four. The notable exceptions are the higher-dimensional generalisations of Robinson-Trautman and Kundt spacetimes, where the congruence is non-twisting, i.e. $\kappa \wedge \mathrm{d} \kappa=0$ with $\kappa=g(k, \cdot)$. In odd dimensions, it has been shown [49] that if the Weyl tensor satisfies
$$W(k, v, k, v)=0, \quad \text { for any vector field } v \text { such that } g(k, v)=0,$$
then the non-shearing congruence of null geodesics generated by $k$ is necessarily non-twisting. The even-dimensional case, however, has not been thoroughly investigated. The only known examples are Taub-NUT-type metrics of [9, 7], as pointed out in [50], and Einstein metrics on Fefferman spaces of CR manifolds, which were described in [33, 11], and where the congruence is generated by a null conformal Killing field.

The aim of the present article is to fill the gap in that respect and provide a detailed understanding of twisting non-shearing congruences of null geodesics in dimension $2 m+2$ where $m>1$. For this purpose, we shall adopt the strategy and philosophy of [60, 54] and subsequent work [35, 36, 43, 44, 45]. For more analytical issues, see also [64, 38, 26, 62]. In particular, we shall emphasise the conformally invariant aspect of these congruences: a congruence $\mathcal{K}$ of null curves can be expressed in terms of an optical geometry $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$, where $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c})$ is a conformal manifold and $K$ the null line distribution tangent to $\mathcal{K}$. The screen bundle $H_{K}:=K^{\perp} / K$ inherits a bundle conformal structure $\mathbf{c}_{H_{K}}$ from c. The congruence $\mathcal{K}$ is then geodesic if $K^{\perp}$ is preserved along $\mathcal{K}$, and in addition, non-shearing if $\mathbf{c}_{H_{K}}$ is also preserved along $\mathcal{K}$ [58, 17].

On the other hand, as advocated by [47, 74, 67, 18], one can start with an almost Robinson manifold $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, N, K)$, that is, a Lorentzian conformal manifold of dimension $2 m+2$ equipped with a totally null complex $(m+1)$-plane distribution $N$ : it defines an optical structure $K$, and thus a congruence $\mathcal{K}$ of null curves, together with a bundle complex structure on the screen bundle $H_{K}$ compatible with $\mathbf{c}_{H_{K}}$. Under suitable conditions, $(N, K)$ induces an almost CR structure on the (local) leaf space of $\mathcal{K}$, which is integrable if and only if $(N, K)$ is integrable. But, unlike in dimension four, $\mathcal{K}$ is shearing in general [75, 39] except in rare constructions such as the Fefferman conformal structure [15, 16, 31, 24] and Taub-NUT-type metrics [9, 7, 3].

The converse problem seems a priori quite hopeless: when does an optical geometry single out an almost Robinson structure in even dimensions greater than four? As this article will reveal, there is a definite, and surprisingly natural, answer to this question provided the congruence is geodesic, twisting and non-shearing. The ramification into almost CR geometry, if somewhat simpler, will then prove as powerful as in dimension four, but will offer a new feature: the underlying almost CR structure may be non-integrable.

The structure of the paper and its main results are as follows. The background material on conformal geometry and optical geometries is given in Sections 2 and 3, where Theorem 3.1 highlights
the integrability condition for the existence of a non-shearing congruence of null geodesics. Section 4 provides a fairly detailed account of partially integrable contact almost CR geometry. We extend in particular the definition of a CR-Einstein structure to non-integrable almost CR geometry, and relate this concept to almost Kähler-Einstein metrics, notably in Proposition 4.4. We then review the notion of almost Robinson manifolds in Section 5. This leads naturally to the following theorem, which gives an invariant relation between twisting non-shearing congruences and almost CR structures:

Theorem 1.1 Let $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$ be a $(2 m+2)$-dimensional conformal optical geometry, where $m>1$, with twisting non-shearing congruence of null geodesics $\mathcal{K}$. The following statements are equivalent:
\begin{itemize}
\item[1.] The Weyl tensor satisfies
$$W(k, v, k, v)=0, \quad \text { for any sections } k \text { of } K, v \text { of } K^{\perp} .$$
\item[2.] The twist of $\mathcal{K}$ induces an almost Robinson structure $(N, K)$ on $\mathcal{M}$.
\item[3.] The twist of $\mathcal{K}$ induces a partially integrable contact almost CR structure $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ of positive definite signature on the (local) leaf space $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ of $\mathcal{K}$.
\end{itemize}

Under the assumptions of Theorem 1.1, Section 6 delves into the relation between the Weyl tensor and the invariants of the almost CR structure - see Theorems 6.1, 6.2 and 6.3, where we give conditions on the Weyl tensor leading to the integrability and flatness of the almost CR structure.

In Section 7, we include a brief discussion on additional prescriptions on the Weyl curvature as potential generalisations of the notion of algebraically special Weyl tensors from four to higher dimensions. One such candidate is used to prove, in Section 8, the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2 Let $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$ be a $(2 m+2)$-dimensional conformal optical geometry, where $m>1$, with twisting non-shearing congruence of null geodesics $\mathcal{K}$. Suppose that the Weyl tensor satisfies
$$W(k, v, k, \cdot)=0, \quad \text { for any sections } k \text { of } K, v \text { of } K^{\perp},$$
and that $\mathbf{c}$ contains a metric $\widehat{g}$ that is Einstein with Ricci scalar $(2 m+2) \Lambda$ on (some open subset of) $\mathcal{M}$. Then
\begin{itemize}
\item[-] the twist of $\mathcal{K}$ induces an almost Robinson structure $(N, K)$ on $\mathcal{M}$;
\item[-] $\mathcal{M}$ is (locally) diffeomorphic to $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \times \underline{\mathcal{M}}$, where $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ is the (local) leaf space of $\mathcal{K}$, and $(N, K)$ induces a partially integrable contact almost CR structure (L, $\underline{J}$ ) of positive definite signature on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$;
\item[-] there is a distinguished contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ such that $\left(\underline{H}, \underline{J}, \underline{\theta}^{0}\right)$ is almost CR-Einstein - in particular, the Webster-Ricci scalar is given by $\underline{\mathrm{Sc}}=m \underline{\Lambda}+\|\underline{N}\|_{\underline{h}}^{2}$, where $\underline{\Lambda}$ is some constant and $\|\underline{N}\|_{\underline{h}}^{2}$ is the square of the norm of the Nijenhuis tensor of $\left(\underline{H}, \underline{J}, \underline{\theta}^{0}\right)$ with respect to the Levi form $\underline{h}$ of $\underline{\theta}^{0}$;
\item[-] locally, the metric takes the form
$$\widehat{g}=\sec ^{2} \phi g, \quad \text { for }-\frac{\pi}{2}<\phi<\frac{\pi}{2},$$
\end{itemize}
where, denoting the natural projection from $\mathcal{M}$ to $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ by $\varpi$,
$$\begin{aligned}
& g=2 \kappa \odot \lambda+h, \\
& \kappa=2 \varpi^{*} \underline{\theta}^{0}, \quad h=\varpi^{*} \underline{h}, \quad \lambda=\mathrm{d} \phi+\lambda_{0} \varpi^{*} \underline{\theta}^{0} \text {, }
\end{aligned}$$
with
$$\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{0}=\frac{\underline{\Lambda}}{2 m+2}+\left(\frac{\Lambda}{2 m+1}-\frac{\underline{\Lambda}}{2 m+2}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m} a_{j} \cos ^{2 j} \phi-2 a_{m} \cos ^{2 m+2} \phi\right. & ) \\
& +\underline{c} \cos ^{2 m+1} \phi \sin \phi
\end{aligned}$$
for some constant $\underline{\mathrm{c}}$ and
$$a_{0}=1, \quad \quad a_{j}=\frac{2 m-2 j+4}{2 m-2 j+1} a_{j-1}, \quad \quad j=1, \ldots, m .$$

Further, the following statements are equivalent:
\begin{itemize}
\item[1.] The Weyl tensor satisfies
$$W(k, u, v, w)=0, \quad \text { for any sections } k \text { of } K, u, v, w \text { of } N ;$$
\item[2.] $(N, K)$ is integrable;
\item[3.] $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ is integrable.
\end{itemize}

The theorem above thus tells us that any Einstein metric that admits a twisting non-shearing congruence of null geodesics with curvature prescription (1.2) depends on the three parameters $\Lambda, \underline{\Lambda}$ and $\underline{c}$. This should be contrasted with the situation in dimension four, where the range of solutions is much larger. Further results under weaker assumptions on the Ricci tensor are given in Theorem 8.1. Using the results of Section 4, Theorem 1.2 also provides a way of constructing examples of Einstein almost Robinson manifolds as lifts of almost CR-Einstein structures over almost Kähler-Einstein manifolds.

In Section 9, we relate the Einstein metrics of Theorem 1.2 for certain values of the parameters $\Lambda, \underline{\Lambda}$ and $\underline{c}$ to Fefferman-Einstein metrics in Section 9.1 and Taub-NUT-type metrics in Section 9.2. These metrics are well-known when the almost Robinson structure is integrable, but to the author's knowledge, the solutions in the non-integrable case are new, and have no analogues in dimension four.

Section 10 contains results on additional geometric structures on the Einstein manifold of Theorem 1.2, notably on the existence of a distinguished conformal Killing field in Proposition 10.1 and on the properties of a dual almost Robinson structure in Proposition 10.2.

Finally, we briefly discuss the possible generalisations to different metric signatures in Section 11. We have relegated the computation of the curvature tensors to Appendix A.

\section*{2 Conformal geometry}

Let $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c})$ be an oriented and time-oriented conformal smooth manifold of Lorentzian signature, i.e. $(+, \ldots,+,-)$, and of dimension $n+2$. Most of our notation and conventions will be relatively standard in the field of differential geometry. Pullback and pushforward maps will be adorned with an upper and lower $*$ respectively, the Lie derivative along a vector field $v$ will be denoted $£_{v}$ and so on. The $k$-th exterior power of the cotangent bundle $T^{*} \mathcal{M}$ will be denoted by $\wedge^{k} T^{*} \mathcal{M}$, and its $k$-th symmetric power by $\odot^{k} T^{*} \mathcal{M}$. For any two 1-forms $\alpha$ and $\beta$, we take the convention that $\alpha \wedge \beta=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha \otimes \beta-\beta \otimes \alpha)$ and $\alpha \odot \beta=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha \otimes \beta+\beta \otimes \alpha)$. We shall also denote the space of sections of a vector bundle $E$ by $\Gamma(E)$. We write ${ }^{\mathbf{C}} E$ for the complexification of $E$. For any subbundle $F$ of $E, \operatorname{Ann}(F)$ will denote the subbundle of $E^{*}$ consisting of elements annihilating sections of $F$. In abstract index notation, sections of $T \mathcal{M}$, respectively, $T^{*} \mathcal{M}$ will be adorned with upper, respectively, lower minuscule Roman indices starting from the beginning of the alphabet, e.g. $\quad v^{a} \in \Gamma(T \mathcal{M})$, and $\xi_{a b} \in \Gamma\left(\otimes^{2} T^{*} \mathcal{M}\right)$. Symmetrisation will be denoted by round brackets, and skew-symmetrisation by square brackets, e.g. $\lambda_{(a b)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{a b}+\lambda_{b a}\right)$ and $\lambda_{[a b]}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{a b}-\lambda_{b a}\right)$. These conventions will also be applied to the other types of geometries appearing in this article.

Following [8], one can naturally introduce density bundles, denoted $\mathcal{E}[w]$ for any $w \in \mathbf{R}$. In particular, we interpret $\mathcal{E}[1]$ as the bundle of conformal scales: sections of $\mathcal{E}[1]$ corresponds to metrics in the conformal class. The correspondence is achieved via the conformal metric, $\boldsymbol{g}_{a b}$, which is a non-degenerate section of $\odot^{2} T^{*} \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{E}[2]$, preserved by the Levi-Civita connection of any metric in c: if $s \in \mathcal{E}[1]$ is a conformal scale, then the corresponding metric in c is given by $g_{a b}=s^{-2} \boldsymbol{g}_{a b}$. This conformal metric allows us to identify sections of $T \mathcal{M}$ with $T^{*} \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{E}[2]$. In this conformal setting, indices will be lowered and raised with $\boldsymbol{g}_{a b}$ and its inverse $\boldsymbol{g}^{a b}$ respectively, but with a choice of metric $g$ in $\mathbf{c}$, we shall often use the metric isomorphism $T \mathcal{M} \cong T^{*} \mathcal{M}$. The subbundle of $\odot^{k} T^{*} \mathcal{M}$ consisting of tracefree elements will be denoted by $\bigodot_{o}^{k} T^{*} \mathcal{M}$. The tracefree part of tensors with respect to $\boldsymbol{g}_{a b}$ will be adorned with a ring, e.g. either as $\lambda_{(a b)_{0}}$ or as $\left(\lambda_{a b}\right)_{)_{0}}$.

If two metrics $g$ and $\widehat{g}$ in c are related by
$$\widehat{g}=\mathrm{e}^{2 \varphi} g, \quad \text { for some smooth function } \varphi \text { on } \mathcal{M},$$
their respective Levi-Civita connections ∇ and $\widehat{\nabla}$ are related by
$$\widehat{\nabla}_{a} \alpha_{b}=\nabla_{a} \alpha_{b}+(w-1) \Upsilon_{a} \alpha_{b}-\Upsilon_{b} \alpha_{a}+\Upsilon_{c} \alpha^{c} \boldsymbol{g}_{a b}, \quad \alpha_{a} \in \Gamma\left(T^{*} \mathcal{M}[w]\right),$$
where $\Upsilon_{a}:=\nabla_{a} \varphi$. The covariant exterior derivative, denoted $\mathrm{d}^{\nabla}$, is given by
$$\left(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla} \alpha\right)_{a b_{1} \ldots b_{k}}=\nabla_{[a} \alpha_{\left.b_{1} \ldots b_{k}\right]}, \quad \alpha_{b_{1} \ldots b_{k}} \in \Gamma\left(\bigwedge^{k} T^{*} \mathcal{M}[w]\right) .$$
By convention, we take the Riemann tensor of a given metric $g_{a b}$ in c to be defined by
$$2 \nabla_{[a} \nabla_{b]} V^{c}=: R_{a b}{ }^{c}{ }_{d} V^{d}, \quad V^{a} \in \Gamma(T \mathcal{M}) .$$
It decomposes as
$$R_{a b c d}=W_{a b c d}+4 \boldsymbol{g}_{[a \mid[c} \mathrm{P}_{d] \mid b]},$$
where its tracefree part $W_{a b c d}$ is the Weyl tensor, and $\mathrm{P}_{a b}$ is the Schouten tensor, given in terms of the Ricci tensor $\operatorname{Ric}_{a b}:=R_{c a}{ }^{c}{ }_{b}$ and the Ricci scalar $\mathrm{Sc}:=\operatorname{Ric}_{a b} \boldsymbol{g}^{a b}$ by
$$\mathrm{P}_{a b}:=\frac{1}{n}\left(\operatorname{Ric}_{a b}-\frac{\mathrm{Sc}}{2(n+1)} \boldsymbol{g}_{a b}\right) .$$
We also define the Schouten scalar $\mathrm{P}:=\mathrm{P}_{a b} \boldsymbol{g}^{a b}$. While the Weyl tensor is conformally invariant, the Schouten tensor transforms as
$$\widehat{\mathrm{P}}_{a b}=\mathrm{P}_{a b}-\nabla_{a} \Upsilon_{b}+\Upsilon_{a} \Upsilon_{b}-\frac{1}{2} \Upsilon^{c} \Upsilon_{c} \boldsymbol{g}_{a b},$$
under the change (2.1).

\section*{3 Optical geometries}

\subsection*{3.1 Basic definitions and facts}

We summarise the exposition given in [17], which also draws on [58]. Let $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$ be a (conformal) optical geometry of dimension $n+2$, that is, $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c})$ is a time-oriented and oriented Lorentzian conformal manifold, and $K$ a null line distribution. This line distribution is oriented by virtue of the orientation and time-orientation of $\mathcal{M}$. It is a subbundle of its orthogonal complement $K^{\perp}$ with respect to $\mathbf{c}$, i.e.
$$K \subset K^{\perp} \subset T \mathcal{M} .$$
We call the oriented rank- $n$ quotient
$$H_{K}:=K^{\perp} / K,$$
the screen bundle of $K$. The conformal structure c on $\mathcal{M}$ induces a conformal structure $\mathbf{c}_{H_{K}}$ of Riemannian signature on $H_{K}$, and in particular, a conformal metric $\boldsymbol{h}$ on $H_{K}$, that is, the non-degenerate section of $\bigodot^{2} H_{K}^{*}$ defined by
$$\boldsymbol{h}(v+K, w+K):=\boldsymbol{g}(v, w), \quad \text { for any } v, w \in \Gamma\left(K^{\perp}\right) .$$
Any non-vanishing section of $K$ will be referred to as an optical vector field, and any non-vanishing section of $\operatorname{Ann}\left(K^{\perp}\right)$ as an optical 1-form.

In abstract index notation, we shall use upper, respectively, lower, minuscule Roman indices starting from the middlle of the alphabet, i.e. $i, j, k, \ldots$ for sections of $H_{K}$, respectively, $H_{K}^{*}$. Thus, the conformal metric above may be denoted $\boldsymbol{h}_{i j}$. If $s$ is a conformal scale, then $h_{i j}=s^{-2} \boldsymbol{h}_{i j}$ is a metric in $\mathbf{c}_{H_{K}}$.

Concretely, it will be convenient to fix a metric $g$ in c and introduce a null line distribution $L$ dual to $K$ to split the filtration (3.1) so that
$$T \mathcal{M}=L \oplus H_{K, L} \oplus K, \quad \text { where } H_{K, L}:=K^{\perp} \cap L^{\perp} .$$
Sections of $H_{K}$ can then be identified with sections of $H_{K, L}$, and we shall use the same index notation for sections of $H_{K, L}$ as for those of $H_{K}$. We shall also introduce a frame $\left\{\ell, e_{i}, k\right\}=$ $\left\{e_{0}, e_{i}, e^{0}\right\}$ adapted to the optical geometry $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$, where $k$ and $\ell$ are sections of $K$ and $L$ respectively, such that $g(k, \ell)=1$, and $\left\{e_{i}\right\}, i=1, \ldots, n$, form an orthonormal frame for $H_{K, L}$. The coframe dual to it will be denoted $\left\{\kappa, \theta^{i}, \lambda\right\}=\left\{\theta^{0}, \theta^{i}, \theta_{0}\right\}$. With this notation, the metric $g$ takes the form
$$g=2 \theta^{0} \odot \theta_{0}+h_{i j} \theta^{i} \odot \theta^{j} .$$
For any section $\alpha_{a}$ of $T^{*} \mathcal{M}$, we shall write
$$\alpha^{0}=\alpha(k), \quad \alpha_{i}=\alpha\left(e_{i}\right), \quad \alpha_{0}=\alpha(\ell),$$
and similarly for tensor fields of other valences. These indices may be viewed abstractly. The tracefree part of a tensor $T_{i j}$, say, with respect to $\boldsymbol{h}^{i j}$ will also be denoted by $\left(T_{i j}\right)_{\circ}$ or $T_{(i j)^{\circ}}$.

\subsection*{3.2 Congruences of null geodesics}

The foliation by null curves tangent to $K$, i.e. the aggregate of the integral curves of any optical vector field $k$ of $K$, will be referred to as the congruence $\mathcal{K}$ of null curves associated to $K$. These curves are oriented since $K$ is oriented.

Henceforth, we assume that the curves of $\mathcal{K}$ are geodesics. This property can be defined as follows: the weighted 1 -form $\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\boldsymbol{g}(k, \cdot)$ corresponding to any optical vector field $k$ satisfies
$$£_{k} \boldsymbol{\kappa}(v)=0, \quad v \in \Gamma\left(K^{\perp}\right) .$$
Condition (3.2) tells us that any optical 1-form is preserved along the flow of $k$, and in particular, descends to a 1-form on the $(n+1)$-dimensional leaf space $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ of $\mathcal{K}$. This means that $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ inherits a rank- $n$ distribution $\underline{H}$ from the screen bundle $H_{K}$.

This leaf space $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H})$ inherits additional structures on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ from the invariants of $\mathcal{K}$. Notably, for any optical vector field $k$, we introduce [17]
\begin{itemize}
\item[1.] the twist of $k$, that is, the section $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ of $\wedge^{2} H_{K}^{*} \otimes \mathcal{E}[2]$ defined by
$$\boldsymbol{\tau}(v+K, w+K):=\mathrm{d}^{\nabla} \boldsymbol{\kappa}(v, w), \quad v, w \in \Gamma\left(K^{\perp}\right) ;$$
\item[2.] the shear of $k$, that is, the section $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ of $\bigodot_{\circ}^{2} H_{K}^{*} \otimes \mathcal{E}[2]$ defined by
$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}(v+K, w+K) \boldsymbol{\kappa}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(£_{k} \boldsymbol{g}(v, w) \boldsymbol{\kappa}-\boldsymbol{g}(v, w) £_{k} \boldsymbol{\kappa}\right), \quad v, w \in \Gamma\left(K^{\perp}\right) .$$
\end{itemize}

It is clear that any rescaling of $k$ induces a rescaling of its twist and shear. These definitions thus extend to the notions of twist and shear of the congruence $\mathcal{K}$, both of which are conformal invariants.

With a choice of metric $g$ in c, we can also define the expansion of $k$ to be the smooth function $\epsilon$ given by
$$\epsilon \kappa:=\kappa \operatorname{div} k-\nabla_{k} \kappa,$$
where $\kappa=g(k, \cdot)$ and $\operatorname{div} k=\nabla_{a} k^{a}$. Again, there is a well-defined notion of expansion of $\mathcal{K}$. While the expansion is not conformally invariant, one can always choose a metric $g$ in the conformal class for which the congruence $\mathcal{K}$ generated by $k$ is non-expanding, $\epsilon=0$. In fact, locally, this defines a subclass ${ }^{n \cdot e \cdot}$ of metrics in $\mathbf{c}$ with the property that whenever $g$ is in $\stackrel{n \cdot e}{\mathbf{c}}$, the congruence $\mathcal{K}$ is non-expanding. Any two metrics in $\stackrel{\text { n.e }}{\mathbf{c}}$ differ by a factor constant along $K$-see [17].

Let us now review the geometric interpretation of the twist and shear of $\mathcal{K}$. The twist of $\mathcal{K}$, if non-zero, induces a skew-symmetric bundle map on $\underline{H}$ whose rank is given by the rank of $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ - this is clear from the geodesic property (3.2), the defining equation (3.3), and the naturality of the exterior derivative. Let us now assume that $\mathcal{M}$ has dimension $2 m+2$ and $\mathcal{K}$ is maximally twisting, that is, $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ has maximal rank. Choose a metric $g$ in $\stackrel{n}{\boldsymbol{c}}$, and an optical vector field $k$ for which the geodesics of $\mathcal{K}$ are affinely parametrised. Then the 1-form $\kappa=g(k, \cdot)$ satisfies $£_{k} \kappa=0$ and $\kappa \wedge(\mathrm{d} \kappa)^{m}$ is non-zero. This means that $\kappa$ is the pullback of a 1-form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ that annihilates $\underline{H}$ and satisfies $\underline{\theta}^{0} \wedge\left(\mathrm{~d} \underline{\theta}^{0}\right)^{m} \neq 0$, i.e. $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ is a contact form. Thus, the distribution $\underline{H}$ on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ must be contact, i.e. $\underline{H}$ bracket-generates the tangent space of $\mathcal{M}$ at every point, i.e. $T \underline{\mathcal{M}}=\underline{H}+[\underline{H}, \underline{H}]$.

On the other hand, if the congruence $\mathcal{K}$ is non-shearing, i.e. $\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0$, it is immediate from (3.4) that the conformal structure induced on $H_{K}$ is preserved along the flow of any generator of $\mathcal{K}$. In this case, the distribution $\underline{H}$ on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ is endowed with a conformal structure $\underline{\mathbf{c}}_{\underline{H}}$. More precisely, there is a one-to-one correspondence between metrics in ${ }^{n, e \cdot}$ and metrics in $\underline{\mathbf{c}}_{\underline{H}}$.

Thus, combining these two properties, we conclude that a maximally twisting non-shearing congruence of null geodesics $\mathcal{K}$ induces a so-called sub-conformal contact structure $\left(\underline{H}, \mathbf{c}_{\underline{H}}\right)$ on its leaf space $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$, i.e. $\underline{H}$ is contact and is endowed with a conformal structure $\underline{\mathbf{c}}_{\underline{H}}$ [2, 3, 17]. In Section 4, we shall equip $\underline{H}$ with a bundle complex structure.

\subsection*{3.3 Integrability condition}

It is well-known that if $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$ is a four-dimensional conformal optical geometry with nonshearing congruence of null geodesics $\mathcal{K}$, then, for any optical vector field $k$, the Weyl tensor satisfies
$$\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} W_{b] e f[c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} k^{e} k^{f}=0, \quad \text { where } \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{a}=\boldsymbol{g}_{a b} k^{b} .$$
In higher dimensions, the analogous result is given by the following proposition:
Theorem 3.1 Let $(\mathcal{M}, \mathbf{c}, K)$ be an $(n+2)$-dimensional conformal optical geometry, where $n>2$, with non-shearing congruence of null geodesics $\mathcal{K}$. Then, for any optical vector field $k$, the Weyl tensor satisfies
$$4 \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} W_{b] e f[c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} k^{e} k^{f}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{a b e} \boldsymbol{\tau}^{e}{ }_{c d}+\frac{4}{n} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{[a}{ }^{e f} \boldsymbol{g}_{b][c} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{d] e f},$$
where $\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{a}=\boldsymbol{g}_{a b} k^{b}$ and $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{a b c}:=3 \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \nabla_{b} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{c]}$.

Proof. With no loss of generality, we work with a metric $g$ in $\stackrel{\text { n }}{\mathbf{c}}$ so that $\mathcal{K}$ is non-expanding, and we choose an optical vector field $k$ for which the geodesic curves of $\mathcal{K}$ are affinely parametrised so that, with $\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{a}=\boldsymbol{g}_{a b} k^{b}$, we have
$$\begin{aligned}
k^{b} \nabla_{b} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{a} & =0, \\
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a}\left(\nabla_{b]} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[c}\right) \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} & =\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{b][c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]},
\end{aligned}$$
where $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{b c}$ is a (weighted) 2-form such that $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{a b c}=3 \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{b c}$. It also satisfies $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{b c} k^{c}=0$. In other words, $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{a b}$ represents the twist $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i j}$ of $k$ as a 2-form on $\mathcal{M}$. Now, taking a covariant derivative of (3.7) along $k^{a}$, commuting the covariant derivatives, applications of the Leibniz rule, using (2.3) and repeated applications of (3.6) leads to
$$\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} R_{b] e f[c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} k^{e} k^{f}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{b]}{ }^{e} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{e[c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} .$$
Taking the trace of this expression gives
$$\operatorname{Ric}_{a b} k^{a} k^{b}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{a b} \boldsymbol{\tau}^{a b},$$
so that using the expression for the Weyl tensor (2.4) yields
$$\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} W_{b] e f[c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} k^{e} k^{f}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{b]}^{e} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{e[c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]}+\frac{1}{n} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{e f} \boldsymbol{\tau}^{e f} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \boldsymbol{g}_{b][c} \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{d]} .$$
Finally, to obtain the expression (3.5), we simply note that $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{a b c}=3 \boldsymbol{\kappa}_{[a} \boldsymbol{\tau}_{b c]}$. \(\square\)

The formula (3.5) can also be derived from the computations given in [49]. We shall soon give a geometric interpretation to it. But before we proceed, we need to introduce some additional geometric concepts.

Remark 3.1 In dimension four, the RHS of condition (3.5) is always zero.

\section*{4 Partially integrable contact almost CR structures}

\subsection*{4.1 Almost CR structures}

We recall some basic notions regarding almost CR geometry. By and large, we follow the conventions and approaches of [69, 76, 23, 11, 12, 40, 14, 18], to which the reader is referred for more detailed accounts. Let $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ be a $(2 m+1)$-dimensional smooth manifold. An almost Cauchy-Riemann (CR) structure on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ consists of a pair ( $\underline{H}, \underline{J}$ ) where $\underline{H}$ is a rank- $2 m$ distribution and $\underline{J}$ a bundle complex structure on $\underline{H}$, i.e. $\underline{J} \circ \underline{J}=-\underline{\mathrm{Id}}$, where $\underline{\mathrm{Id}}$ is the identity map on $\underline{H}$. This means that the complexification ${ }^{\mathbf{C}} \underline{H}$ of $\underline{H}$ splits as ${ }^{\mathbf{C}} \underline{H}=\underline{H}^{(1,0)} \oplus \underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ where $\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$ and $\underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ are the rank- $m$ i-eigenbundle and -i-eigenbundle of $\underline{J}$ respectively. If $\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$ (or equivalently $\underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ ) is involutive or integrable, ${ }^{1}$ i.e. $\left[\underline{H}^{(1,0)}, \underline{H}^{(1,0)}\right] \subset \underline{H}^{(1,0)}$, we refer to $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ simply as a $C R$ structure. When $m=1$, an almost CR structure is always integrable. An (almost) pseudo-Hermitian structure on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ is an (almost) CR structure $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ together with a choice of non-vanishing section of $\operatorname{Ann}(\underline{H})$.

We shall assume further that the almost CR structure is contact or non-degenerate, i.e. $\underline{H}$ is a contact distribution, and that it is partially integrable, i.e. the bracket of two sections in

\footnotetext{
${ }^{1}$ We shall not distinguish between the two terms, involutive and integrable, here, brushing aside any analytic issues that may arise.
}
$\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$ is a section of ${ }^{\mathbf{C}} \underline{H}$. One can also describe such an almost CR structure as a sub-conformal contact structure ( $\underline{H}, \underline{\mathbf{c}_{H}}$ ) equipped with a compatible bundle complex structure. Further equivalent descriptions can be found in the aforementioned references.

In order to make the description of $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ more concrete, let us fix a contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$. Then there exists a unique vector field $\underline{e}_{0}$, known as the Reeb vector field, satisfying $\underline{\theta}^{0}\left(\underline{e}_{0}\right)=1$ and $\mathrm{d} \underline{\theta}^{0}\left(\underline{e}_{0}, \cdot\right)=0$. It induces a splitting
$${ }^{\mathbf{c}} T \underline{\mathcal{M}}={ }^{\mathbf{c}} \underline{L} \oplus \underline{H}^{(1,0)} \oplus \underline{H}^{(0,1)},$$
where $\underline{L}$ is the real line distribution spanned by $\underline{e}_{0}$. Complete $\underline{e}_{0}$ to a (complex) frame $\left\{\underline{e}_{0}, \underline{e}_{\alpha}, \overline{\underline{e}}_{\bar{\beta}}\right\}$, $\alpha, \bar{\beta}=1, \ldots, m$, adapted to $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$, i.e. $\left\{\underline{e}_{\alpha}\right\}$ and $\left\{\underline{\bar{e}}_{\bar{\beta}}\right\}, \alpha, \bar{\beta}=1, \ldots, m$, span $\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$ and $\underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ respectively. Denote by $\left\{\underline{\theta}^{0}, \underline{\theta}^{\alpha}, \underline{\bar{\theta}}^{\bar{\alpha}}\right\}$, the coframe dual to $\left\{\underline{e}_{0}, \underline{e}_{\alpha}, \underline{\bar{e}}_{\bar{\beta}}\right\}, \alpha, \bar{\beta}=1, \ldots, m$. Then the contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ satisfies $\mathrm{d} \underline{\theta}^{0}=\underline{\mathrm{h}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{\theta}^{\alpha} \wedge \underline{\theta}^{\bar{\beta}}$, where $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ is a Hermitian matrix referred to as the Levi form of $\underline{\theta}^{0}$. The signature of $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ is an invariant of $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$. We shall henceforth assume that $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ has positive definite signature.

We shall also use the indices just introduced in an abstract way. Thus, sections of $\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$ and $\underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ will be adorned with minuscule Greek indices, plain and barred respectively, and similarly for their duals, e.g. $\underline{V}^{\alpha} \in \Gamma\left(\underline{H}^{(1,0)}\right)$ and $\underline{\mu}_{\bar{\alpha}} \in \Gamma\left(\left(\underline{H}^{(0,1)}\right)^{*}\right)$. In addition, sections of $\operatorname{Ann}(\underline{H})$ and their duals will be adorned with a lower, respectively, upper 0, e.g. $\underline{\alpha}_{0} \in \Gamma(\operatorname{Ann}(\underline{H}))$ and $\underline{v}^{0} \in \Gamma(\underline{L})$. As before, symmetrisation and skew-symmetrisation will be denoted by round brackets and square brackets respectively. Index types can be converted using the Levi form $h_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ of a given contact form. Clearly, complex conjugation on ${ }^{\mathbf{C}} \underline{H}$ changes the index type, so we shall write $\underline{v}^{\bar{\alpha}}$ for $\underline{v}^{\alpha}$, and so on. We also note that $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}=\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$. The tracefree part of a tensor of mixed valence with respect to $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$, for instance $\underline{T}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$, will be adorned with a ring, e.g. $\left(\underline{T}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}\right)_{\circ}$ so that $\left(\underline{T}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}\right)_{\circ} \underline{h}^{\alpha \bar{\beta}}=0$.

An (infinitesimal) symmetry of $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ is a vector field $\underline{v}$ on $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ that preserves both $\underline{H}$ and $\underline{J}$, i.e. $\left(£_{\underline{v}} \underline{\theta}^{0}\right) \wedge \underline{\theta}^{0}=0$ for any contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ of $\underline{H}$, and $£_{\underline{v}} \underline{\alpha} \in \Gamma\left(\operatorname{Ann}\left(\underline{H}^{(0,1)}\right)\right)$ for any 1-form $\underline{\alpha}$ in $\operatorname{Ann}\left(\underline{H}^{(0,1)}\right)$. Such an infinitesimal symmetry $\underline{v}$ is said to be transverse if it inserts non-trivially into any contact form, i.e. $\underline{v}$ is the Reeb vector field of some contact form of $\underline{H}$.

Analogous to conformal density bundles, one can also introduce CR density bundles $\underline{\mathcal{E}}\left(w, w^{\prime}\right)$ for any $w, w^{\prime} \in \mathbf{C}$ such that $w-w^{\prime} \in \mathbf{Z}$. The details of the definition of these $\mathbf{C}^{*}$-principal bundles over $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ are given in [23, 11, 12]. We shall simply note here that $\overline{\mathcal{E}\left(w, w^{\prime}\right)}=\underline{\mathcal{E}}\left(w^{\prime}, w\right)$. Such density bundles allow us to define analogues of the conformal metric in the CR setting, namely, a canonical section $\underline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{0}$ of $T^{*} \underline{\mathcal{M}} \otimes \underline{\mathcal{E}}(1,1)$, and a canonical section $\underline{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ of $\left(\underline{H}^{(1,0)}\right)^{*} \otimes\left(\underline{H}^{(0,1)}\right)^{*} \otimes \underline{\mathcal{E}}(1,1)$ with the property that for each $\underline{s} \in \Gamma(\underline{\mathcal{E}}(-1,-1)), \underline{\theta}^{0}=\underline{s} \underline{\theta}^{0}$ is a contact form with Levi form $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}=\underline{s h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$. These sections are weighted analogues of the contact form and its Levi form. The latter and its inverse $\underline{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ identify $\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$ with $\left(\underline{H}^{(0,1)}\right)^{*}(1,1)$ and $\underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ with $\left(\underline{H}^{(1,0)}\right)^{*}(1,1)$. In effect, indices can also be raised and lowered using $\underline{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$, e.g. $\underline{v}_{\alpha}=\underline{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{v}^{\bar{\beta}}$, thereby changing the weights of the tensors.

\subsection*{4.2 Webster-Tanaka connections}

Let $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ be a partially integrable contact almost CR structure of dimension $2 m+1$ as before. For definiteness, we shall assume $m>1$. Then for each contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$, there is a unique linear connection $\underline{\nabla}$ on $T \underline{\mathcal{M}}$ called the Webster-Tanaka connection, which preserves $\underline{\theta}^{0}, \mathrm{~d} \underline{\theta}^{0}$ and the complex structure $\underline{J}$ and has prescribed torsion as follows. If $\left\{\underline{\theta}^{0}, \underline{\theta}^{\alpha}, \bar{\theta}^{\bar{\alpha}}\right\}$ is an adapted coframe,
the Cartan structure equations read as
$$\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{d} \underline{\theta}^{0}=\mathrm{i} \underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{\theta}^{\alpha} \wedge \underline{\bar{\theta}}^{\bar{\beta}}, \\
& \mathrm{d} \underline{\theta}^{\alpha}=\underline{\theta}^{\beta} \wedge \underline{\Gamma}_{\beta}{ }^{\alpha}+\underline{A}^{\alpha}{ }_{\bar{\beta}} \underline{\theta}^{0} \wedge \bar{\theta}^{\bar{\beta}}-\frac{1}{2} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\bar{\beta} \bar{\gamma}}{ }^{\alpha} \underline{\theta}^{\bar{\beta}} \wedge \underline{\theta}^{\bar{\gamma}}, \\
& \mathrm{d} \underline{\theta}^{\bar{\alpha}}=\underline{\theta}^{\bar{\beta}} \wedge \underline{\Gamma}_{\bar{\beta}}{ }^{\bar{\alpha}}+\underline{A}^{\bar{\alpha}}{ }_{\beta} \underline{\theta}^{0} \wedge \underline{\theta}^{\beta}-\frac{1}{2} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\beta \gamma}{ }^{\bar{\alpha}} \theta^{\beta} \wedge \theta^{\gamma} .
\end{aligned}$$
where $\underline{\Gamma}_{\beta}{ }^{\alpha}$ is the connection 1-form of $\underline{\nabla}$ for that coframe, $\underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ the Levi form of $\underline{\theta}^{0}, \underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}$ (and its conjugate $\underline{A}_{\bar{\alpha} \bar{\beta}}$ ) the pseudo-Hermitian torsion tensor, and $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ (and its conjugate $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\bar{\alpha} \bar{\beta} \bar{\gamma}}$ ) the Nijenhuis tensor, ${ }^{2}$ and these satisfy the symmetries $\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}=\underline{A}_{(\alpha \beta)}$ and $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}=\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{[\alpha \beta] \gamma}$ with $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{[\alpha \beta \gamma]}=0$.

Remark 4.1 The Nijenhuis tensor $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ is the obstruction to the integrability of $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ and is a CR invariant. On the other hand, $\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}$ depends on the choice of contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$. Its vanishing is equivalent to its Reeb vector field $\underline{e}_{0}$ being an infinitesimal symmetry of $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$.

The structure equations (4.1) give the commutation relations
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\beta}}-\underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\beta}} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha}\right) \underline{f}=-\mathrm{i} \underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{f}, \\
& \left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{0}-\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha}\right) \underline{f}=\underline{A}_{\alpha}{ }^{\bar{\beta}} \underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\beta}} \underline{f}, \\
& \left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{\beta}-\underline{\nabla}_{\beta} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha}\right) \underline{f}=\underline{N}_{\alpha}{ }^{\bar{\gamma}} \underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\gamma}} \underline{f},
\end{aligned}$$
for any smooth function $\underline{f}$, and similarly for their complex conjugates.
The curvature tensors of the Webster-Tanaka connection are given by
$$\begin{aligned}
\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\beta}}-\underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\beta}} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha}\right) \underline{V}^{\gamma}+i \underline{h}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{V}^{\gamma} & =: \underline{R}_{\alpha \bar{\beta} \delta}^{\gamma} \underline{V}^{\delta}, \\
\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{0}-\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha}\right) \underline{V}^{\gamma}-\underline{A}_{\alpha}{ }^{\bar{\beta}} \nabla_{\bar{\beta}} \underline{V}^{\gamma} & =: \underline{R}_{\alpha 0 \delta} \underline{V}^{\delta}, \\
\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{\beta}-\underline{\nabla}_{\beta} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha}\right) \underline{V}^{\gamma}-\underline{N}_{\alpha \beta}{ }^{\bar{\delta}} \nabla_{\bar{\delta}} \underline{V}^{\gamma} & =: \underline{R}_{\alpha \beta \delta} \underline{V}^{\delta},
\end{aligned}$$
for any section $\underline{V}^{\alpha}$ of $\underline{H}^{(1,0)}$, and similarly for their complex conjugates. Analogous formulae can be derived for sections of $\underline{H}^{(0,1)}$ and their duals.

The curvature and torsion tensors are related by the first Bianchi identities:
$$\begin{aligned}
2 \underline{R}_{\beta}^{[\gamma}{ }_{\alpha}^{\delta]} & =-\underline{N}_{\epsilon \beta \alpha} \underline{N}^{\gamma \delta \epsilon}, \\
\underline{R}_{\alpha 0 \beta}^{\gamma} & =\underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}+\underline{A}^{\delta \gamma} \underline{N}_{\delta \alpha \beta}, \\
\underline{R}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}^{\delta} & =\underline{\nabla}^{\delta} \underline{N}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}-2 \underline{A}_{\alpha[\beta} \delta_{\gamma]}^{\delta}, \\
\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{N}_{\beta \gamma \alpha} & =-2 \underline{\nabla}_{[\beta} \underline{A}_{\gamma] \alpha}, \\
\underline{\nabla}_{[\delta} \underline{N}_{\beta \gamma] \alpha} & =0,
\end{aligned}$$
together with their complex conjugates, from which we obtain $\underline{R}_{[\beta \delta \gamma]}{ }^{\alpha}=0$ and $2 \underline{R}_{0[\beta \gamma]}{ }^{\alpha}=\underline{A}^{\alpha \delta} \underline{N}_{\beta \gamma \delta}$.

\footnotetext{
${ }^{2}$ This is a slight abuse of terminology, since strictly, the Nijenhuis tensor is the real tensor defined by both $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ and $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\bar{\alpha} \bar{\beta} \bar{\gamma}}$.
}

We record the second Bianchi identities in the following general form:
$$\begin{aligned}
& 2 \underline{\nabla}_{[\delta} \underline{R}_{\epsilon] \bar{\gamma}} \alpha^{\beta}+\underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\gamma}} \underline{R}_{\delta \epsilon \alpha}{ }^{\beta}-\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\delta \epsilon}{ }^{\bar{\phi}} \underline{R}_{\bar{\phi} \bar{\gamma} \alpha}{ }^{\beta}+2 \underline{\mathrm{i}}_{[\delta \mid \bar{\gamma}} \underline{R}_{\mid \epsilon] 0 \alpha}{ }^{\beta}=0, \\
& \underline{\nabla}_{\gamma} \underline{R}_{\bar{\delta} 0 \alpha}{ }^{\beta}-\underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\delta}} \underline{R}_{\gamma 0 \alpha}{ }^{\beta}+\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{R}_{\gamma \delta}{ }^{\beta}-\underline{A}_{\gamma}{ }^{\bar{\epsilon}} \underline{R}_{\bar{\delta} \bar{\epsilon} \alpha}{ }^{\beta}+\underline{A}_{\delta}{ }^{\epsilon} \underline{R}_{\gamma \epsilon \alpha}{ }^{\beta}=0, \\
& 2 \underline{\nabla}_{[\gamma} \underline{R}_{\delta] 0 \alpha}{ }^{\beta}+\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{R}_{\gamma \delta} \alpha^{\beta}-2 \underline{A}^{\bar{\epsilon}} \underline{R}_{\delta] \bar{\epsilon} \alpha}{ }^{\beta}-\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\gamma \delta}{ }^{\bar{\epsilon}} \underline{R}_{\bar{\epsilon} 0 \alpha}{ }^{\beta}=0, \\
& \underline{\nabla}_{[\gamma} \underline{R}_{\delta \epsilon] \alpha}{ }^{\beta}+\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{[\delta \epsilon \mid \phi} \underline{R}_{\mid \gamma]}{ }^{\phi} \alpha^{\beta}=0,
\end{aligned}$$
together with their complex conjugates.
At this stage, we define the Chern-Moser tensor $\underline{S}_{\alpha \bar{\gamma} \beta \bar{\delta}}$ of $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ to be the totally tracefree totally symmetric part of $\underline{R}_{\alpha \bar{\gamma} \beta \bar{\delta}}=\underline{R}_{\alpha \bar{\gamma} \beta}{ }^{\epsilon} \underline{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\epsilon \bar{\delta}}$, i.e.
$$\left.\underline{S}_{\alpha}{ }^{\gamma}{ }_{\beta}{ }^{\delta}:=\left(\underline{R}_{(\alpha}{ }^{(\gamma} \beta\right)^{\delta)}\right)_{\circ} .$$
The Chern-Moser tensor is a CR invariant. The vanishing of both $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ and $\underline{S}_{\alpha \bar{\gamma} \beta \bar{\delta}}$ is equivalent to the almost CR structure being locally $C R$ flat, i.e. $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ is locally diffeomorphic to the CR sphere.

We shall also need the Webster-Ricci tensor $\underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\gamma}{ }^{\delta}:=\underline{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{R}_{\alpha \bar{\beta} \gamma}{ }^{\delta}$, the Webster-Ricci scalar $\underline{\mathrm{Sc}}:=\underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\gamma}{ }^{\gamma}$, the Webster-Schouten tensor and the Webster-Schouten scalar
$$\underline{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}:=\frac{1}{m+2}\left(\underline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}-\frac{1}{2 m+2} \underline{\mathrm{Sc}} \underline{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}\right), \quad \underline{\mathrm{P}}:=\underline{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} \underline{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$$
respectively. Equation (4.3a) then allows us to decompose $\underline{R}_{\alpha \bar{\gamma} \beta}{ }^{\delta}$ as
$$\underline{R}_{\alpha}{ }^{\gamma}{ }_{\beta}{ }^{\delta}=\frac{1}{4} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}^{\gamma \delta \epsilon} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \epsilon}-\frac{1}{2} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\epsilon(\alpha \beta)} \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\gamma \delta \epsilon}-\frac{1}{2} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}^{\epsilon(\gamma \delta)} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \epsilon}+\underline{S}_{\alpha}{ }^{\gamma}{ }_{\beta}{ }^{\delta}+4 \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{(\alpha}{ }^{(\gamma} \delta_{\beta)}^{\delta)},$$
where
$$\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha}^{\gamma}:=\underline{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha}^{\gamma}+\frac{1}{m+2}\left(-\frac{1}{2} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\beta \delta \alpha} \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\beta \delta \gamma}+\frac{1}{4} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\alpha \beta \delta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\gamma \beta \delta}+\frac{1}{8(m+1)} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\epsilon \delta \beta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\epsilon \delta \beta} \delta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\right) .$$

Definition 4.1 Let $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ be a partially integrable contact almost CR manifold. We say that $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ is almost $C R$-Einstein if it admits a contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ such that its pseudo-Hermitian torsion tensor $\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}$, its Webster-Schouten tensor $\underline{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ and the Nijenhuis tensor $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ satisfy
$$\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}=0, \quad \quad \underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\gamma(\alpha \beta)}=0, \quad\left(\underline{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}-\frac{1}{m+2} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\alpha \delta \gamma} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\bar{\beta}}^{\delta \gamma}\right)_{\circ}=0 .$$
We refer to $\left(\underline{H}, \underline{J}, \underline{\theta}^{0}\right)$ as an almost CR-Einstein structure. When $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ is integrable, i.e. $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}=0$, we say that $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ is CR-Einstein.

We shall now give an equivalent formulation of almost CR-Einstein structures.
Proposition 4.1 A partially integrable contact almost CR manifold ( $\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J}$ ) is almost CR-Einstein if and only if it admits a contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ such that its pseudo-Hermitian torsion tensor $\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}$, its Webster-Ricci tensor $\underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}$ and the Nijenhuis tensor $\underline{N}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ satisfy
$$\begin{aligned}
\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta} & =0 \\
\underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{N}_{\gamma(\alpha \beta)} & =0 \\
\underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha}{ }^{\beta}-\underline{N}_{\alpha \delta \gamma} \underline{N}^{\beta \delta \gamma} & =\underline{\Lambda} \delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}, \quad \text { for some constant } \underline{\Lambda} .
\end{aligned}$$

Proof. That (4.6) implies (4.5) is clear. For the converse, let us assume (4.5). Then (4.6) holds except that we do not know whether $\underline{\Lambda}$ is constant. We proceed to demonstrate that this is the case. To this end, we take covariant derivatives of (4.6c) to get
$$\begin{aligned}
\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{\Lambda} \delta_{\alpha}^{\beta} & =\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha}^{\beta}-\underline{\nabla}_{0}\left(\underline{N}_{\alpha \delta \gamma} \underline{N}^{\beta \delta \gamma}\right) \\
\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\Lambda} & =\underline{\nabla}_{\beta} \underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha}^{\beta}-\underline{\nabla}_{\beta}\left(\underline{N}_{\alpha \delta \gamma} \underline{N}^{\beta \delta \gamma}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
Under our assumptions (4.5), the first Bianchi identities (4.3b), (4.3c) and (4.3d) reduce respectively to
$$\begin{aligned}
\underline{R}_{\beta 0 \alpha}{ }^{\gamma} & =0, \\
\underline{R}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}{ }^{\delta} & =\underline{\nabla}^{\delta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}, \\
\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{\mathrm{~N}}_{\beta \gamma \alpha} & =0,
\end{aligned}$$
and the second Bianchi identities (4.4a) and (4.4b) become
$$\begin{aligned}
& \left.2 \underline{\nabla}_{[\delta} \underline{R}_{\epsilon}\right] \bar{\gamma} \alpha{ }^{\beta}+\underline{\nabla}_{\bar{\gamma}} \underline{\nabla}^{\beta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\delta \epsilon \alpha}+\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\delta \epsilon}{ }^{\bar{\phi}} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\bar{\phi} \bar{\gamma}}{ }^{\beta}=0, \\
& \underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{R}_{\epsilon}{ }^{\gamma}{ }_{\alpha}{ }^{\beta}=0,
\end{aligned}$$
respectively. Combining (4.7) with (4.9) and (4.11) clearly yields $\underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{\Lambda}=0$.
Now, we trace (4.10) over $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and over $\bar{\gamma}$ and $\epsilon$ to find
$$\underline{\nabla}_{\beta} \underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha}{ }^{\beta}=\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \mathrm{Sc}+\underline{\nabla}^{\beta} \underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{N}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}-\underline{N}_{\alpha \beta \gamma} \underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{N}^{\beta \gamma \delta}+\underline{\nabla}_{\beta}\left(\underline{N}_{\gamma \delta \alpha} \underline{N}^{\gamma \delta \beta}\right)-\underline{\nabla}_{\beta}\left(\underline{N}_{\alpha \gamma \delta} \underline{N}^{\beta \gamma \delta}\right) .$$
After plugging this expression into (4.8) and a number of tensorial manipulations, using (4.6b) in particular, we arrive at
$$\begin{aligned}
& \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{1-m}\left(\underline{\nabla}^{\beta} \underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}+\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\beta \gamma \delta}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\beta \gamma \delta}+\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\beta \gamma \delta}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\beta \gamma \delta}\right. \\
&\left.+\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\beta \gamma \delta}+\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\beta \gamma \delta}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}-2\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\delta \beta \gamma}\right) .
\end{aligned}$$
To show that the RHS of this equality vanishes, we shall need the identity
$$\underline{\nabla}^{\beta} \underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{N}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}=-\underline{N}^{\beta \gamma \delta} \underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{N}_{\beta \gamma \alpha}-\underline{N}_{\beta \gamma \delta} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{N}^{\beta \gamma \delta}-\underline{N}_{\beta \gamma \alpha} \underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{N}^{\beta \gamma \delta},$$
which can be obtained by commuting the covariant derivatives and using our assumptions (4.5) again. This allows us to simplify our expression to
$$\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{1-m}\left(\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\beta \gamma \delta}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\beta \gamma \delta}-2\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\delta} \underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}\right) \underline{\mathrm{N}}^{\delta \beta \gamma}\right) .$$
Renaming the indices and using (4.3e) eventually leads to $\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\Lambda}=0$, as required. \(\square\)

Remark 4.2 We see at once from (4.6a) that an almost CR-Einstein manifold admits a transverse infinitesimal CR symmetry. If the almost CR structure is integrable, Definition 4.1 corresponds to the one given in [11] and [33]. In the latter reference, they are referred to as transversally symmetric pseudo-Einstein spaces. Condition (4.6c) alone defines the notion of pseudo-Einstein structures [32]. The geometric interpretation of (4.6b) will be given in Section 4.3.

Remark 4.3 In dimension three, one can still define a Webster-Tanaka connection. Since the Nijenhuis tensor $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ and Chern-Moser tensor $\underline{S}_{\alpha \bar{\gamma} \beta \bar{\delta}}$ do not exist here, CR flatness is equivalent to the vanishing of the fourth-order CR invariant
$$\underline{Q}_{\alpha \beta}:=\mathrm{i} \underline{\nabla}_{0} \underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}-2 \mathrm{i} \underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{T}_{\beta}+2 \underline{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha}{ }^{\gamma} \underline{A}_{\gamma \beta}, \quad \text { where } \quad \underline{T}_{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{3}\left(\underline{\nabla}_{\alpha} \underline{\mathrm{P}}-\mathrm{i} \underline{\nabla}^{\gamma} \underline{A}_{\gamma \alpha}\right) .$$
One can also use equations (4.5) to define the notion of a CR-Einstein manifold, but the second Bianchi identities no longer implies (4.6c) in the sense that $\underline{\Lambda}$ is not necessarily constant. One may then make the additional assumption that the Webster-Ricci tensor is proportional to the Levi form by a constant factor. Such a condition is however too strong since it is equivalent to $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ being locally flat as can be gleaned from equation (4.12).

\subsection*{4.3 Relation to almost Kähler geometry}

Recall (see e.g. [25]) that an almost Kähler manifold is an almost Hermitian manifold $(\mathcal{M}, \underset{\sim}{h}, \underset{\sim}{J})$, where $\underset{\sim}{h}$ is a Riemannian metric and $\underset{\sim}{J}$ an almost complex structure compatible with $\underset{\sim}{h}$, such that the Hermitian 2-form $\underset{\sim}{\omega}:=\underset{\sim}{h} \circ \underset{\sim}{J}$ is closed, i.e. $\mathrm{d} \underset{\sim}{\omega}=0$. When $\underset{\sim}{J}$ is integrable, $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underset{\sim}{h}, \underset{\sim}{J})$ is said to be Kähler. An almost Kähler manifold that is not Kähler will be referred to as strictly almost Kähler. We take the dimension of $\mathcal{M}$ to be $2 m$ with $m>1$, since clearly, for $m=1$, almost Kähler necessarily implies Kähler. In abstract index notation, we shall use minuscule Roman letters starting from the middle of the alphabet, i.e. $i, j, k, \ldots$, for sections of $T \mathcal{M}$, of its dual, and tensor products thereof. Indices will be lowered and raised by means of ${\underset{\sim}{h}}_{i j}$ and its inverse ${\underset{\sim}{h}}^{i j}$.

Denoting by $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ the Levi-Civita connection associated to $\underset{\sim}{h}$, the Hermitian 2-form satisfies [25]
$$\nabla_{i}{\underset{\sim}{\omega}}_{j k}=2{\underset{\sim}{J}}_{j}{ }^{\ell}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\ell k i},$$
where $\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{j k}{ }^{i}=\underline{\mathrm{N}}_{[j k]}{ }^{i}$ is the Nijenhuis tensor of $\underset{\sim}{J}$ which, for an almost Kähler manifold, satisfies ${\underset{\sim}{\mathrm{N}}}_{[j k i]}=0$ and $\underset{\sim}{J}\left({ }_{i}{ }^{\ell}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{j) \ell k}=0\right.$. This is the obstruction to the integrability of $\underset{\sim}{J}$. Thus, when $\underset{\sim}{J}$ is integrable, we have $\nabla_{i} \underline{\omega}_{j k}=0$.

The complexified tangent bundle $T \mathcal{M}$ splits as ${ }^{\mathrm{c}} T \mathcal{M}=T^{(1,0)} \mathcal{M} \oplus T^{(0,1)} \mathcal{M}$, and in line with our previous notation, we shall use minuscule Greek letters for sections of $T^{(1,0)} \mathcal{M}$, and their barred analogues for sections of $T^{(0,1)} \mathcal{M}$, e.g. ${\underset{\sim}{v}}^{\alpha} \in \Gamma\left(T^{(1,0)} \underline{\mathcal{M}}\right)$, and ${\underset{\sim}{w}}^{\bar{\alpha}} \in \Gamma\left(T^{(0,1)} \underline{\mathcal{M}}\right)$, and similarly for their duals. In this notation, the only non-vanishing complex components of ${\underset{\sim}{N}}_{i j k}$ are ${\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$ and $\mathbb{N}_{\bar{\alpha} \bar{\beta} \bar{\gamma}}$.

Lemma 4.1 An almost Kähler manifold $(\underset{\sim}{\mathcal{M}}, \underset{\sim}{h}, \underset{\sim}{J})$ admits a unique linear connection $\underset{\sim}{\circ}$ that preserves both $\underset{\sim}{h}$ and $\underset{\sim}{J}$ and has torsion given by
$$2 \stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}_{[i} \stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}_{j]} \underset{\sim}{f}={\underset{\sim}{N}}_{i j}{ }^{k} \stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}_{k} \underset{\sim}{f}, \quad \quad \text { for any smooth function } \underset{\sim}{f} \text { on } \mathcal{M} .$$
Its relation to the Levi-Civita connection $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ is given by
$$\stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}_{i}{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{j}={\underset{\sim}{\nabla}}_{i}{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{j}-{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}^{k} \mathcal{N}_{k i j}, \quad \text { for any } 1 \text {-form }{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{j} .$$
Proof. The required properties of $\stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}$ follow from the ansatz (4.13) and the fact that ${\underset{\sim}{N}}_{(i j) k}=0$ and $J_{(j}{ }^{\ell} \widetilde{\sim}_{k) \ell i}=0$. Uniqueness can easily be proved as in the case of the Levi-Civita connection. \(\square\)

The connections $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ and $\underset{\sim}{\stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}}$ clearly coincide if and only if $\underset{\sim}{J}$ is integrable. Their relation can be expressed by writing the structure equations explicitly in terms of a unitary frame $\left\{{\underset{\sim}{\theta}}^{\alpha}\right\}$ :
$$\mathrm{d}{\underset{\sim}{\theta}}^{\alpha}={\underset{\sim}{\theta}}^{\beta} \wedge{\underset{\sim}{\check{I}}}_{\beta}^{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2} \underset{\sim}{N} \bar{\beta} \stackrel{\alpha}{\gamma} \bar{\theta}^{\bar{\beta}} \wedge{\overline{\underset{\sim}{\theta}}}^{\bar{\gamma}}=-{\underset{\sim}{\Gamma}}^{\alpha}{ }_{\beta} \wedge{\underset{\sim}{\theta}}^{\beta}-{\underset{\sim}{\Gamma}}^{\alpha}{ }_{\bar{\beta}} \wedge{\underset{\sim}{\theta}}^{\bar{\beta}},$$
and similarly for its complex conjugate. Here, ${\underset{\sim}{\Gamma}}^{\alpha}{ }_{\beta}$ and $\stackrel{\circ}{\Gamma}_{\beta}{ }^{\alpha}$ are the respective connection 1-forms of $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ and $\stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}$ with respect to $\left\{{\underset{\sim}{\theta}}^{\alpha}\right\}$. A cursory comparison of (4.14) with (4.1b), with $\underline{A}_{\bar{\alpha} \bar{\beta}}=0$, reveals that the connection $\stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}$ is closely related to the Webster-Tanaka connection, and its properties, such as the Bianchi identities, mirror those of the Webster-Tanaka connection. This analogy justifies the discrepancy in the choice of staggering of indices between ${\underset{\sim}{\Gamma}}^{\alpha}{ }_{\beta}$ and $\stackrel{\circ}{\Gamma}_{\beta}{ }^{\alpha}$. For the same concern of convention, the respective curvature tensors of $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ and $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ will be defined by
$$2 \underset{\sim}{\nabla}\left[i \widetilde{\sim}_{j}\right]{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{k}=:{\underset{\sim}{R}}_{i j k}{ }^{\ell}{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{\ell}, \quad 2 \stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}_{[i} \stackrel{\circ}{\nabla}_{j]}{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{k}=:-\stackrel{\circ}{R}_{i j k}{ }^{\ell}{\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{\ell},$$
for any 1-form ${\underset{\sim}{\alpha}}_{i}$. The complex components of ${\underset{\sim}{R}}_{i j k \ell}$ can then be expressed as
$${\underset{\sim}{R}}_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta}=2 \underset{\sim}{\nabla}\left[\gamma \mid{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\alpha \beta \mid \delta]}, \quad{\underset{\sim}{R}}_{\gamma \delta}{ }^{\beta}{ }_{\alpha}={\underset{\sim}{D}}^{\beta}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\gamma \delta \alpha}, \quad{\underset{\sim}{R}}_{\gamma}{ }^{\delta \beta}{ }_{\alpha}={\underset{\sim}{R}}_{\gamma}{ }^{\delta}{ }_{\alpha}{ }^{\beta}-{\underset{\sim}{N}}^{\beta \epsilon \delta}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\epsilon \alpha \gamma},\right.$$
and from the last equation, we deduce ${\underset{\sim}{R}}^{\gamma \delta}{ }_{\alpha \beta}=-{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\alpha \beta \epsilon}{\underset{\sim}{N}}^{\gamma \delta \epsilon}$. The Ricci tensor of $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ is defined to be $\underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{i j}:={\underset{\sim}{R}}_{i k j}{ }^{k}$ as is conventional. The complex components of the Ricci tensor are given by
$$\underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha \beta}=2{\underset{\sim}{\nabla}}^{\gamma}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\gamma(\alpha \beta)}, \quad \underline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{\alpha}{ }^{\beta}={\underset{\operatorname{Ric}}{\circ}}_{\alpha}{ }^{\beta}-{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\alpha \delta \gamma}{\underset{\sim}{N}}^{\beta \delta \gamma},$$
where we have defined
$${\underset{\operatorname{Ric}}{\text { Ric }}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}:={\underset{\sim}{R}}_{\gamma}^{\gamma}{ }_{\alpha \bar{\beta}} .$$
This choice of definition is analogous to the definition of the Webster-Ricci tensor, and will prove judicious in the light of Corollary 4.1 below. The next result follows directly from identities (4.15).

Proposition 4.2 Let $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underset{\sim}{h}, \underset{\sim}{J})$ be an almost Kähler manifold. Denote by $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ the Levi-Civita connection of $\underset{\sim}{h}$ and by $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ the compatible linear connection of Lemma 4.1.
\begin{itemize}
\item[1.] The Ricci tensor of $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$ commutes with $\underset{\sim}{J}$, i.e. ${\underset{\sim}{J}}_{(i}{ }^{k} \operatorname{Ric}_{j) k}=0$, if and only if ${\underset{\sim}{\nabla}}^{\gamma}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\gamma(\alpha \beta)}=0$.
\item[2.] The metric $\underset{\sim}{h}$ is Einstein, i.e.
$$\operatorname{Ric}_{i j}=\underset{\sim}{\Lambda}{\underset{\sim}{\lambda}}_{i j}, \quad \text { with constant } \underset{\sim}{\Lambda},$$
if and only if
$${\underset{\sim}{\nabla}}^{\gamma}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\gamma(\alpha \beta)}=0, \quad{\underset{\operatorname{Ric}}{\circ}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}-{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\alpha \delta \gamma}{\underset{\sim}{N}}_{\bar{\beta}}^{\delta \gamma}=\underset{\sim}{\Lambda}{\underset{\sim}{\lambda}}_{\alpha \bar{\beta}}, \quad \text { with constant } \underset{\sim}{\Lambda} .$$
\end{itemize}

Let us return to a partially integrable contact almost CR manifold $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$. We recall here that we assume that the Levi form has positive definite signature. Suppose that $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ admits an infinitesimal transverse symmetry $\underline{e}_{0}$, i.e. $\underline{e}_{0}$ is the Reeb vector field of some contact form $\underline{\theta}^{0}$ for which the pseudo-Hermitian torsion tensor vanishes, i.e. $\underline{A}_{\alpha \beta}=0$. Let us denote by $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ the (local) leaf space of the corresponding foliation. Then, since $\underline{J}$ and $\mathrm{d} \underline{\theta}^{0}$ are preserved along the flow of $\underline{e}_{0}$, they descend to $\mathcal{M}$ endowing it with an almost Kähler structure $(\underset{\sim}{h}, \underset{\sim}{J})$ - under the assumption of integrability, this is already proved in [33]. In a nutshell:

Proposition 4.3 Let $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underline{H}, \underline{J})$ be a partially integrable contact almost CR manifold that admits an infinitesimal transverse symmetry $\underline{e}_{0}$. Then $(\underline{H}, \underline{J})$ induces an almost Kähler structure $(\underset{\sim}{h}, \underset{\sim}{J})$ on the (local) leaf space $\underline{\mathcal{M}}$ of the foliation defined by $\underline{e}_{0}$. Further $\underline{J}$ is integrable if and only if $\underset{\sim}{J}$ is.

In fact, the Webster-Tanaka connection $\underline{\nabla}$ descends to the compatible connection $\underset{\sim}{\dot{\nabla}}$ on $(\underline{\mathcal{M}}, \underset{\sim}{\mathcal{h}}, \underset{\sim}{J})$ defined in Lemma 4.1. In particular, the restriction of the curvature of $\underline{\nabla}$ to projectable vector fields can be identified with the curvature of $\underset{\sim}{\nabla}$.
